## ABSTRACT 

Tokenizer-free language models remove the inductive bias of fixed tokenizers by modeling text directly as bytes, but the resulting longer sequences substantially increase computation and eliminate explicit text abstractions. We ask whether this additional computation can be useful, and whether standard Transformers can learn the abstractions that tokenization provides. We study these questions on Transformers without specialized tokenization-related architectures. With token-superposition training and hash embeddings, byte Transformers consis-tently outperform subword Transformers as model size scales. We further find that byte Transformers build local text abstractions as external tokenizers: a set of segmentation-like positions are used to collect local context representations, and restricting up to 25% of intermediate layers to these local representations pre-serves downstream performance. Finally, these learned structures induce highly non-uniform generation difficulty, with uncertainty concentrated near local struc-ture boundaries; exploiting them for speculative decoding yields 3.4× more ac-cepted tokens than in subword Transformers. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Tokenizer-free language models, or byte language models, aim to remove externally imposed tok-enizers by modeling text directly as byte sequences (Xue et al., 2022; YU et al., 2023; Slagle, 2024; Tsiamas et al., 2025; Shah et al., 2026). This formulation provides a universal input space and a new joint learning setting. However, it also introduces two main challenges. First, byte sequences are longer than subword sequences. If a typical subword contains K (usually around 4) bytes, a byte model must process K times more sequence positions. Second, prior studies suggest that effec-tive language modeling benefits from prior abstractions over raw text. Directly modeling bytes can therefore underperform tokenized models, despite having a more general input representation. Recent works (BLT Pagnoni et al., 2025, H-Net Hwang et al., 2026, ByteFlow Deng et al., 2026) develop new network architectures with built-in tokenization mechanisms. For example, in BLT, local encoder layers first encode byte streams into variable-sized chunks, then global layers operate on the chunks, and local decoder layers decode chunks back to bytes. While these architectures demonstrate the promise of joint tokenization, they often need specialized training and optimization strategies, making it less clear how much of the observed gains are from the joint formulation. In this work, we retain the flat Transformer architecture (without the explicit local-global-local hierarchy) and investigate the two challenges underlying byte-level language modeling: Can the additional computation caused by longer sequences be beneficial, and how does a Transformer recover useful text abstractions directly from bytes? 

We study the scaling behavior of byte Transformers, and the main findings are: 1) with two modeling ingredients (token-superposition training (Peng et al., 2026) and hash embeddings), byte Transform-ers consistently outperform subword Transformers when scaling the number of parameters (Figure 1a). 2) Byte models remain effective as training data continues to scale. These results motivate a different perspective on sequence length: rather than treating longer sequences as a defect, they can provide additional computation under the same parameter budget, which is a useful scaling axis for language models. Further evaluations on downstream text-only and vision–language tasks show that byte Transformers significantly improve performance on tasks that require fine-grained perception (around 40% relative improvements over subword Trans-formers on CUTE scores, 20% on OCRBench (Liu et al., 2024)). We next investigate how a byte Transformer develops the abstractions that tokenization provides explicitly. By studying an input-copying task on byte models, we find that copying actions need to attend only to certain segmentation positions (not necessarily whitespace), where information from local text pieces can be read out. Based on this finding, we further constrain a range of intermediate layers (up to 25% of all layers) to access only these locally aggregated representations, resembling a global modeling stage over learned local structures. The resulting model retains downstream per-formance without additional training. This suggests that the apparent hierarchy of local abstraction and global reasoning is not merely imposed by tokenizer-free architectures such as BLT and H-Net, but can also emerge implicitly in the byte Transformer. Finally, we study whether these learned local structures can be exploited during inference. By plot-ting the per-byte loss distribution, we find that byte Transformers have much higher variance than subword models: many byte positions have near-zero loss (in bits per byte), while substantially larger losses concentrate near the boundaries of the learned local structures. This observation sug-gests that the extra generation steps are not uniformly expensive in terms of model uncertainty. We therefore apply speculative decoding to construct local structures during inference (Wang et al., 2024; Kallini et al., 2026), and show that a drafting model on byte Transformers can produce 3.4×

more accepted tokens than on subword Transformers. To summarize, our results support a simple view of tokenizer-free language modeling: longer byte sequences provide useful additional computation. At the same time, the model can learn useful local abstractions internally rather than relying on a fixed tokenizer. This perspective suggests sequence length, learned abstraction, and computation allocation as closely connected dimensions for future language-model design. 

## 2 SCALING BYTE TRANSFORMER LANGUAGE MODELS 

We first ask how the additional tokens in byte sequences affect model quality under parameter and compute constraints. We then examine which task capabilities benefit from byte resolution and how shared depth and sparse capacity change these trade-offs. 2.1 BUILDING BYTE MODELS 

We first establish a controlled comparison between byte- and subword-level Transformers. Both models are trained from scratch with a mixed attention model. 1 The byte model uses a vocabulary of size 256 , while the subword model uses a 49 , 152 token vocabulary from the cosmo2-tokenizer (Allal et al., 2025). We use “token” to refer to both byte and subword, depending on the model in context. Directly applying the standard training recipe to bytes is suboptimal. Byte sequences contain sub-stantially more tokens, while each byte token carries less local semantic context than a subword token. We find the following two ingredients are effective for training byte models. First, we use token-superposition training (TST; Peng et al., 2026) to improve learning efficiency from the longer byte sequences. TST averages four consecutive token embeddings and predicts the corresponding next four-token bag. Applying TST during the first 30% of training provides 4×

token exposure in this stage, while reverting to standard next-token prediction for the remaining 70% results in 1.9× total text exposure under the same number of training tokens (see Appendix A.2 for details).

## 3 EMERGENT LOCAL ABSTRACTIONS IN BYTE TRANSFORMERS 

Tokenization provides explicit local abstractions that can organize text into useful computational units. In this section, we investigate whether similar structures can emerge implicitly in byte Trans-formers without an explicit tokenizer or hierarchical architecture. We study this question through two complementary experiments. First, we examine how byte models organize information during prediction using an input-copying task. Second, we evaluate whether the representations revealed by this analysis can support alternative computation patterns through a frozen layer intervention. 3.1 COPYING AS A PROBE OF LOCAL INFORMATION STRUCTURE 

To probe how text semantics are abstracted and accessed in models, we study the copying task by prompting the model with “Your task is to copy the string: X. Now copy it: X”, where X is a short string composed of multiple meaningful English words. We evaluate B YTE -1B by analyzing attention patterns during the copying process, focusing on how different layers access the source text when generating the copied sequence (see Appendix E.1 for details). As shown in Figure 3, B YTE -1B exhibits a clear attention aggregation pattern during copying. In particular, attention in intermediate layers becomes concentrated on a small set of source positions that are repeatedly accessed by multiple consecutive target bytes. This aggregation occurs even without any whitespace in inputs, indicating that it does not simply arise from explicit separators. 5

The repeated reuse of these positions suggests that the byte model internally develops a mechanism for consolidating local information into representative states, providing an implicit structure over the byte sequence. 3.2 TESTING LOCAL ABSTRACTIONS WITH A FROZEN LAYER INTERVENTION 

The copying task reveals that multiple byte predictions can repeatedly access a small set of locally aggregated source representations. However, attention patterns alone do not establish whether these representations are sufficient for subsequent computation. These additional modeling steps raise the question of where the extra computation (Section 2.2) is useful and where it could be re-duced. ## 5 RELATED WORK 

Tokenizer-free language models. Tokenizer-free language models operate directly on fine-grained inputs such as bytes or characters, avoiding a separately constructed subword vocabulary. ByT5 (Xue et al., 2022) studies byte-to-byte pretraining, while MambaByte (Wang et al., 2024) models byte sequences with selective state-space models. Other works (Clark et al., 2022; YU et al., 2023; Pagnoni et al., 2025; Hwang et al., 2026), introduce hierarchical mechanisms that compress or aggregate fine-grained inputs for coarser-grained computation. In contrast, we study byte lan-guage modeling with Transformers with hybrid attention that preserves byte resolution throughout the network. 

Compute scaling and allocation. Model scale and computation can be varied independently through tokenization and architectural design. Compute Optimal Tokenization (Limisiewicz et al., 2026) studies how token granularity changes the relationship between raw data, sequence length, and compute. Repeated-depth models such as LoopFormer (Jeddi et al., 2026) increase computation while sharing parameters, whereas sparse MoE models such as DeepSeek-V3 (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025) increase parameter capacity without proportionally increasing activated computation. Build-ing on these directions, we use shared depth to increase S UBWORD computation and sparse experts to reduce B YTE activated computation at a given parameter capacity, examining how computation shapes the byte–subword modeling trade-off. 

## 6 DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION 

Our results provide a complementary perspective to recent byte-level approaches, which introduce explicit hierarchical or learned tokenization mechanisms to control the cost of long byte sequences. Using a flat Transformer, we show that this sequence-length overhead can instead provide use-ful computation: byte models benefit from scaling and favor substantially more training data per parameter under a fixed FLOP budget. Moreover, although we do not impose a hierarchy, byte Transformers develop segmentation-like positions that aggregate local text information, and inter-mediate layers can exploit these representations without loss in downstream performance. This connects flat byte Transformers to hierarchical architectures and suggests that local abstraction and global modeling can emerge implicitly from byte-level training. These learned structures also have inference-time consequences, as prediction difficulty concentrates near their boundaries and can be exploited by speculative decoding. Together, our results suggest that sequence length, learned ab-straction, and computation should be viewed as coupled design dimensions, motivating future work on adaptive computation and learned computational granularity for byte language models. 9Preprint 

Limitations Our study is limited to vanilla Transformer backbones and the evaluated training and task scales; whether the observed scaling behavior and emergent local structures generalize to other architectures, larger models, and broader modalities remains open. 10 Preprint

## A ARCHITECTURE AND OBJECTIVES 

A.1 TOKENIZERS 

Our byte tokenizer encodes text directly as UTF-8 without learned merges or Unicode normalization. To support document/chat control and padding within a 256-entry vocabulary, we use 0xC0 , 0xC1 ,and 0xF5 –0xFF as special-token IDs. These 13 bytes values are forbidden in UTF-8 and never occur in valid UTF-8 text (Yergeau, 2003). For subword models, we use HuggingFaceTB/cosmo2-tokenizer (Allal et al., 2025) 8, a pretrained byte-level BPE tokenizer with 49,152 vocabulary entries. On the pretraining dataset, it averages approximately 4.56 UTF-8 bytes per token. We use this ratio in the compute and data exposure calculations in Section 2.2 and Appendices C. A.2 TOKEN -S UPERPOSITION TRAINING (TST) TST (Peng et al., 2026) lets each model input represent several tokens. The causal model and its single output head, with parameters θ, use the preceding bags B<i to produce one shared token distribution pθ (· | B<i ) for all tokens in the next bag Bi. Architecture. We adapt B YTE and S UBWORD models into vision–language models (VLMs) us-ing an identical vision–language architecture. A frozen SigLIP2 SO400M vision encoder connects to the pretrained language model through a two-layer GELU MLP projector, which maps visual representations into the language embedding space. The projected visual tokens enter the language sequence through a unified image-token interface. 

Training. We use a two-stage training pipeline following the standard LLaVA recipe. Stage 1 trains only the projector on LLaVA-Pretrain, keeping the vision encoder and language model frozen, with learning rate 10 −3 and effective batch size 256. Stage 2 jointly fine-tunes the projector and language model on LLaVA-NeXT-SFT, keeping the vision encoder frozen, with learning rate 2 ×

10 −5 and effective batch size 128. Both stages use AdamW, bfloat16 mixed precision, 3% warmup followed by cosine learning-rate decay, and one training epoch.

E.2 FROZEN LAYER INTERVENTION 

Local span construction. The frozen layer intervention introduces a compressed global stage over local representations while preserving the original byte-resolution computation before and after the selected layers. Inspired by the local–global–local organization of BLT and H-Net (Pagnoni et al., 2025; Hwang et al., 2026), we identify local spans in the prompts of the 28-layer B YTE -1B and SUBWORD -1B models using a matched 200M model from the corresponding family. For a prompt token Ti, the smaller model with parameters θ computes the token loss 

LCE (Ti | T<i ; θ) = − ln pθ (Ti | T<i ). (19) Tokens with high loss mark patch boundaries, grouping predictable continuations into local spans. For text-only tasks with T prompt tokens, we target ⌈T / 4⌉ patches, corresponding to approximately 

4× compression of the text sequence. The first token always starts a patch, and we select the highest loss threshold that yields the target number of patch starts, retaining all ties. For vision–language tasks with T non-image prompt tokens and V image tokens, we apply the same 4× target ratio only to the text portion, giving a target of V + ⌈T / 4⌉ patches. Each image token is kept as a singleton patch, while text patch boundaries are selected using the same loss threshold procedure. G.2 ORACLE SIMULATION AND ACCEPTANCE METRICS 

To isolate draft–target agreement from the strategy used to construct draft candidates, we use an oracle simulation on the given evaluation text. It directly measures consecutive agreements, setting aside choices such as draft-tree expansion, pruning, and stopping heuristics. Both models condition on the preceding tokens of this text at each scored token (teacher forcing). A draft token is counted as accepted when the two models select the same token, including when it differs from the reference token. The acceptance rate is the fraction of scored tokens accepted. The following step always uses the reference continuation as context. Each verification step ends at the first rejection or document boundary and counts as one simulated target forward pass, with no proposal-length cap. The mean accepted draft length is the number of accepted draft tokens per simulated target forward pass. To compare text coverage across granularities, we also report accepted bytes per forward, summing the decoded byte lengths of the accepted draft tokens. Both averages include verification steps with no accepted tokens. Special tokens contribute no bytes; correction and bonus outputs are excluded. Related work uses similar diagnostics of draft–target agreement. Mamou et al. (2024) let the draft continue until the first rejection, identifying the maximum accepted draft length at each iteration without choosing a fixed lookahead or a stopping heuristic. Their oracle follows generated histories, whereas ours measures agreement on the given evaluation text. EvoESAP (Liu et al., 2026) likewise evaluates acceptance on fixed reference prefixes using teacher forcing. It averages the expected single-token acceptance computed from the overlap between draft and target next-token distributions. Our diagnostic measures consecutive agreements and their byte coverage. Figure 15 shows high acceptance rates in the lowest-BPB intervals for both families. A much larger share of B YTE tokens lies in these intervals, and most of them are accepted. Together with the longer byte acceptance lengths in Table 3, this supports using small drafts to supply many low-BPB byte continuations for joint target verification. 

Implications for speculative decoding. The drafting experiment provides a proof of concept for computation allocation at inference. Longer byte agreement runs indicate that small drafts can match 33 Preprint their targets across many consecutive byte tokens in reference text. These runs provide a teacher-forced diagnostic of drafting opportunities. Generated histories may differ from the reference text, so these lengths do not provide a general upper bound on accepted draft length during generation. In speculative decoding (Leviathan et al., 2023), accepted tokens become part of the subsequent con-text, and each round includes correction or bonus outputs. Establishing practical speedups therefore requires decoding along generated histories with finite proposals and measuring the costs of drafting and target verification. 34
