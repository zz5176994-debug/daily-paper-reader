Abstract 

Training-free model merging aims to combine multiple fine-tuned models into a single model without further optimization on labeled data. Yet, in transformers, independently merging individual layers can affect a shared attention computation because the query–key and value–output operators depend on composed matrices, overlooking the functional structure. Moreover, when merging earlier components, downstream components receive different activations than they do in the original model, thus, the merged and original execution paths no longer match. In this paper, we introduce Sequential Local Operator Alignment , a training-free method that merges transformers along the execution path of the partially merged model. Our method uses calibration data to estimate the local behavior of each functional component, aligns operators sequentially under the intermediate activation of the partially merged model, and subsequently factorizes the merged operators back into valid transformer parameters. We empirically show that this sequential step reduces error accumulation across layers. Furthermore, the proposed operator factorization step enables rank expansion, providing a principled mechanism for increasing multi-task capacity. We demonstrate that our approach generalizes across modalities, model scales, and varying numbers of tasks, from CLIP and RoBERTa to billion-parameter LLMs, and further extends naturally to the merging of LoRA-fine-tuned models. The results indicate improvements over strong merging baselines without requiring rank expansion, while optional expansion provides a further accuracy–inference-cost trade-off. ## 1 Introduction 

Modern neural networks are often adapted into many task-specific variants from the same pretrained initialization [Bommasani et al., 2021, Devlin et al., 2019, Raffel et al., 2020, Radford et al., 2021a, Ding et al., 2023]. However, maintaining a separate model for every task is expensive [Guo et al., 2021, Wortsman et al., 2022, Yadav et al., 2025], while retraining a single multi-task model can require substantial data, compute, and engineering effort [Ruder, 2017, Crawshaw, 2020, Yang et al., 2024b]. Training-free model merging [Xu et al., 2024, Fu et al., 2025, Matena and Raffel, 2022, Ilharco et al., 2023, Ortiz-Jimenez et al., 2023, Jin et al., 2023] provides a practical alternative: given several independently fine-tuned models, the goal is to combine them into one model without further training. An effective merged model should preserve the task-specific capabilities of the original models while offering the deployment efficiency of a single network [Yang et al., 2026]. While simple and often effective, this layer-wise perspective is parameterization-dependent: a model’s function is not uniquely tied to the particular tensors used to represent it [Zhao et al., 2025, B¨ urgin et al., 2026, Bona-Pellissier et al., 2023, Elhage et al., 2021, Dar et al., 2023]. A natural way is to merge objects at the level at which they enter the model’s computation, rather than at the level of an arbitrary parameter decomposition. To this end, we introduce Sequential Local Operator Alignment (SLOA), a training-free framework for merging transformer models by aligning local functional operators (see Fig. 1 for the illustration). SLOA associates each model component with the local operator. For each component, SLOA constructs a merged operator from the task models. SLOA considers two alignment criteria: (i) behavior matching , on calibration inputs to the component, the merged operator should produce outputs close to those of the task-specific operators, and (ii) data-aware operator regularization , the merged operator is kept close to the task-specific operators under a metric induced by the calibration activations. This metric emphasizes directions that are active on the data. Importantly, our contribution is to instantiate them as a unified local-operator alignment framework for training-free transformer merging. The same objective applies across LayerNorm modules, linear maps, QK attention operators, and VO attention operators by changing only the behavior map and the induced metric. A key feature of our framework is that alignment is performed sequentially . When earlier operators have already been merged, the hidden activations entering later components are no longer identical to the activations produced by any individual task model. If later components are merged only under the original task-model activations, the local objective assumes that all previous merges were perfect. Our approach instead evaluates the activation-matching term on the activations produced by the partially merged model. This makes each local merge corrective, aligning downstream operators along the actual execution path of the merged network 2and reducing error accumulation across layers. Our operator view separates the merged operator from its eventual factorization. For QK and VO components, each individual task head has rank at most the head dimension, but the merged multi-task operator may require a higher-rank representation, as different tasks can use different subspaces. Therefore, we treat rank as a capacity-control mechanism . After merging an operator, we can factorize it at the original rank to preserve the architecture, or expand the rank to retain additional singular directions that capture task-specific behavior. Empirically, our method achieves state-of-the-art training-free merging performance across vision benchmarks, RoBERTa models, and billion-parameter LLMs. Moreover, SLOA naturally extends to models fine-tuned with LoRA [Hu et al., 2022], further broadening its applicability to parameter-efficient adaptation settings. It consistently outperforms baselines that merge individual weights or rely on isolated activation alignment, supporting the central claim that model merging should be performed at local functional operators.

## 3 Methodology: Sequential local operator alignment (SLOA) 

The above example shows that independent merging can produce arbitrary functional errors when computation is performed inside a transformer. To address this, we introduce Sequential Local Operator Alignment , a training-free method that merges models by aligning the local functional operators. 

3.1 Local functional operators 

We define local functional operators as the parameterized object that determines a component’s local computation once its input activation and any non-learned part are fixed. For example, the local operator of a linear layer is its weight matrix, while the local operator of an attention head is more naturally expressed through the composed QK and VO operators (see Fig. 1). We write 𝜽 for a generic local operator and A ( 𝒁 ; 𝜽 ) for its behavior map, where 𝒁 denotes the local input needed to evaluate the operator. For most operators, 𝒁 is the activation 𝑿 , and for VO, it includes both the attention matrix and the residual state. Additional remarks. We conclude this section with two remarks: (i) RegMean [Jin et al., 2023] regularizes each linear-layer weight through Gram-matrix shrinkage. However, in attention, similar projection matrices do not necessarily imply similar computations. For example, changes to the query and key matrices can cancel each other and leave the QK operator unchanged, even though a penalty on the two matrices would still be nonzero. We build 𝚲 𝑡 from calibration states of the partially merged model. The resulting metric, 𝚲 𝑡 ⊗ 𝚲 𝑡 ,weights differences in the QK operator along both inputs to the attention-score map. Unlike separate penalties on 𝑾 𝑄 and 𝑾 𝐾 , it constrains the computation they define together while keeping the objective quadratic in the operator variable. Thus, SLOA keeps the local operators close to their task-specific counterparts without forcing their internal attention factors to use the same coordinates. (ii) ProDistill [Xu et al., 2025] progressively matches block outputs on paired partially merged and task-model activations by optimizing element-wise task-vector coefficients. SLOA shares this progressive matching principle, but changes both the merging variables and the level at which behavior is matched. Specifically, it aligns composed QK and VO operators through pre-softmax score matching and attention-weighted output matching, respectively. For fixed local inputs, these behavior maps are linear, yielding quadratic alignment objectives that can be solved with structured linear algebra. ## 4 Experiments 

In this section, we demonstrate SLOA for model merging across vision, natural language understanding, and large language model settings, using both fully fine-tuned and LoRA-adapted experts. In §4.1, we evaluate Vision Transformers [Dosovitskiy et al., 2020] on image classification benchmarks comprising 8, 14, and 20 tasks, focusing on the ViT-B/32 architecture. In §4.2, we consider merging RoBERTa models [Liu et al., 2019] for natural language understanding (NLU). Finally, in §4.3, we evaluate SLOA at a larger model scale by merging LoRA-adapted LLaMA-2-7B experts [Touvron et al., 2023] trained for math, code, and instruction-following tasks. Fig. 3 previews the per-task gains of SLOA over all baselines. Unless otherwise stated, all training-free methods that require calibration, including SLOA, use 𝑁 =256 samples per task drawn exclusively from the corresponding training split; no test data is used for merging. All results are averaged over three random seeds. Additional experiments in §F, and full experimental details are provided in §E. 

4.1 Merging Vision Transformers (ViT-B) 

We first evaluate SLOA on multi-task merging of ViT-B/32 experts using the task-specific checkpoints provided by Tang et al. [2024]. All experts are fine-tuned from a shared CLIP initialization [Radford et al., 2021b], each on a distinct image classification task. We consider three increasingly challenging settings involving 8, 14, and 20 experts. Our data-free baselines are weight averaging [Wortsman et al., 2022], Task Arithmetic [Ilharco et al., 2023], TIES-Merging [Yadav et al., 2023], TSV-M [Gargiulo et al., 2025], and Iso-C and Iso-CTS [Marczak et al., 2025]. We also compare against training-free methods that use calibration data: Fisher Merging [Matena and Raffel, 2022], RegMean [Jin et al., 2023], and RegMean++ [Nguyen et al., 2026]. Table 2 summarizes the results. At the original rank, SLOA outperforms all baselines across the three settings. Its advantage over the strongest baseline, RegMean++, increases with the number of merged tasks, from 0 .67percentage points for 8 tasks to 1 .9 for 14 tasks and 5 .5 for 20 tasks. These results demonstrate that SLOA handles an increasing number of tasks more effectively than the competing methods on these benchmarks.

4.3 Merging LLaMA-2-7B LoRA experts 

SLOA applies directly to LoRA-adapted experts without modifying its local objective. For each task 𝑡 , we first form the effective fine-tuned weight 𝑾 𝑡 = 𝑾 0 + 𝑩 𝑡 𝑨 𝑡 and then construct the same functional operators used for fully fine-tuned experts. This approach also avoids the factorization ambiguity of LoRA: since 

𝑩 𝑡 𝑨 𝑡 = (𝑩 𝑡 𝑹 )( 𝑹 −1 𝑨 𝑡 ) for any invertible 𝑹 , merging 𝑨 𝑡 and 𝑩 𝑡 separately can make the result depend on an arbitrary choice of low-rank coordinates. By operating on the effective weights and their composed operators, SLOA is invariant to this ambiguity. We evaluate this setting by merging three LoRA-adapted LLaMA-2-7B experts specialized in math, code, and instruction-following (details in §E.2.3). LLaMA-2 is a decoder-only architecture with rotary positional embeddings, whose position-dependent rotations complicate the representation of the QK computation as a single composed operator. We therefore compose only the VO circuit (SLOA-VO), while merging 𝑾 𝑄 and 

𝑾 𝐾 individually as linear operators. We evaluate both the original VO rank, 𝑟 𝑜𝑣 =128, and expanded ranks, 

𝑟 𝑜𝑣 ∈ { 256 , 384 }. Our comparisons include the baselines from §4.1 and the LoRA-specific baseline KnOTS-TIES [Stoica et al., 2025, Yadav et al., 2023]. We evaluate math, code, and instruction-following performance on GSM8K [Cobbe et al., 2021], HumanEval [Chen et al., 2021], and IFEval [Zhou et al., 2023], respectively. At the architecture-preserving rank 𝑟 𝑜𝑣 =128, SLOA-VO achieves the highest average score among all merging methods, exceeding RegMean++ by 1 .7 points. Increasing 𝑟 𝑜𝑣 to 384 further improves GSM8K and IFEval, with a small reduction in HumanEval performance (27 .44 versus 28 .05 at 𝑟 𝑜𝑣 =128), indicating that the benefits of additional VO capacity vary across tasks. These results extend sequential operator alignment to LoRA experts on a 7B-parameter decoder-only model. Complementary experiments on LoRA-adapted ViT-B/32 experts, using both composed QK and VO operators, are presented in §F.2. 95 Related work 

Data-free parameter-space merging. A broad class of data-free model merging methods considers task-specific models directly in parameter space. Uniform model soups average weights across models with the same architecture [Wortsman et al., 2022], while Task Arithmetic merges task vectors, defined as differences between fine-tuned and pretrained weights [Ilharco et al., 2023]. Later methods reduce task interference: TIES-Merging removes small-magnitude updates and resolves sign conflicts [Yadav et al., 2023], DARE randomly drops and rescales task-vector entries [Yu et al., 2024], and spectral methods such as TSV-M and Iso-C/Iso-CTS manipulate the singular structure of task vectors to denoise or rescale merged updates [Gargiulo et al., 2025, Marczak et al., 2025]. While attractive since they require no data or optimization, these methods merge parameter tensors instead of the computation units they implement. This is limiting in transformers, where attention depends on composed QK and OV operators and not isolated query, key, value, and output layers. SLOA instead merges these functional operators, removing dependence on arbitrary parameterizations. 

Calibration-based training-free merging. Another line of work uses a small calibration set while still avoiding gradient-based training. Fisher Merging [Matena and Raffel, 2022] weights parameters according to their Fisher information, preserving parameters estimated to be important for the task losses. RegMean [Jin et al., 2023] solves a closed-form regression problem that aligns hidden representations of the merged model with those of the task-specific models. RegMean++ [Nguyen et al., 2026] improves this idea by computing statistics across layers, reducing the covariate shift caused by earlier merging errors. SLOA belongs to this training-free regime, but differs in the object being aligned. Prior methods mainly treat layers as independent units. In contrast, SLOA defines a local operator for each functional component: LayerNorm affine maps, linear maps, QK operators, and OV operators. This allows the same closed-form alignment principle to apply to both standard linear layers and coupled attention computations. Moreover, SLOA performs alignment 

sequentially under the activations produced by the partially merged model, so later operators are merged on the activations they will actually receive at inference time. 

Test-time adaptation and training for merging. A separate class of methods improves merging by introducing learned coefficients, routers, task-specific components, or distillation objectives. AdaMerging [Yang et al., 2024b] learns layer-wise merging coefficients using unlabeled test samples. Twin-Merging [Lu et al., 2024] decomposes task vectors into shared and private components and uses a router to select fusion weights. EMR-Merging [Huang et al., 2024] maintains both shared and task-specific parameters, requiring task identity at inference time. Representation Surgery [Yang et al., 2024a] trains an auxiliary module to align representations, while ProDistill [Xu et al., 2025] formulates merging as a distillation problem with learned merging coefficients. These methods can achieve strong performance, but they require additional training, test-time data, task identity, routing, or teacher-student optimization. SLOA avoids these requirements: after collecting calibration activations, each local merge is solved in closed form, produces a single deployed model, and requires neither task identity nor test-time adaptation at inference time. 

## 6 Conclusion 

We presented Sequential Local Operator Alignment (SLOA), a training-free framework for merging trans-formers by aligning local functional operators . By treating the QK and VO products as the effective operators, matching each operator’s behavior under the partially merged model’s activations with a data-aware operator geometry, and merging sequentially in topological order, SLOA preserves task-specific behavior while reducing the growth of activation error across depth. The operator-level view also decouples the merged attention operator from its final factorization, making head rank an explicit capacity-control parameter. We evaluate SLOA on ViT-B/32 and ViT-B/16 with up to 20 tasks, RoBERTa on GLUE, LoRA-adapted ViT 10 experts, and a VO-only variant for LoRA-adapted LLaMA-2-7B. Across these settings, SLOA outperforms strong merging baselines at the original head rank, showing that its gains do not require widening the deployed model. Its relative advantage becomes more pronounced as the number of merged tasks grows. Increasing the head rank can provide further gains beyond those obtained at the original rank. One limitation of SLOA is that, like other calibration-based training-free methods, it requires a small unlabeled calibration set that represents each task’s input distribution. When such data are unavailable, data-free methods may be more suitable. In addition, SLOA follows a greedy sequential procedure. At each step, it optimizes a well-defined local objective, but it does not optimize a single end-to-end objective. Finally, SLOA requires more computation during merging than data-free methods and simpler calibration-based approaches because it propagates activations, solves local regression problems, and factorizes the merged operators. Increasing the rank beyond 𝑑 𝑘 also widens the attention heads, which increases inference time and memory use. Promising directions include reducing reliance on in-distribution calibration data through synthetic, generated, or proxy inputs, and combining SLOA with data free merging when calibration data provide insufficient coverage of some tasks. Extending the composed QK formulation to attention with relative or rotary position encodings would also broaden its architectural scope. Another direction is automatic per-head rank selection, allowing the merged model’s capacity to be allocated according to deployment constraints. More broadly, the proposed method supports a functional view of transformer merging: align local computations along the merged execution path, and choose the attention factorization to control how much of the aligned operators the deployed model retains for multi-task merging. In light of this view, the unit of merging does not need to be the unit of parameterization.

## B Extended related work 

B.1 Alignment under model symmetries and activation matching 

Neural networks contain many parameter symmetries: different parameterizations can represent the same function [Dinh et al., 2017, Neyshabur et al., 2016, Zhao et al., 2025, Bona-Pellissier et al., 2023]. Prior work on model alignment and mode connectivity studies how to account for such symmetries before merging, for example by matching neurons, features, or permutations across independently trained networks [Ainsworth et al., 2023, Singh and Jaggi, 2020, Xu et al., 2024, Zhang et al., 2025]. These methods highlight an important principle: successful merging should compare models in a functionally meaningful coordinate system, rather than relying only on raw parameter coordinates. Our work is motivated by the same non-identifiability issue, but addresses a different symmetry that appears inside transformer attention. For a single attention head, the transformation (𝑾 𝑄 , 𝑾 𝐾 ) ↦ → ( 𝑾 𝑄 𝑹 , 𝑾 𝐾 𝑹 −⊤ )

leaves the QK operator 𝑾 𝑄 𝑾 ⊤

unchanged, and analogous changes of the value-head coordinates preserve the VO operator. Thus, aligning or averaging 𝑾 𝑄 , 𝑾 𝐾 , 𝑾 𝑉 , and 𝑾 𝑂 separately can break a computation that is shared at the operator level. Instead of first searching for a coordinate transformation between factorizations, SLOA removes this ambiguity by merging the invariant local operators themselves and factorizing the merged operators only after alignment. 

B.2 Transformer circuits and operator structure 

Several analyses of transformers emphasize that attention heads are naturally described through composed circuits rather than isolated projection matrices [Elhage et al., 2021, Dar et al., 2023, Vaswani et al., 2017, Theus et al., 2026]. In this view, the QK circuit controls the pre-softmax attention scores, while the VO, or OV, circuit controls how attended token representations are written back to the residual stream. This perspective has been influential for understanding transformer computation, but it has been less directly exploited in training-free model merging. SLOA turns this circuit-level view into a concrete merging algorithm. For each attention head, it constructs and aligns the QK and VO operators as residual-space maps. This also separates the operator being merged from the rank used to implement it. Each task-specific head realizes a low-rank operator because the head dimension is limited, but the merged multi-task operator can have higher effective rank when different tasks use different subspaces. SLOA therefore treats rank as a capacity-control mechanism: after solving for the merged ambient operator, it can either factorize it at the original head rank or retain additional singular directions through rank expansion. This differs from spectral task-vector methods, which apply SVD to task updates in weight space; in SLOA, the SVD is applied to the merged functional attention operator and determines how much of that operator is represented in the final head. 20 C Technical comparison to RegMean, RegMean++, rotation-symmetry match-ing, and ProDistill

C.2 Comparison with RegMean++ 

We next compare SLOA with RegMean++ [Nguyen et al., 2026]. RegMean++ identifies an important limitation of RegMean: independently merging each linear layer using task-model activations ignores the way representation errors propagate through the merged network. RegMean++ addresses this by recomputing statistics progressively using activations produced by previously merged layers. SLOA shares this sequential motivation, but differs in the local objective, the treatment of attention, the operator geometry, and the final factorization step. 

RegMean++ as progressive RegMean. RegMean++ keeps the core RegMean closed-form linear-layer solve. The key modification is how the layer statistics are collected. However, its parameterization still acts on the individual task-vector coordinates of the original projection matrices. It does not explicitly identify the invariant QK and VO operators, nor does it merge in 30 the quotient space induced by attention-head reparameterizations. Consequently, the factorization ambiguity remains inside the optimization problem. SLOA removes this ambiguity before solving the merge. Since the optimization variable is the composed operator itself, two different factorizations that realize the same QK or VO operator correspond to the same object in SLOA. The merged operator is therefore invariant to internal changes of head coordinates, and factorization is postponed until after the operator has been aligned. 

SLOA is more fine-grained than layer-wise distillation inside transformer blocks. ProDistill progresses layer by layer. The layer is treated as the unit whose output representation should match the teacher representation. This is a general design and can be applied to nonlinear modules because the optimization is performed by gradient descent. SLOA instead uses a topological order over local operators inside the network. For a transformer block, SLOA can separately align LayerNorm affine maps, standard linear maps, QK attention operators, and VO attention operators. This allows the method to respect the internal computational structure of attention rather than treating the whole attention layer or block as a single black-box feature map. In particular, SLOA distinguishes the two roles of self-attention: 

• the QK operator determines token-token routing through the pre-softmax attention scores; 

• the VO operator determines what information is written back to the logit after attention weights are applied. This decomposition is absent from ProDistill. ProDistill aligns block outputs, whereas SLOA aligns the local functional mechanisms that produce those outputs. 

Both methods are progressive, but the progressive state has a different meaning. Both ProDistill and SLOA use a progressive procedure in which later modules are merged under activations affected by earlier merges. However, the role of these activations differs. In ProDistill, the progressive state consists of paired features (𝑧 1, 𝑧 2), where 𝑧 1 is produced by the partially merged student and 𝑧 2 is produced by the corresponding teacher. The layer objective trains task-vector coefficients so that the student layer output matches the teacher layer output on these paired inputs. SLOA decouples operator alignment from factorization rank. A further distinction is architectural capacity. ProDistill keeps the original model architecture and learns coefficients for the existing task-vector parameters. In particular, an attention head retains the original head dimension. Thus, ProDistill does not create a mechanism for increasing the rank of the QK or VO operators after merging. 31 SLOA explicitly separates the ambient operator from its final factorization. This rank-expansion mechanism is not available in ProDistill, because ProDistill does not decouple the merged attention operator from its original factorization. 

The two methods make different trade-offs. ProDistill is a general distillation-based merging framework. Its strength is that it can optimize feature matching for arbitrary modules without requiring the module to be linear in a chosen operator. This generality comes at the cost of gradient-based optimization and a parameter-coordinate task-vector representation. SLOA makes the opposite trade-off. It focuses on modules that admit a local behavior map linear in an appropriate operator. This restriction enables closed-form merging, data-dependent operator geometry, attention-operator invariance, and post-merge rank control. Therefore, SLOA is not simply a training-free variant of ProDistill. It changes the mathematical object being merged: from task-vector coordinates trained to match layer features, to local functional operators solved by sequential regularized regression.

E.2.1 Dataset for vision task. 

In our vision model merging experiments, we utilize the datasets and task-specific fine-tuned checkpoints provided by the FusionBench framework [Tang et al., 2024]. E.2.3 Dataset for LLM experiments 

For the LLM merging experiments, we did not use an off-the-shelf pool of LoRA experts for LLaMA-2-7B [Touvron et al., 2023]; instead, we constructed our own task pool. We train three LoRA experts [Hu et al., 2022] independently from the LLaMA-2-7B base checkpoint, each targeting all linear projections at rank 32( 𝛼 =64), for 3 epochs with a cosine learning-rate schedule (peak learning rate 2 × 10 −4) and an effective batch size of 32: a math expert on MetaMathQA [Yu et al., 2023], a code expert on Magicoder-Evol-Instruct-110K [Wei et al., 2024], and an instruction-following expert on the persona-conditioned instruction-following split of Tulu-3-SFT [Lambert et al., 2025]. Each expert is trained with a single, fixed prompt template applied to every example (the code expert uses Magicoder’s own instruction/response wrapper; the math and instruction-following experts share an Alpaca-style instruction/response template) see 4, and the same template is reused uniformly at both calibration and evaluation time. We evaluate each task on its corresponding standard benchmark rather than a held-out split of its own training corpus: GSM8K [Cobbe et al., 2021] for math (exact-match accuracy under flexible answer extraction), HumanEval [Chen et al., 2021] for code (pass@1), and IFEval [Zhou et al., 2023] for instruction-following (prompt-level strict accuracy). All three are evaluated zero-shot through lm-evaluation-harness 3, with each task’s training template baked into its eval prompt. F.1 Scaling to a larger vision backbone: ViT-B/16 

To test whether SLOA’s advantage persists as model capacity grows, we repeat the ViT-B/32 multi-task merging experiments ( §4.1) on the larger ViT-B/16 backbone across the same 8-, 14-, and 20-task benchmarks (Table 8); as a complementary scaling direction, we also evaluate SLOA on a much larger, 7B-parameter decoder-only LLM in the main text ( §4.3, Table 4). The trend mirrors ViT-B/32: SLOA’s margin over the best baseline widens as the number of merged tasks grows. On 8 tasks, the data-free baseline Iso-CTS is narrowly the strongest method overall (89.01 vs. 88.04 for SLOA ( 𝑟 = 128)); by 14 tasks, SLOA (85.16) has overtaken it (84.54); and by 20 tasks, SLOA leads by nearly 5 points (83.43 vs. 78.50). As on ViT-B/32, increasing 

𝑟 from 64 to 128 improves performance at every task count, with the gain growing alongside the number of merged tasks. Ortiz-Jimenez et al. [2023] show that larger, better-pretrained vision backbones tend to be more weight-disentangled than smaller ones, so task-specific fine-tuning directions interfere less under simple weight-space combination. This may explain why data-free baselines remain competitive with SLOA on ViT-B/16 when only a few tasks are merged. However, as the number of merged tasks grows and interference increases, SLOA, which aligns functional operators rather than raw weights, wins by an increasingly clear margin.

F.4 Error accumulation across network depth 

To directly assess whether and how merging error compounds across the network’s topological ordering of operators, we measure, at each vision-encoder block, the mean relative 𝐿 2 error between the merged model’s activation and the corresponding task-specific expert’s activation, averaged across all 20 tasks. We report this on ViT-B/16 in the 20-task setting, the most challenging regime in our vision benchmarks, comparing SLOA ( 𝑟 ∈ { 64 , 128 , 256 }) against RegMean, RegMean++, and Iso-CTS (best performing data-free method). Figure 6(a) shows that error compounds with depth for every method, but at sharply different rates. Through the first six blocks, SLOA and RegMean++ track closely; the two only begin to diverge from block 7 onward, once upstream discrepancies have had several blocks to accumulate. SLOA’s error also grows more slowly relative to its own input-level error, peaking at 1.65–1.69 × its starting value versus 1.87 × for RegMean++ and 2.08 × for RegMean. This depth-dependent divergence, rather than a uniform improvement at every block, localizes SLOA’s advantage to the later stages of the network, where uncorrected merging error would otherwise compound through the topological ordering of blocks. Expanding the rank from 𝑟 = 64 to 𝑟 = 256 narrows the error gap further. 

F.5 Decoupling the QK and OV circuits 

To test whether the QK and OV circuits should always use the same rank, we vary 𝑟 qk and 𝑟 ov independently on ViT-B/32 with 20 tasks and 1024 calibration samples, while fixing the other rank at 64. Figure 7 shows three main trends. First, expanding either circuit alone improves performance only modestly and saturates after roughly 𝑟 = 256. Second, QK expansion is more effective than OV expansion, indicating that query-key interactions are a stronger bottleneck for task-specific representation. Third, jointly expanding both circuits gives the best and most sustained gains. F.6 Sensitivity to regularization strength 

The regularization term in Eq. (5) is controlled by 𝛼 and stabilizes the solution when calibration data is limited. Figure 9 evaluates this effect on ViT-B/32 with 8 tasks. With few samples, especially 64 or 128, larger 

𝛼 improves performance by anchoring the solution toward the task-specific weights. With more samples, 256 or 512, the activation-alignment term is already sufficiently constrained, so strong regularization becomes unnecessary and can reduce performance. The optimal 𝛼 therefore decreases as the calibration set grows, approaching 𝛼 ≈ 0.05 at larger sample sizes. These results suggest that SLOA is robust to 𝛼 when enough data is available, while the regularizer is most useful in the low-data regime. 

F.7 Component ablation. 

SLOA extends standard linear-layer merging with three components: sequential operator alignment, the QK operator, and the OV operator ( §3.1). Figure 8 compares four configurations on ViT-B/32 with 20 tasks: sequential alignment with isolated weight merging, sequential alignment plus QK only, sequential alignment plus OV only, and the full SLOA. Sequential alignment alone is already strong, confirming that the sequential operator structure is a major source of improvement. However, adding only QK or only OV degrades performance, as the two circuits are tightly coupled inside self-attention; modifying one without the other introduces a functional error that propagates through later layers. In contrast, combining both operators recovers this loss and improves over sequential alignment alone, showing that QK and OV should be merged jointly. 

F.8 Sequential correction versus training-free baselines.

G.3 Inference cost 

Table 15 isolates the cost of deploying the merged model itself, separate from the one-time merging cost reported above. Since every baseline in Table 10 merges by combining weights within the original 64/64 attention-head architecture, all of them, share an identical inference profile: throughput of approximately 3257 images/s, 0.307 ms/image latency, and 0.60GB peak memory. At the default rank ( 𝑟 = 64, no expansion), SLOA’s merged model is indistinguishable in inference cost from every baseline, while still improving accuracy to 82.37%. Rank expansion to 𝑟 = 128 or 𝑟 = 256 increases inference time and GPU memory, both direct consequences of the wider attention head, in exchange for further accuracy gains (83.53% and 83.74%, respectively). This is a deliberate, user-controlled trade-off between capacity and test-time cost.
