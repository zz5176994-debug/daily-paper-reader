# Abstract 

We study learning rate (LR) scaling for hybrid architectures combining Transformer and State-Space Model (SSM) blocks, a class adopted by several recent production language models. In particular, we focus on the gap between the theoretical scaling rules derived for SSMs under zero-order-hold (ZOH) discretization at infinite width with growing state size, and the field-standard practical implementations using simplified-ZOH Mamba at fixed state size. Surprisingly, in this practical regime hybrid architectures achieve a near-zero LR transfer gap across widths 256 –2048 

and depths 4–32 up to billion-parameter scale using only the original μP prescrip-tion, even though SSM operations fall outside its Tensor Programs representability conditions and every parameterization we test fails the standard coordinate-check diagnostic of μP correctness. We attribute this to a two-condition decomposition of LR transfer in hybrid architectures: a global update-to-weight invariance, enforced by μP’s initialization and LR scaling; and a local per-component balance, provided by AdamW’s per-parameter normalization. Our observations show that the optimal LR is invariant to width up to 8×, that this width invariance holds across depth, sequence length, batch size, and Transformer-to-SSM ratio, and that it transfers to Nemotron-H, a production hybrid outside our custom architecture set. We hope these findings fill the gap between theoretical scaling rules and practical hybrid implementations, and stimulate further research toward bridging it.

22 Background and Related Work 

2.1 Maximal Update Parameterization ( μP) 

Under standard parameterization (SP), weight matrices are initialized as W ∼ N (0 , 1/√d) per element with a single learning rate η shared across all parameters; as fan-in width d grows, the update-to-weight ratio ∥∆W ∥F /∥W ∥F shifts with d and the optimal LR has to be re-tuned at each target scale. Yang et al. [2021] introduced Maximal Update Parameterization ( μP) within the Tensor Programs framework, which scales hidden-layer initialization to W ∼ N (0 , 1/d ) and learning rates to ηbase /d so this ratio stays O(1) as d → ∞ , allowing the LR found at a small proxy 

width to transfer directly to the target. The Tensor Programs framework gives a rigorous infinite-width limit for architectures composed of representable operations, and it was validated for pure Transformers up to 6.7B parameters [Yang et al., 2021] and studied empirically up to 10B [Lingle, 2024]. Yang and Littwin [2023] extended the theory to Adam [Kingma and Ba, 2015] and other entrywise optimizers, characterizing μP as the unique maximal-update (feature-learning) limit for these optimizers. Subsequent works extend μP to other architectures and optimizer families [Dey et al., 2024, Zheng et al., 2025, Mała´ snicki et al., 2026, Qiu et al., 2025]; Hayou [2026] gives the first formal width-transfer proof for linear MLP, and Noci et al. [2024] provide a complementary spectral analysis showing μP induces a super consistency of the loss-landscape sharpness across widths. 

2.2 μP-SSM: Scaling Rules for State-Space Models 

State space models are the family of architectures built on continuous-time linear dynamical systems [Gu et al., 2020, 2022], represented by Mamba [Gu and Dao, 2024]. Chennuru Vankadara et al. [2024] showed that three operations in the Mamba block violate the representability conditions of the Tensor Programs framework, so standard μP does not guarantee width-independent dynamics for SSMs. They derived SSM-specific scaling rules ( μP-SSM) such that each component’s contribution to the output remains constant with width; their analysis establishes that SSMs fail the feature learning condition under both SGD and Adam in the infinite-width limit, with empirical validation under SGD on pure S4 and Mamba. Three gaps motivate extending μP to hybrid architectures. (1) Pure-SSM only : Chennuru Vankadara et al. [2024]’s Assumption 3.1 takes all non-SSM components to already follow μP, focusing the analysis on the SSM layer alone; the interaction between well-parameterized Transformer blocks and SSM blocks in a hybrid was not tested. (2) Infinite-width asymptotic : their claims hold as 

Nu, N x → ∞ (where Nu is the SSM operating width and Nx the state dimension), and the asymptotic failure may not manifest at the practical finite widths (256–2048) we test, particularly when AdamW’s per-parameter normalization ( g/ √v) compensates. (3) True-ZOH with growing Nx: the derivation assumes the true zero-order-hold discretization with state size Nx → ∞ , while the field-standard Mamba implementation [Gu and Dao, 2024] uses a simplified-ZOH at fixed Nx=16 regardless of width.

Tomihari and Sato [2026] prove the formal mechanism on pure Transformers: Adam’s coordinate-wise normalization makes updates depend on gradient signs, conferring robustness to cross-block heterogeneity. 32.4 Hybrid Architectures and Production Training 

Hybrid Transformer-SSM models are increasingly used in production language models [NVIDIA, 2025, Jamba Team, 2025, Dong et al., 2025, Zuo et al., 2025] and studied as competitive alternatives to pure Transformers in controlled comparisons [Waleffe et al., 2024]; production hybrid training, however, generally relies on per-scale hyperparameter tuning rather than μP-style transfer. Falcon-H1 [Zuo et al., 2025] is the closest concurrent work that tunes hybrids at scale: they jointly optimize 35 per-component multipliers (LRs, weight decay, and forward-pass activation scales) for hybrid architectures up to 34B parameters. Their stated motivation is production training stability (e.g., scaling the ∆-projection to prevent its explosion) and per-component performance tuning, not μP-style LR transfer; their work and ours therefore solve different problems, with our work isolating the minimal sufficient ingredients for the LR-transfer subproblem under AdamW. Nemotron-H [NVIDIA, 2025], which we revisit in Section 4.3, similarly relies on per-scale hyperparameter tuning despite using a different hybrid composition (Mamba-2 [Dao and Gu, 2024] SSD blocks with ∼8% attention layers and squared-ReLU FFN). 

# 3 Experimental Setup 

We empirically validate μP on hybrid Transformer-SSM architectures, focusing on LR transfer across widths while maintaining training stability. Production hybrid architectures use a wide range of mixing patterns without consensus on a canonical design (Section 2.4), and training dynamics depend on block composition. Therefore we first probe LR transferability at heterogeneous block interfaces, where training dynamics are most likely to shift; then across depth and width; and finally in larger training runs with downstream evaluation.

4.3 Generalization to Nemotron-H 

To test whether naïve μP generalizes beyond our simple custom architectures, we validate on Nemotron-H [NVIDIA, 2025], a production hybrid using Mamba-2 [Dao and Gu, 2024], squared-ReLU FFN, and grouped-query attention [Ainslie et al., 2023] without rotary embeddings [Su et al., 2024]. We follow the Nemotron-H architecture: Mamba-2 (N), squared-ReLU FFN (F), and attention-only blocks (A) with GQA at ratio 4 and no RoPE (Appendix C.6). This architecture is designed with different block types than our set. Naïve μP achieves η∗=1 .0 across all (depth, width) combinations on Nemotron-H, matching the optimum found across our custom architectures. The transfer gap is 0.0% at every cell, within a broad basin ( η∈{ 1, 3, 5} all within ∼3% of optimum) that makes transfer robust to LR misspecification. SP’s optimum shifts 3× across width ( 0.003 → 0.001 ), incurring a 1.9% transfer penalty at both depths if the proxy LR is reused. 

# 5 Analysis 

5.1 Naïve μP Violates Coordinate Checks but Learning Rate Transfers 

μP’s LR transfer is theoretically expected to hold only when the coordinate check is satisfied [Yang et al., 2021], i.e., when pre-activation magnitudes remain width-invariant. However, on hybrid architectures, all four parameterizations we test fail the coordinate check (Figure 8): activations vary with width across SP, naïve μP, and both block-wise variants, more than in a pure Transformer (Table 31). Despite this universal coordinate check failure, naïve μP achieves 0.0% transfer gap across every architecture in Table 1, while block-wise μP’s optimum LR drifts with width on every SSM-bearing architecture. Coordinate checks therefore do not predict LR transfer on hybrids; Section 5.2 instead proposes a two-condition decomposition as an explanatory diagnostic of where transfer holds and where it breaks. 75.2 Two Conditions for Scalable Transfer in Hybrid Architectures 

We propose a two-condition decomposition of LR transfer, drawing on existing theory and supported by an optimizer ablation. 

Condition 1: Global update-to-weight ratio invariance. For a parameter matrix W , define 

ρ(W ) = ∥∆W ∥F

; update-to-weight ratio invariance requires ρ(W ) = O(1) as dmodel → ∞ for every parameter group. This is the design principle of μP: Yang et al. [2021], Yang and Littwin [2023] establish width-independent ρ, for SGD and Adam-style optimizers. Under SP, ρ = O(η√d) grows with width, causing the optimal learning rate to shift with model width; under naïve μP, ρ = O(η)

depends on the learning rate but is width-independent. Section 5.1 showed that LR transfer holds in our hybrid setting despite a width-invariance violation at the activation level, motivating the second condition below. 

Condition 2: Local per-component update balance. For any pair of parameter groups WX , W Y

at configuration c, let βX/Y (η; c) = ∥∆WX ∥F /∥∆WY ∥F denote the ratio of their per-step update magnitudes; a width-shift in β means the optimization rebalances across components as the model scales, breaking the proxy’s local optimality at the target. 5.3 Optimizer Choice Under the Two Conditions 

For Condition 1, naïve μP delivers ρ = O(η) across widths under AdamW [Yang et al., 2021, Yang and Littwin, 2023], so we treat Condition 1 as held under AdamW throughout this subsection. For Condition 2, in contrast, the choice of optimizer determines which of two normalization mechanisms is active: per-component LR scaling, or per-parameter normalization. 

Under SGD: per-component LR scaling. Under SGD, βM/T ∝ (ηM /η T ) γM/T , where γM/T =RMS( ∇WM L)/RMS( ∇WT L) is the gradient-RMS ratio for a Mamba/Transformer parameter pair. Width-dependence in γ must therefore be canceled by per-component LR scaling, which is the role of Chennuru Vankadara et al. [2024]’s SSM rules in their derivation regime. 

Under AdamW: per-parameter normalization. Under AdamW, ηeff (W ) = η/ √vW normalizes each parameter’s gradient by its own running RMS magnitude, so the update direction is dominated by gradient sign rather than magnitude (Tomihari and Sato, 2026, Thm. 4.7–4.9; Balles and Hennig, 2018, Zhang et al., 2024). The ratio βM/T then decouples from γ, and naïve μP achieves ∆β → 0

without any per-component LR scaling (interpretive across the SSM boundary). Table 4 confirms this and exposes a stacking failure: the (SGD, naïve μP) row gives ∆βAlog /FFN =235 –342% ; AdamW closes the same pair to 20 –21% (naïve μP row); adding Vankadara’s per-component LR scaling on top inflates it to 166 –436% (block-wise μP row), as the explicit ηA ∝ Nu correction stacks with the normalization AdamW already supplies.

5.4 Summary 

The decomposition isolates two distinct failure modes of LR transfer in hybrid architectures: Con-dition 1 violations shift the optimum LR with width (the canonical μP failure mode); Condition 2 violations rebalance components across widths (a finite-width hybrid-specific failure mode). In our regime (simplified-ZOH Mamba at fixed Nx=16 ), the μP-SSM scaling rules do not show evidence of delivering Condition 2 under either optimizer; under SGD the rule’s intended cancellation does not apply, and under AdamW the rules add scaling on top of the normalization g/ √v already supplies. AdamW’s g/ √v alone delivers Condition 2 across the SSM boundary. Whether either picture changes at infinite width or under true-ZOH SSMs with growing Nx remains open. In this section, we extracted two conditions from existing theory, and neither covers our hybrid setting under our regime. We use the two conditions as explanatory diagnostics and support this empirically (Tables 4–6, Section 5.1; Appendices E.1–E.3).

Limitations. Our results characterize a specific operating regime; three scope boundaries are worth flagging. First, billion scale, the full training horizon, and multiple seeds are validated individually rather than jointly: the LR-transfer sweep reaches 1.24B parameters (three seeds, 20k steps), and one 1.24B model trains cleanly to a Chinchilla-optimal horizon (24.9B tokens) at the transferred LR with a single LR and seed (Appendix C.3); whether the near-zero gap persists when all three hold at once remains open. Larger architectures, including Mixture-of-Experts, remain untested, and Mamba-3 [Lahoti et al., 2026] is left to future work: its complex-valued state lies outside the real-valued μPrules, and we know of no complex-state μP formulation; a hybrid built on a real-valued non-Mamba recurrent mixer, Gated Linear Attention (GLA) [Yang et al., 2024], does transfer (Appendix C.5.3). Second, we additionally ran Sophia [Liu et al., 2024], which also normalizes per element and likewise transfers ( 0.00% gap on HHHH and MHMH at 20k steps, three seeds), whereas Muon [Jordan et al., 2024], under its own recommended scaling, does not (Appendix E.3.7); the two differ in prescription as well as normalizer, so this does not isolate per-element normalization. Finally, two formal-theory questions remain open: whether the two-condition decomposition is jointly sufficient for transfer at infinite width, and a faithful test of μP-SSM under ZOH SSMs with growing Nx, the regime in which Chennuru Vankadara et al. [2024]’s prescription was derived (our comparisons apply it to the field-standard simplified-ZOH Mamba [Gu and Dao, 2024] at fixed Nx=16 ; Appendix A.4).

Where this leaves μP-SSM and our work. Same-group follow-ups extend the μP framework along axes other than the SSM rule itself (Haas et al. [2024] ( μP2) address sharpness-aware minimization; sparse-architecture extensions appear in Dey et al. [2024], Mała´ snicki et al. [2026]), and a side-by-side study under matched Nu/N x has not yet appeared in the published literature. Production hybrids that deploy μP at scale (Falcon-H1 with 35 per-component multipliers; community Mamba-

μP implementations with zero-initialized convolutions) adopt their own per-component recipes targeting production-training stability, a problem distinct from the LR-transfer question we focus on (Section 2.4). Under AdamW on hybrids with fixed Nx, the block-wise μP rule fires at the gradient level (Appendix E.2) but is redundant with Adam’s g/ √v at training time (Section 5.3). 17 Our approximated block-wise variant addresses this redundancy by scaling ηA and ηB with the naïve-μP exponent (dropping the ηA ∈ Θ( Nu) term that AdamW already supplies), preserving the per-component initialization structure while avoiding the double normalization. 

Regime gaps from the original derivation. We work outside the regime in which Chen-nuru Vankadara et al. [2024]’s rules were derived along four axes: (i) hybrid Transformer-SSM blocks vs the pure SSMs of their experiments; (ii) AdamW vs the SGD step from which their Table 1 is derived; (iii) fixed Nx=16 vs growing Nx (Nu/N x=16 in their reported experiments; proportional scaling of Nx with Nu is impractical at scale because SSM state memory scales as 

Nu ×Nx, and is therefore not used in any production hybrid we are aware of); and (iv) the discretiza-tion Bd ≈ ∆t · B used in the official Mamba library [Gu and Dao, 2024] vs the true-ZOH form 

Bd = (∆ t · A)−1(exp(∆ t · A) − I) · ∆t · B underlying their Table 1 derivation. The simplified-ZOH choice (iv) is the field standard, shared by Mamba-2 [Dao and Gu, 2024], Jamba [Jamba Team, 2025], Nemotron-H [NVIDIA, 2025], and Falcon-H1 [Zuo et al., 2025], so it is not a quirk of our setup. We apply Vankadara’s initialization formulas and (for the non-approximated variant) their LR exponents anchored at our proxy width N ref  

> u

=512 ; whether the same exponents are theoretically optimal under simplified ZOH is not established. Implementing true-ZOH Mamba and re-evaluating block-wise μP on its own derived discretization, ideally also under proportional Nu/N x scaling and at production-scale Nx, is left to future work. 

Relation to the main text. The “block-wise μP (approximated)” results in Section 4 and Table 1 use our approximated variant (Section 3, Appendix A.3) under AdamW on hybrids with a single shared ηbase . The full block-wise μP prescription is reported alongside in Table 1 (transfer column) and Table 11 above (convergence comparison); a gradient-level comparison is in Appendix E.2. Our main claim (naïve μP + AdamW achieves 0.0% transfer gap across all 7 architectures) rests on naïve 

μP’s own empirical behavior, not on a comparison to block-wise. In the side-by-side comparisons we do report, naïve achieves lower loss than both block-wise variants under AdamW on hybrids, and the full prescription’s optimum additionally drifts with width on every SSM-bearing architecture in the 2k-step transfer grid.

C.6 Nemotron-H Block Composition 

Section 4.3 reports an LR-transfer experiment on a Nemotron-H [NVIDIA, 2025] reproduction, but the headline architecture set used in the rest of the paper (T, M, H blocks) does not include the block types Nemotron-H is built from. For reproducibility, we record the block-type letters our implementation uses and the layer-pattern they produce. 24 Block types. Nemotron-H uses three block types, which we expose via single-letter codes consistent with the production paper: • N: Mamba-2 layer [Dao and Gu, 2024] (SSD-style selective scan; requires the mamba_ssm 

CUDA kernel). • F: FFN layer (squared-ReLU MLP only, no attention or SSM in this layer). • A: attention-only layer that replaces an N in an N-F pair when sparse attention is inserted. This is distinct from our headline T (Transformer block: GQA + SwiGLU FFN, fused) and M (Mamba-1) blocks: Nemotron-H separates the FFN into its own F layer instead of fusing it with attention or SSM, and inserts a small fraction of attention-only A layers rather than full Transformer blocks. 

Layer pattern. The base pattern alternates N and F at every depth (Mamba-2 followed by MLP). A small fraction of attention layers (default α=0 .08 ) is dispersed evenly by replacing an N with an A in some N-F pairs; the first layer is always N and the last is always F. At the depths we test: depth 16 : NFNFNFNFAFNFNFNF (7 N + 8 F + 1 A; 6.2% attention) depth 32 : NFNFNFNFNFNFNFNFAFNFNFNFNFNFNFNF (15 N + 16 F + 1 A; 3.1% attention) The attention fraction we observe ( 3–6% ) is slightly below the ∼8% target because rounding to integer attention positions takes precedence over the exact ratio at small depth. 

Deviations from the production specification. Our Nemotron-H reproduction matches the pub-lished model on the load-bearing components: Mamba-2 SSD blocks, squared-ReLU FFN, no positional encoding in attention, and an ∼8% attention-layer density (rounded to integer positions per depth as above). We tested two attention implementations to bracket the production model: • Multi-head attention (MHA) variant. Our initial reproduction used full multi-head attention ( nkv = nheads ) without rotary position embeddings. This is simpler than the production architecture but probes the same N/F/A composition. • Precise GQA variant. A second reproduction uses grouped-query attention at ratio 4 (nkv = nheads /4) with no rotary embeddings, matching the published Nemotron-H attention configuration. This is the implementation reported in Section 4.3 and Table 3. • Dense attention pattern (no sliding window or other sparsification). The A-layers are full dense self-attention in both variants. Nemotron-H reports sparse attention at production scale; the relevant μP analysis for sparse architectures is in Dey et al. [2024], who show that sparsity-aware μP rules generalize the standard prescription. Our finding ( 0.0% LR transfer gap on Nemotron-H under naïve μP) is therefore a result on the dense-attention reproduction and does not yet probe the sparse-pattern regime; we expect transfer to extend, but flag this as untested. • Smaller SSM state dimension. Our reproduction uses Nx=16 across all widths, while the production Nemotron-H 8B model uses Nx=128 in its Mamba-2 SSD blocks. Our small-scale validation tests the LR-transfer mechanism rather than reproducing the production model exactly; the field-standard Nx=16 choice (also used in Mamba-1, Jamba, and Falcon-H1) keeps our setup comparable across architectures. 

MHA vs GQA: transfer property holds in both. Both attention implementations show ∼0% LR transfer gap under naïve μP, but the optimum LR and absolute loss differ. Both implementations preserve the headline transfer property: naïve μP with AdamW achieves ∼0% transfer gap independent of the attention configuration.

D.7 Gradient Clipping Threshold 

We use grad_clip =1 .0 throughout the main experiments and verify that the LR transfer claim does not depend on this specific threshold. We sweep grad_clip ∈ { off , 0.5, 1.0, 5.0} on MHMH at

D.8 Where Naïve μP Transfer Does Not Extend 

The headline claim of 0.0% LR transfer gap holds for naïve μP + AdamW across the architectures, widths, and depths in our main grid. This subsection documents four boundary conditions where the transfer property weakens or does not apply. Each case fits the two-condition decomposition of Section 5.2; together they sharpen, not contradict, the headline claim. 30 (i) Standard parameterization. SP is the baseline against which μP was originally designed; without per-component re-scaling, the optimum LR shifts 3–10 × with width across the architectures we tested (Appendix C). On the imbalanced Jamba layout (Section D.6), the SP optimum drifts from 

η=0 .01 at w=256 to η=0 .003 at w=1024 , with the proxy LR producing catastrophic loss at the target width. This is the regime μP was designed to fix; our headline claim is measured against it. 

(ii) Approximated block-wise μP on SSM-heavy serial layouts. On the serial T:M sweep (Sec-tion D.6, Table 29), the approximated optimum drifts by one grid step at w ≥ 512 for SSM-bearing layouts ( M1T3 , Jamba, MM ; M3T1 only at w=1024 ), staying at η∗=1 .0 on TT and MTMT . Reusing the proxy η=1 .0 at w=1024 incurs a residual loss penalty of 4–36% on the SSM-dominated layouts (Table 30); the same parameterization transfers cleanly with 0% gap on the balanced MTMT layout in the main results. 

(iii) Faithful block-wise μP under AdamW at scale. The SSM-specific rules of Chennuru Vankadara et al. [2024] were derived and validated for pure SSMs trained with SGD in the infinite-width limit. On hybrids at 20k steps on W IKI TEXT -103, the faithful prescription produces uniformly higher loss than naïve μP across the architectures and widths we measured: +5 .6 to +7 .7% on HHHH, MHMH, and MTMT at w ∈ { 256 , 512 , 1024 } (full table in Appendix A.4). At the 2k-step transfer horizon, its optimum LR also drifts with width on every SSM-bearing architecture ( η∗=1 at w=1024 across all 6, with η=3 diverging at that width on each); only pure-Transformer ( TT ) transfers cleanly. The approximated variant used in the main experiments stays within 0.5% of naïve μP at w=1024 by dropping the ηA ∈ Θ( Nu) term that AdamW’s g/ √v already supplies (Section 5.3, Appendix A.3). 

(iv) Optimizers without per-parameter normalization. Plain SGD does not exhibit the same transfer behavior even when μP is applied (full optimizer ablation in Section E.3). This localizes the LR-transfer property of naïve μP + AdamW to the per-parameter-normalization mechanism ( g/ √v), which matches the two-condition decomposition: μP alone does not deliver transfer in our hybrid setting without an optimizer that supplies Condition 2. Naïve μP + AdamW transfers cleanly because μP supplies the global condition and AdamW supplies the local one. Adding component-specific scaling on top does not help at the widths and budgets we test.

[2024]’s framework as follows: mamba_A_log is the SSM state-transition matrix A; the B and C matrices in Vankadara’s prescription are slices of the input-projection x_proj (the rows producing the per-step B and C at each position). We use 

ssm_split_projections =True for this sweep so that B and C are separate parameter groups, allowing Vankadara’s per-component rules ( ηB ∝ 1/√Nu, ηC ∝ 1/(Nx

√Nu)) to act at the granularity their derivation assumes; this matches the configuration used in the main width-transfer experiments (Table 1). The labels mamba_in_proj (dmodel → 2dinner input gate) and mamba_out_proj (dinner →

dmodel ) are not B or C; they are the input/output projections that surround the SSM core. 

Effective update at step 1: the direct μP test under SGD. μP theory constrains the update 

(ηeff (W, scheme ) · grad (W )), not the raw gradient. Vankadara’s prescription predicts that the effective update is width-invariant ( α(Nu) = 0 ) under SGD when the per-component rules are properly applied. We compute the step-1 effective update RMS for Alog , B, and C under both naïve μPand the faithful Vankadara prescription, and fit log u ∼ α log Nu + γ log d + log C over the joint 

(d, expand , d_state ) grid (3 seeds, n = 108 per row; the headline result is reported in the main paper as Table 6). 

Vankadara’s prediction holds at init for B and C but fails for Alog . At step 1 the faithful prescription produces near-zero Nu dependence for B (α = +0 .006 ) and C (α = +0 .029 ): ηB and 

ηC cancel the underlying gradient growth and deliver the predicted width-invariant update. For 

Alog , however, the faithful update has α = +1 .00 , growing approximately linearly with Nu. This is not a finite-width residual: under naïve μP the Alog update already has α = +0 .86 at step 1, so 

ηA ∝ Nu is not canceling an underlying 1/N u decay (no such decay is present in our gradients), but 

amplifying the existing Nu-dependence. This is consistent with the simplified-ZOH discretization gap (Appendix A.4): Vankadara’s derivation assumes true ZOH, where Alog ’s gradient may decay as predicted, but our implementation uses the field-standard simplified form (Mamba library, Mamba-2 [Dao and Gu, 2024], Jamba [Jamba Team, 2025], Nemotron-H [NVIDIA, 2025], Falcon-H1 [Zuo et al., 2025]). 

The init-level invariance does not survive training. Across the stable window (steps 100–199), the faithful α(Nu) for B drifts from +0 .006 to +1 .35 (∆α = +1 .34 ) and for C from +0 .029 to 35 +0 .71 (∆α = +0 .68 ); for Alog , faithful drifts from +1 .00 to +3 .42 (∆α = +2 .42 ). The explicit per-component LR rules thus achieve their predicted invariance only at step 1 for B and C; during training the structure is destroyed at a rate larger for the faithful prescription than for naïve ( ∆α

for naïve stays in [0 .33 , 0.68] across all three components). The init-level Vankadara replication is therefore a 1-step phenomenon, not a property the framework continues to enforce as the optimizer trajectory leaves the infinite-width regime; the LR-operating-point caveat below sharpens this reading. 

Stable window: LR-specific operating points, not scaling laws. The stable-window exponents in Table 6 are at base LR η = 1 under SGD and should be read as LR-specific equilibrium operating points rather than fundamental scaling laws. Cross-checking naïve μP SGD stable-window fits at base LR η = 1 and η = 10 , the coefficients inflate 4–18 × while residuals remain near-Gaussian in both cases; alternative functional forms (semi-log, log+ linear envelope) produce the same coefficient inflation, so LR-dependence is not a log-log artifact. The residual-shape distinction is robust across LRs and describes a structural difference between optimizers: AdamW’s g/ √v introduces coordinate-wise coupling that disrupts log-linear structure (heavy-tailed residuals, excess kurtosis +5 to +19 ), while SGD’s unit update reflects pure weight-trajectory dynamics (near-Gaussian residuals). The Table 6 stable-window column should be read as confirming that the init-level invariance for B and 

C does not persist along the trajectory, not as a precise width-scaling exponent for any particular LR. 

Outlier structure (AdamW, naïve μP). Pooled log-residuals under AdamW are heavy-tailed for every scheme (excess kurtosis +5 to +19 , skewness +1 to +3 ). Top-1% |residual | events cluster in specific (d, expand ) cells rather than uniformly (Fig. 12): for naïve μP, expand = 1 is uniformly quiet ( ∼ 0% ), while expand ∈ { 2, 4} concentrate outliers at the extreme d values. Per-run trajectories show these as isolated single-step gradient collapses: transient update-instability events, not Gaussian measurement noise. This clarifies that the low R2 of AdamW stable-window fits reflects structured outliers (configuration-dependent transient spikes) rather than purely random scatter. 

The cleanliness–performance inversion under AdamW. The same effective-update analysis run on AdamW data (not shown for length) inverts the ordering: at the stable-window operating point under AdamW, the faithful prescription’s Alog update has the cleanest log-log structure (highest 

R2), but block-wise underperforms naïve on hybrid training (Appendix A.4, Appendix E.3). This is consistent with the redundancy story (Section 5.3): under SGD, ηA ∝ Nu is doing useful but in our setting overcorrected work for Alog , while under AdamW the g/ √v normalization already supplies width-stable per-component balance, so adding the explicit rule slows convergence without changing the convergent optimum.
