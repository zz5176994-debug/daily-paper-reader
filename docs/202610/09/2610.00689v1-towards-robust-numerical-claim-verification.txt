Abstract 

Large language models (LLMs) are widely used for claim verification, yet remain brittle for numerical reasoning: even small changes in value can sharply degrade accuracy. We show that this brittleness persists in frontier LLMs, but can be mitigated through adver-sarial fine-tuning on numerically perturbed ex-amples. Using parameter-efficient fine-tuning, small Qwen3 models (0.6B–8B) reach 98.7% accuracy on label-flipping perturbations, out-performing larger zero-shot models and fron-tier systems (GPT-5.4 Pro (74.0%) and Gemini 2.5 Flash (73.9%)). The gains generalise to unseen perturbation types, indicating robust nu-merical decision boundaries rather than mem-orised edits. Robustness also transfers with-out target-domain data, significantly improving cross-lingual performance in Spanish. We fur-ther show that the same fine-tuning recipe con-fers robustness to evidence-side perturbations, using the VitaminC dataset. 

1 Introduction 

Claim verification is an important societal task, and LLMs are a popular choice. Claims that contain numerical values such as statistics, counts, percent-ages, and dates are especially challenging to verify, even for humans (Aly et al., 2021) and for frontier large language models (V et al., 2024; Aarnes and Setty, 2025; Akhtar et al., 2023), as they require careful interpretation of context (time spans, units, denominators) and cross-checking against reliable sources. Figure 1 illustrates the problem: a simple surface rewrite that changes “3.5%” to “six percent” flips the correct label, yet a zero-shot LLM misses it. Despite strong performance on arithmetic or structured quantitative tasks (Fang et al., 2025; Schwartz et al., 2024), LLM robustness remains un-even in realistic verification settings. when the numerical values in a claim are slightly perturbed, revealing brittleness and sensitivity that can undermine trust in their outputs (Akhtar et al., 2023; Aarnes and Setty, 2025; Yang et al., 2025). This brittleness is particularly problem-atic in evidence-grounded verification, where a model must reconcile a claim’s surface form (e.g., a written-out number) with the evidence’s surface form (e.g., a digit), and anchoring on lexical over-lap rather than semantic equivalence is an easy failure mode. This gap highlights that strong per-formance on benchmark-style numerical reasoning does not automatically translate into robust, real-world verification of numerical claims. In this paper, we investigate whether adver-sarial fine-tuning (training on numerically per-turbed claims) can improve robustness in numer-ical claim verification. We apply six perturba-tion categories (numeration, approximation, range, negation, masking, and random replacement) to the QuanTemp dataset (V et al., 2024), fine-tune Qwen3 models from 0.6B to 8B parameters us-ing QLoRA and soft prompt tuning, and evaluate cross-dataset generalisation to English and Span-ish corpora unseen during training, addressing four arXiv:2610.00689v1 [cs.CL] 30 Sep 2026 research questions: 

RQ1 : Does adversarial fine-tuning improve ro-bustness to label-flipping perturbations over clean-data fine-tuning? 

RQ2 : Does training on a perturbation subset gen-eralise, and how does training composition affect robustness? 

RQ3 : How do model scale and adaptation method (QLoRA vs. soft prompts) interact with adversarial fine-tuning? 

RQ4 : Does adversarial fine-tuning generalise across domains and languages? 

Our experiments show that adversarial fine-tuning is highly effective: a model trained on clean data alone collapses to under 13% accuracy on label-flipping perturbations, while QLoRA fine-tuning on all six perturbation types restores accu-racy to 96–100%. Training composition matters more than variety; fine-tuned small models (0.6B) surpass zero-shot 32B models on the hardest pertur-bations. Crucially, adversarially fine-tuned Qwen3-8B models also substantially outperform frontier closed-source models (GPT-5.4 Pro, Gemini 2.5 Flash) – for instance, achieving 97.7% vs. 74.0% micro-average accuracy on label-flipping perturba-tions. Cross-dataset generalisation is practically robust: fine-tuned models outperform the zero-shot baseline on Spanish (97.5% vs. 96.4%), whereas on AVeriTeC the accuracy difference is not significant and the gain is in coverage (the baseline returns no parseable verdict on 37.5% of instances). Detailed answers to each RQ are in Section 5. Our main contributions are: (i) a systematic study of LLM brittleness to numerical claim per-turbations and its recovery by fine-tuning on adver-sarial (counterfactually constructed) examples; (ii) 

ablations showing which perturbation types are es-sential (Masked , Numeration ) and which are redun-dant ( Approximation , Random ); (iii) the counterin-tuitive finding that unperturbed training examples, rather than the perturbations themselves, shift the decision boundary and reduce label-preserving ac-curacy; (iv) evidence that adversarially fine-tuned small models (0.6B–8B) substantially outperform zero-shot 32B and frontier closed-source models (GPT-5.4 Pro, Gemini 2.5 Flash) on label-flipping perturbations; and (v) the first cross-lingual evalua-tion showing numerical robustness transfers from English to Spanish without target-language training data. 

2 Related work 

Benchmarks such as FEVER (Thorne et al., 2018) and methods from retrieval pipelines to program-guided reasoning (Guo et al., 2022; Pan et al., 2023) are popular. Numerical claims are a particularly hard sub-problem (V et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2023); we build on QuanTemp (V et al., 2024), and the perturbation taxonomy of Aarnes and Setty (2025). LLM robustness to numerical perturbations is fragile. While targeted training and better en-codings reduce numerical errors (Wallace et al., 2019; Geva et al., 2020; Schwartz et al., 2024), small value changes still flip predictions across QA (Akhtar et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2024a), claim verification (Aarnes and Setty, 2025; Mamta and Cocarascu, 2025), and mathematical reasoning (Yang et al., 2025). The brittleness is compounded in multilingual set-tings, where surface-form variation across lan-guages (e.g., decimal separators, numeral systems, written-out forms) creates additional attack surface that monolingual robustness studies have not ad-dressed (Alam et al., 2025). Adversarial training addresses this brittleness: worst-case perturbation augmentation (Madry et al., 2018), gradient-based NLP perturbations (Guo et al., 2021), and math-problem adversaries (Zhou et al., 2024b) all improve robustness, as do coun-terfactually revised (Kaushik et al., 2020) and ad-versarially collected (Nie et al., 2020) training sets. We apply this strategy to numerical claim verifica-tion across six perturbation types. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning makes large-model adaptation practical via LoRA (Hu et al., 2022), QLoRA (Dettmers et al., 2023), and soft prompt tuning (Lester et al., 2021). Fine-tuned models consistently outperform larger zero-shot LLMs on fact-checking (Setty, 2024; Tang et al., 2024; Larsson et al., 2026). To the best of our knowledge, there are no prior studies applying ad-versarial fine-tuning for numerical perturbations in claim verification setting.

3.1 Generating Numerical Perturbations 

We use the QuanTemp dataset (V et al., 2024) of real-world numerical claim–evidence pairs, with evidence verdict summaries removed and labels binarised to True /False .

Dataset and Perturbation Types. Numerical values in claims are automatically tagged by a spaCy 1 named entity tagger model. We apply the six perturbation types defined by Aarnes and Setty (2025) to the numerical values (see Ap-pendix A.2 for definitions and examples): Nu-meration (digit ↔word form, e.g. “350” →“three hundred fifty”); Approximation (insert “about”); 

Range (exact value → numerical range); Ran-dom (substitute a random value of equal character length); Negation (invert the value); Masking (re-place the value with “#” symbols of equal character length). For numeration, approximation, and range, we generate both a value-preserving and a value-changing variant, which are label-preserving and label-flipping for True claims, while both remain 

False for False claims; random, negation, and masking produce only False variants. Of note, masking differs from the other types by making a claim false through the omission of its numerical values. To prevent cross-split leakage, perturba-tions are generated within each predefined Quan-Temp split, so perturbed variants of a claim remain in the split of their source. 3.2.1 Training Data Sampling 

We use four training configurations: (1) original only; (2) leave-one-out (all perturbation types ex-cept one); (3) single perturbation type; and (4) all perturbation types. Each is balanced 1:1 between original and perturbed examples; the validation set retains its natural distribution. 

3.3 Evaluation and Fine-tuning 

A mid-sized base model serves both as the primary model for fine-tuning and as the baseline for differ-ent prompting strategies. We also evaluate a larger base model variant and two proprietary models in a zero-shot setting to establish an upper-bound refer-ence point. Lastly, three smaller model variants are fine-tuned with all perturbations and original data for traditional adversarial training (see Section 4 for the specific models used). Full code and data can be accessed through our GitHub repository 3.

3.4 Prompting Strategy 

All models use the same system and instruction prompts for fine-tuning and evaluation. Besides the 

Zero-shot setup, the base model is also evaluated with a Two-shot strategy, including one True and one False claim–evidence pair. These prompts are provided in Appendix B.3. We also tested Numer-ical Counterexample Prompt (NCP), but omit it from the main evaluation due to high parse-failure rates (42–61%; details are found in Appendix C.2).

4 Experimental Setup 

4.1 Model Set and Inference Settings 

We use Qwen3-8B as the primary model, with ad-ditional experiments across Qwen3 0.6B, 1.7B, 4B, and 32B (Unsloth Q4_K_M GGUF quanti-sation (Dettmers et al., 2023)). Models run at 

0.001 temperature, with thinking enabled, and use the prompting strategy described in Section 3. The Qwen3 family is selected because it provides openly available checkpoints from 0.6B to 32B under a consistent pre-training recipe, enabling controlled scale ablations within a single family. Aarnes and Setty (2025) report the same numeri-cal brittleness on LLaMA-family models for the underlying claim verification task, so we expect our qualitative findings to reflect the perturbation taxonomy and verification task rather than Qwen3-specific properties; quantitative effect sizes may differ across families. Results for unquantised mod-els can be found in Appendix H. As closed-source frontier baselines we evaluate MGPT-5.4 (GPT-5.4 Pro via the Azure OpenAI API, version 2024-12-01-preview ) and MGemini-2.5 

(gemini-2.5-flash , knowledge cutoff January 2025), both queried zero-shot with the same prompt template. 

4.2 Parameter-Efficient Adaptation 

Models are trained for 2 epochs with a 1:1 ratio of original to perturbed examples, using AdamW (8-bit) with learning rate 1×10 −4, cosine schedule, and a maximum sequence length of 16,384 tokens. Full hyperparameters are listed in Appendix B.1. For soft prompt tuning we set m = 20 , while keep-ing all other hyperparameters the same as in the QLoRA setting.

5.5 Statistical Significance 

The comparisons so far are single-run point esti-mates. We recompute the cross-dataset results from the stored per-instance predictions with a paired bootstrap (10,000 resamples) and an exact McNe-mar test. Because M Base leaves many instances un-parseable while the fine-tuned models leave none, we report three protocols (Appendix D). On CLEF Spanish the gain is significant under every one: paired over the 9,090 instances both models parse, MAll-Perturbs gains +2.33 pp ( p = 8 .0e −41 ) in ev-ery perturbation direction. Since that split is 95.8% 

False , macro-F1 is more informative: it rises 8.43 pp, almost entirely in the minority True class, whose F1 rises 16.4 pp from a baseline precision of just 42.4% (Table 15). On AVeriTeC the appar-ent parseable-only deficit is not significant once paired ( −1.07 pp, p = 0 .511 ); the real advantage there is coverage, as M Base returns no parseable verdict on 37.5% of instances against zero for MAll-Perturbs . We therefore describe AVeriTeC as a coverage rather than an accuracy gain. 

5.6 Sensitivity to Numerical Distance 

Our taxonomy only coarsely distinguishes a pertur-bation that nudges a value from one that replaces it with an implausible figure. Binning each per-turbed instance by relative numerical distance sep-arates the two (Appendix F). The benefit of ad-versarial fine-tuning is largest exactly on the near-misses: +7.50 pp within 10% relative distance and 

+8.44 pp between 10% and 25%, falling monotoni-cally to +2.91 pp beyond 100%, every bin individ-ually significant. This bears directly on the concern that the models might be recognising perturbation artefacts rather than comparing quantities: an arte-fact detector would improve uniformly, or most where the edit is most conspicuous, whereas the observed profile is the opposite. 

5.7 Evidence-Side Numerical Revisions 

The perturbations studied so far all edit the claim 

while holding the evidence fixed, leaving open whether that robustness is direction-specific. We therefore evaluate on VitaminC (Schuster et al., 2021), which pairs each claim with two snapshots of one Wikipedia sentence, before and after a fac-tual revision: the claim is unaltered natural text, and the quantity deciding the verdict changes in the 

evidence . Restricted to real, genuinely numerical revisions, it yields 11,887 test instances, including 5,227 contrastive pairs differing solely by a num-ber in the evidence (Appendix G.1); we therefore also report pair accuracy , the share of pairs with 

both snapshots correct. 

Claim-side robustness does not transfer. 

MAll-Perturbs reaches only 78.35% accuracy and 54.45% pair accuracy, below the untuned M Base at 90.96% and 84.25% ( −12.60 pp and −29.81 pp, exact McNemar p < 10 −190 ; Table 18). The errors are strongly asymmetric: MAll-Perturbs wrongly confirms a refuting snapshot on 31.5% of pairs but wrongly refutes a supporting one on only 15.0%, whereas M Base is balanced (8.4% and 8.8%). Inspecting the 1,426 pairs where only MAll-Perturbs errs shows a consistent pattern: “$24.9 million” is judged to support “grossed more than $25 million”. The model checks whether the two quantities are approximately equal rather than evaluating the inequality the claim asserts: sufficient on our claim-side perturbations, which move the value away from the evidence, but not on bounded claims. Prompting does not repair it: Structured Chain-of-Thought (SCoT) and NCP are neutral or harmful, and a Threshold Comparison Prompt (TCP) naming the failure directly recovers only about half the deficit (Appendix G.4). 

Training on evidence-side revisions closes it, and the two competencies are complementary. 

Trained with the same recipe on VitaminC itself, MVitaminC reaches 98.05% accuracy and 96.56% pair accuracy, exceeding M Base by +7.09 pp and 

+12.30 pp ( p < 10 −140 ), and wrong confirmations fall from 31.5% to 2.5%. The recipe transfers even though the perturbation direction, the discrepancy’s source and the evidence length all differ; the result-ing model does not. Specialisation is costly in both directions (Table 6): M VitaminC scores only 80.86% on QuanTemp against M All-Perturbs ’s 97.52%, below even M Base . Training on both corpora jointly in-curs no trade-off: M Mixed attains 98.25% accuracy on VitaminC and 98.20% on QuanTemp, and is significantly better than each specialist on that spe-cialist’s own test set (p = 1 .0e −4 and p = 0 .002 ). Claim-side and evidence-side numerical robustness are thus distinct competencies, neither obtainable from the other, but jointly learnable. 

6 Discussion 

RQ1: Does adversarial fine-tuning improve robust-ness to label-flipping perturbations over clean-data fine-tuning? Yes: M Only-Original is near-perfect on value-preserving evaluations yet collapses to 5– 13% across every label-flipping perturbation, an 83–90% drop. Standard fine-tuning thus teaches the model to confirm the original claim’s surface form rather than verify it against evidence: with-out adversarial exposure, it never sees a plausible-looking claim that is numerically inconsistent with the evidence. All QLoRA models in All Perturb Group overcome this, matching or exceeding their 

T→T performance on every T→F perturbation. 

F→F results are largely unaffected: false claims are already in the majority, and there is no truth-confirming prior to override. In T→T, all-perturbs variants lose 2.6–11.0% on Original relative to MOnly-Original , likely because the adversarial objec-tive shifts the decision boundary away from truth-confirming features; Exclusion Group largely miti-gates this, as excluding original data removes the tension.

RQ3: How do model scale and adaptation method interact with adversarial fine-tuning? 

Scale matters, but fine-tuning matters more: M0.6B All-Perturbs beats zero-shot M 32B Base , GPT-5.4 Pro and Gemini 2.5 Flash on T→F by at least 14 pp in micro-average accuracy. Across the all-perturbs series, T→F accuracy is flat at 88% for 0.6B and 1.7B, jumps to 98% at 4B, and saturates at 8B; the jump comes mostly from Numeration , Range , and 

Approximation . Both 4B and 8B outperform every Base Group model by at least 23 pp. QLoRA models in All Perturb Group and Exclu-sion Group consistently outperform soft-prompt tuning. Soft prompts can shift the output dis-tribution but do not update the weight-level rep-resentations needed to detect numerical incon-sistencies; QLoRA modifies attention and feed-forward projections directly. Soft-prompt tuning still beats M Only-Original on T→F, so adversarial ex-posure helps, but it stays below the zero-shot base-line, suggesting the prepended vectors introduce conflicting biases rather than targeted numerical sensitivity. 

RQ4: Does adversarial fine-tuning generalise across domains and languages? Across languages, yes; on AVeriTeC, the gain is in coverage rather than accuracy (Sections 5.4–5.5). On CLEF 2025 Spanish the evidence is unstructured article text, as in QuanTemp, so the shift is primarily lexical and the structural signal transfers across languages. On AVeriTeC the baseline retains a narrow edge on label-flipping, where all examples are False: its tendency to predict False inflates the score, the artefact already seen in QuanTemp T→F.

7 Conclusion 

This paper is a diagnostic study of LLM robust-ness to numerical claim perturbations. Standard fine-tuning teaches the model to confirm a claim’s surface form rather than verify it against evidence, and accuracy on label-flipping inputs collapses to single digits. Adversarial fine-tuning on counter-factual examples recovers near-perfect accuracy, with small QLoRA-tuned models (0.6B–8B) sur-passing zero-shot 32B and frontier closed-source baselines on the hardest perturbations. Composi-tion matters more than variety: Masked and Nu-meration are essential, Approximation and Random 

redundant. Robustness also transfers across lan-guages and domains without target-language data, suggesting a generalised numerical decision bound-ary rather than memorised edits. Limitations 

Data and perturbation scope. Our perturbation taxonomy rewrites numerical expressions in the claim while leaving evidence unchanged. Sec-tion 5.7 reaches the evidence side through Vitam-inC’s human-authored revisions, but our generator does not produce them, and jointly perturbing claim and evidence remains an open direction. The six types focus on scalar quantities as digits or written-out numbers, so robustness to unit conversions, date arithmetic, compound quantities, or numbers in tables is not guaranteed. Training and primary evaluation use QuanTemp (English fact-checking); cross-dataset experiments add Spanish, AVeriTeC and VitaminC but remain in English and Spanish and, apart from VitaminC’s single-sentence evi-dence, within the fact-checking domain, so broader language coverage and generalisation to scientific, financial, or tabular settings both require further evaluation. 

Model and evaluation setup. Experiments use only the Qwen3 family (0.6B–32B) with QLoRA and soft prompt tuning. Aarnes and Setty (2025) document the same numerical brittleness on LLaMA-family models, so we expect the qual-itative findings to hold across LLaMA, Mistral, Gemma, and comparable open families, though confirming this directly is left for future work; quantitative effects (the scale at which diminish-ing returns set in, the size of the QLoRA-vs.-soft-prompt gap) may shift across families and PEFT methods. The soft-prompt baseline reuses QLoRA hyperparameters, making it a matched-budget rather than optimally tuned comparison. All experiments assume gold evidence, isolating verifi-cation from retrieval; whether the robustness gains survive noisy or incomplete retrieval is left for fu-ture work. 

Interpretability. We lack a mechanistic account of why adversarial fine-tuning works. It is unclear whether models learn to attend more carefully to numerical tokens, develop surface-form-invariant representations, or rely on other signals; attribution, probing, and activation-patching analyses would be needed to explain the gains and anticipate failure modes — a direction we intend to pursue. 

Ethical Considerations 

This work studies the robustness of LLMs used for numerical claim verification. We identify spe-cific weaknesses that existing models exhibit when claims contain perturbed numerical expressions, and we show that adversarial fine-tuning substan-tially reduces these vulnerabilities. We do not endorse any specific model or configuration for deployment in real-world fact-checking pipelines; fact-checking is a nuanced task where model out-puts should be treated as decision-support tools rather than authoritative verdicts, and human over-sight remains essential. The perturbation techniques studied could, in principle, be used to craft adversarial claims that evade automated fact-checkers. We consider this a reason to publish rather than withhold the find-ings: understanding and documenting these failure modes is a prerequisite for building more resilient systems, and the perturbation types we study are straightforward enough that they are already avail-able to any motivated adversary. All data used in this work is sourced from publicly available fact-checking corpora, and no personal or sensitive in-formation is involved.

G VitaminC: Evidence-Side Revisions 

G.1 Subset Construction and Splits 

VitaminC contains 488,904 claim–evidence pairs, of which 163,180 (33.4%) are annotator-written synthetic revisions rather than real Wikipedia ed-its. We retain only real revisions, and only the 

SUPPORTS /REFUTES labels that map onto our binary protocol, discarding NOT ENOUGH INFO . We further require that the revision is genuinely numerical, i.e. that the multiset of numeric tokens differs between the two evidence snapshots, and that the claim car-ries a quantitative entity ( CARDINAL , MONEY , PER - 

> CENT

or QUANTITY ), applying the same spaCy tagging used to build our QuanTemp splits. Ap-proximately 47% of VitaminC’s real revisions alter a numeric token by this measure, consistent with the 48% quantitative rate reported from manual annotation (Schuster et al., 2021), which is what makes the corpus a dense source of numerical con-trasts. 

Splits. We build the subset with the filters above applied to each official VitaminC split. The test split yields 11,887 instances over 3,701 revisions (6,650 True , 5,237 False ). Because the same claim recurs against both snapshots, these instances include 5,227 contrastive pairs : two evaluations of one claim whose gold labels differ solely because a number in the evidence changed. The dev split yields 16,434 instances over 5,143 revisions. The splits are disjoint not only by revision and claim text but by Wikipedia page, since VitaminC is par-titioned at the page level, so no article contributes to more than one split. We use dev only for prompt selection, and never for model selection or report-ing. 

Evidence length. VitaminC evidence is a sin-gle revised sentence, with a median of 26 words (mean 28). For comparison, our QuanTemp splits have a median of 535 words (Appendix A.1) and AVeriTeC a mean of 114 words (Appendix A.3). The corpus therefore isolates numerical compari-son from long-context retrieval, and it constitutes a distribution shift for models trained on Quan-Temp’s much longer passages – a shift that works 

against the fine-tuned models, making their deficit in Table 18 conservative rather than inflated. 

G.2 Pair Accuracy 

Instance accuracy understates how hard this setting is, because a model that ignores the quantity and keys off claim–evidence surface overlap can still score well on the supporting snapshot of every pair. We therefore report pair accuracy : the share of con-trastive pairs for which both snapshots are labelled correctly. This is the direct analogue of our label-flipping evaluation, and we additionally decompose its errors into the two directions – wrongly refuting a supporting snapshot, and wrongly confirming a refuting one – because their asymmetry identifies the failure mode rather than merely its size. 

G.3 Training Details Training sample. From the training split we draw 16,000 instances by sampling whole revi-sions rather than instances, so that a contrastive pair is never divided; revisions carrying both la-bels are preferred, and in the resulting sample all 4,044 revisions are contrastive and the label dis-tribution is balanced (7,997 True / 8,003 False ). Validation is a further 2,001 instances over 508 re-visions, drawn from the training split and verified case-disjoint from it, which leaves the official dev split untouched. The sample is sized to be com-parable to the 17,258 QuanTemp training records rather than to exhaust the 77,568 available Vitam-inC instances. 

Deduplication. Our pipeline deduplicates train-ing samples by claim text, which is correct for QuanTemp but destructive here: because VitaminC pairs one claim with two evidence documents, key-ing on text alone discards one snapshot of every pair, reducing the 16,000-instance sample to 7,987 and removing precisely the contrast the corpus provides. We therefore key deduplication on the (claim text, gold label) pair, which retains 15,952 of 16,000 VitaminC samples (99.7%) while chang-ing the QuanTemp training set by only 234 samples (≈1%). Two samples sharing a claim but carrying opposite gold labels are contrastive, not duplicate. 

Optimisation. MVitaminC and M Mixed are trained from the base model with the hyperparameters of Appendix B.1 (rank 32, batch size 5, no gradient accumulation, two epochs). M VitaminC uses a maxi-mum sequence length of 2,048, since VitaminC’s longest prompt is 648 tokens. G.4 Additional Results Prompting does not close the gap. Because the deficit looks like a decision rule rather than missing knowledge, we ask whether prompting can recover it. Neither of our existing strategies helps: SCoT, which explicitly instructs the model to extract, nor-malise and compare quantities step by step, lowers 

pair accuracy to 46.15% and raises wrong confirma-tions to 44.2%, and NCP is approximately neutral at 53.74%. We additionally designed a Threshold Comparison Prompt (TCP) that names the failure directly, stating that a claim asserting a bound holds only if the evidence value satisfies that compari-son and not merely if the two numbers are close, illustrated with invented figures so that no test in-stance enters the prompt. Selected on the VitaminC dev split ( +5.09 pp accuracy, CI [+4 .56 , +5 .60] ;

+11.08 pp pair accuracy, CI [+9 .99 , +12 .17] ), it transfers to the test split with +6.81 pp accuracy (CI [+6 .18 , +7 .42] ) and +14.71 pp pair accuracy (CI [+13 .45 , +15 .97] ), both p < 10 −100 . Even so, TCP recovers only about half the deficit and re-mains 15.09 pp below M Base in pair accuracy. No-tably, under every prompt the fine-tuned model emits a bare verdict of roughly 15 characters and never produces the reasoning SCoT demands: ad-versarial fine-tuning appears to have fixed the out-put format at the cost of instruction-following, which is why prompts can shift its verdicts but cannot restore deliberation.

Validity checks. Three checks guard the compar-ison. First, we re-ran M All-Perturbs on the QuanTemp test split through the same code path used for all Vi-taminC evaluations and reproduced the published figure to within three instances of 6,175 (97.47% against 97.52%), which excludes a pipeline regres-sion as the explanation for its VitaminC result. Sec-ond, the VitaminC splits share no revision, no claim text and no Wikipedia page, so the evaluation is clean by construction and the TCP prompt was se-lected on dev rather than test. Third, all models are evaluated at the same 4-bit quantisation and with parse failures scored as errors, which is con-servative for M Base : it is the only model with a non-trivial failure rate (704 of 11,887, 5.92%), and on parseable responses alone it reaches 96.68%. We note as a scope condition that M Base produces several hundred tokens of free-form reasoning per instance, whereas the fine-tuned models emit a single verdict, so part of the baseline’s strength on this shorter-evidence corpus reflects inference-time computation that the fine-tuned models do not spend. 

H Unquantised Model Results 

Tables 19, 20 and 21 report the same evaluation as Section 5 but use unquantised 16-bit checkpoints (BF16, or FP16 on GPUs without BF16 support) rather than the q4_k_m quantisation used in the main paper. Models are organised into the same four groups as in the main results: Base Group (baselines and clean fine-tune), All Perturb Group (all-perturbation training), Single Perturb (single-type training), and Exclusion Group (leave-one-out training). The qualitative patterns mirror the quantised results closely across all three evaluation directions: clean fine-tuning collapses on label-flipping perturbations, all-perturbs training restores near-perfect accuracy, single-type training fails to generalise across perturbation types, and the same perturbation types remain essential ( Masked , Nu-meration ) or redundant ( Approximation , Random ). Per-cell numbers differ by at most a few percentage points in either direction, but the relative ordering of model groups and the qualitative conclusions of the main paper hold. 

H.1 True → False 

Base Group collapses on Negative and Masked ,the absolute outlier being MOnly-Original with 94.70% on originals but only 9.42% micro-average (−85.28 pp) and 79–87% drops across every per-turbed category – closely tracking the collapse seen in the quantised setting (Table 2). All Perturb Group is uniformly more robust, with M All-Perturbs 

showing the lowest accuracy fluctuations in its group. Single Perturb reaches perfect accuracy on its fine-tuned perturbation type but collapses elsewhere, again matching the single-type gener-alisation pattern reported in the main results. Ex-clusion Group achieves the best overall accuracies: MExcl-Masked drops −75.85% on its held-out per-turbation type (close to the −80.13% reported for the quantised counterpart), while M Excl-Approx and MExcl-Range improve on their held-out type, confirm-ing that the Approximation and Range signals are recoverable from compositional cues in the remain-ing training mix. 

H.2 True → True 

Table 20 presents the results for the T→T evalua-tion. In sharp contrast to T→F, Base Group dom-inates: M Base reaches a micro-average of 96.69%, while All Perturb Group trails in the 83–91% range. This is the same direction observed in the quan-tised results (Table 4), where all-perturbs training loses ground on label-preserving inputs because the adversarial objective shifts the decision boundary away from truth-confirming features. Single Per-turb and Exclusion Group instabilities concentrate on the Range perturbation type, again consistent with the quantised findings. 

H.3 False → False 

In the F→F evaluation (Table 21), most models achieve robust accuracies of 96–99%, indicating that fine-tuning largely resolves the small blind spots observed in Base Group– identical to the pattern in the quantised setting (Table 3). The leave-one-out models show little degradation on their held-out perturbation types, with M Excl-Approx and MExcl-Range both scoring near saturation. 

Summary. Across all three evaluation directions, the unquantised results reproduce the qualitative findings of the q4_k_m experiments: the same model groups, perturbation types, and headline conclusion, that adversarial fine-tuning mitigates label-flipping brittleness at a small cost to label-preserving accuracy, hold independently of quanti-sation.
