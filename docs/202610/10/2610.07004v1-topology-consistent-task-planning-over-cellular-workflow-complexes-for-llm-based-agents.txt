ABSTRACT
Task planning for LLM agents requires workflows that satisfy both user intent and
complex sub-task dependencies. While existing planners work well for sequen-
tial or directed acyclic graph (DAG)-like structures, they struggle with workflow
patterns such as verification-correction loops, convergent branch merging, and
reusable intermediate states that arise naturally in real-world tool orchestration.
We present TopoPlanner, a topology-consistent planning framework that lifts tool
dependency graphs into cellular workflow complexes and uses them as topology-
aware context for LLM tool planning. TopoPlanner retrieves a request-relevant
closed subcomplex through cosheaf-consistent cellular retrieval, performs multi-
dimensional structural reasoning over the retrieved topology, and interfaces the
resulting cellular representation with the planner LLM for tool-sequence generation.
Experiments on four tool-planning benchmarks with topology-guided loop, merge,
and loop-merge workflows show consistent improvements over prompt-based and
graph-enhanced baselines across different local LLM backbones.

INTRODUCTION
Large language model (LLM) agents are becoming a practical interface for tool use, autonomous
exploration, and complex decision making (Wang et al. (2024a); Bubeck et al. (2023); Jiang et al.
(2025)). Their effectiveness depends critically on task planning: given a natural-language request,
the agent must decompose the request into executable sub-tasks, organize their dependencies, and
produce a workflow that can be grounded into tools or actions (Schick et al. (2024); Shen et al.
(2024a;b)). In realistic agentic settings, this workflow must capture not only individual sub-tasks, but
also the dependency structure that makes them executable as a coherent process.
This exposes a structural gap in existing planners. Most LLM planning methods are strongest when
the workflow is sequential or approximately directed acyclic graph (DAG)-like, where pairwise task
adjacency already captures most of the required structure (Wei et al. (2022); Yao et al. (2024); Shinn
et al. (2024)). As illustrated in Fig. 1, real-world tool orchestration is often more complex: it may
involve verification-correction loops, convergent branch merging, and reusable intermediate states.
Although these structures can be encoded by edges, their joint regional organization remains implicit
in pairwise message passing and pooled graph summaries. In these settings, the key dependency
patterns arise from how multiple relations jointly form loop, merge, and reuse structures, rather than
from isolated edges. This motivates topology-consistent task planning beyond pairwise adjacency.

Recent graph-enhanced approaches inject structural bias into agent planning by coupling LLMs with
graphs or GNNs (Tang et al. (2024); Chai et al. (2023); Wu et al. (2024); Chen et al. (2025; 2026)).
GTool (Chen et al. (2026)) studies graph-enhanced tool planning by encoding tool-dependency graphs
with a conventional node-edge GNN and conditioning the planner on one pooled graph token. These
methods improve over pure prompting, but they still reason mainly over node-edge structure and
therefore leave cyclic or convergent workflow regions implicit. This is restrictive when the critical
structure of a workflow lies in loop, merge, and reuse patterns rather than in isolated pairwise relations.
Meanwhile, recent evidence suggests that cyclic structure is not incidental: stronger reasoning models
tend to exhibit richer loop patterns in their reasoning graphs (Minegishi et al. (2025)).
This perspective motivates a unified algebraic-topological formulation of workflow structures. Starting
from a task workflow graph, we promote independent cycles induced by non-tree edges into explicit
cycle-level cells, resulting in a cellular workflow complex. In this complex, boundary relations
organize sub-tasks, dependencies, and non-linear workflow regions within one multi-dimensional
structure, making region-level dependencies explicit rather than implicit in pairwise paths.
Building on this formulation, we propose TopoPlanner, a topology-consistent task planning frame-
work for LLM agents. TopoPlanner lifts the tool dependency graph into a cellular workflow complex,
retrieves a request-relevant closed subcomplex through cosheaf-consistent cellular retrieval, performs
multi-dimensional structural reasoning over the retrieved topology, and interfaces the resulting cel-
lular representation with the planner LLM for tool-sequence generation. To evaluate this setting,
we extend four tool-planning datasets, including the three TaskBench domains (Shen et al. (2024b))
and ToolBench (Chen et al. (2026)), with topology-guided loop, merge, and loop-merge cases that
emphasize non-linear workflow structure. Experiments show that TopoPlanner consistently improves
planning quality over prior baselines. Our contributions in this work are summarized as follows:
• We formulate loop, merge, and reuse dependencies as explicit regions in cellular workflow
complexes, providing a unified multi-dimensional representation of non-linear workflows.
• We introduce TopoPlanner, which lifts a workflow graph into a cellular complex used for cosheaf-
consistent retrieval, multi-dimensional reasoning, and topology-guided planning.
• We construct topology-guided extensions of four benchmarks with loop, merge, and loop-merge
cases, and show consistent improvements over the compared baselines across different local LLM
backbones.

PRELIMINARIES
Definition 1. (Cell Complex). Hansen & Ghrist (2019) A regular cell complex is a topological space
X decomposed into a collection of disjoint subspaces {Xσ}σ∈PX, referred to as cells, satisfying the
following conditions:
1. For each x ∈X, there exists an open neighborhood intersecting only finitely many cells.
2. For any pair of cells Xσ, Xτ, the intersection Xτ ∩Xσ is nonempty if and only if Xτ ⊆Xσ,
where Xσ denotes the topological closure of Xσ.
3. Each cell Xσ is homeomorphic to an open ball in Rn for some non-negative integer n.
4. (Regularity) The closure Xσ of every cell is homeomorphic to a closed ball in Rnσ, with the
interior mapped homeomorphically onto Xσ itself.
Definition 2. Given a directed workflow graph G = (V, E, T), let Gu denote its underlying undirected
graph, with the original edge directions retained as attributes. For a connected Gu, a chosen spanning

0-cells and 1-cells correspond to V and E, and its 2-cells are attached along the fundamental cycles

while cycle-level workflow regions are made explicit.
Definition 3. (Topology-Consistent Task Planning). Let G = (V, E, T) be a text-attributed task
graph, and let X = fT (G) be its lifted regular cell complex under a cellular lifting map fT

THE PROPOSED MODEL
In this section, we present TopoPlanner as a topology-consistent task planning framework over
cellular workflow complexes, organized into four modules: Cellular Workflow Lifting, Cosheaf-
Consistent Cellular Retrieval, Multi-Dimensional Structural Reasoning, and Topology-Guided LLM
Tool Planning. Figure 2 summarizes the overall framework. The retrieved cellular representation is
interfaced with the planner LLM through a soft graph token.
3.1
CELLULAR WORKFLOW LIFTING

DATASET AND DATA EXTENSION
4.1
DATASET
The proposed framework is evaluated on four tool-planning datasets: HuggingFace, Multimedia, and
Daily Life from TaskBench (Shen et al. (2024b)), plus ToolBench (Chen et al. (2026)); their statistics
are summarized in Table 1. HuggingFace focuses on AI model tasks in NLP and computer vision,
Multimedia covers tasks such as downloading and format conversion, Daily Life contains everyday
service operations such as shopping and route navigation, and ToolBench features a substantially
larger and more diverse API inventory. The original queries are mainly categorized as Single, Chain,
and DAG. To evaluate topology-aware planning, we further introduce a Cycle category covering
loop, merge, and loop-merge workflows. We use Cycle as an umbrella category for workflow motifs
that induce a closed region in the lifted cellular representation: directed feedback loops, convergent
merges formed by multiple dependency paths, and loop-merge compositions. A merge is represented
as a cycle-level cell because its multiple dependency paths bound a closed region; this structural
notion does not imply repeated execution. For motifs containing a directed loop, the loop denotes
bounded revision, reuse, or finite transformation rather than unbounded execution; runtime repetition
and termination are governed by the downstream executor or stopping policy.
4.2
AGENTIC TOPOLOGY-GUIDED DATA EXTENSION
To evaluate topology-aware task planning, we extend the datasets with queries whose task graphs
contain higher-dimensional workflow structures, as illustrated in Figure 1. Rather than letting a lan-
guage model freely invent samples, we first fix a target topology and then generate a natural-language
request under that structural constraint. This fixes the dependency structure before generation while
allowing variation in request wording.
The extension uses a code-executing construction workflow inspired by code-action agents (Wang
et al. (2024b)): a human designer specifies the structural constraints, executable scripts instantiate
candidate workflows, and an LLM generates each request conditioned on the selected tools and
fixed topology. Each accepted workflow is represented by task nodes, task links, task steps, and
its generated user request. The resulting samples are validated, merged into the original dataset
format, and split with grouped train-test partitioning to reduce structural leakage. All baselines and
TopoPlanner are evaluated on this same extended dataset and split; implementation and validation
details are provided in Appendix D, especially Sections D.1 and D.3. Appendix G.1 further reports a
motif-leakage audit, held-out-topology evaluation, and results on original non-augmented workflows.

Baselines. Direct: Queries a pre-trained LLM directly to generate task plans without using any graph
structure; TalkGraph (Fatemi et al. (2024)): Encodes graph-structured data as text for black-box
LLMs, enabling reasoning over nodes and edges through prompt engineering; GNN4TaskPlan (Wu
et al. (2024)): Leverages LLMs to interpret user requests and employs GraphSAGE with ranking-loss
training to retrieve relevant sub-tasks; Plan-over-Graph (Zhang et al. (2025)): Leverages an LLM
to extract task graphs and applies topological sorting over LLM-predicted task dependencies to
generate execution plans; USPilot (Chen et al. (2025)): Combines a large language model with an
LLM-enhanced GNN, using dynamic routing and adapters for task planning; GTool (Chen et al.
(2026)): A strong graph-enhanced tool-planning baseline that encodes the tool-dependency graph
with a conventional node-edge GNN, pools the graph into a single soft graph token for the planner
LLM, and adds an auxiliary edge-existence objective.
Implementation Details. All local-model training and evaluation experiments are conducted on four
NVIDIA H800-80G GPUs. We use e5-large-v2 (Wang et al. (2022)) to initialize tool, request,
and cellular features. TopoPlanner is configured with a hidden dimension of 1024. In the main local-
LLM setting, the planner backbone is frozen and only the cellular modules and graph-token interface
are trained with AdamW (Kingma & Ba (2014)) (lr = 1 × 10−3, weight decay 0.05, batch size 1)
for at most 12 epochs with early stopping (patience 2). For the 7B LoRA continuation experiments,
we use a smaller learning rate of 1 × 10−5. The maximum input and generation lengths are 1024
and 128, respectively. We sweep the coefficient of LCI by varying λCI ∈{0, 0.005, 0.01, 0.05, 0.1}
across the three TaskBench domains.

the compared prompting and graph-enhanced baselines across the evaluated datasets and local LLM

baseline are often more pronounced in l-F1 and ACC, which require recovering dependencies and

graph representation. TopoPlanner instead makes loop, merge, and loop-merge regions explicit as
2-cells, retrieves their request-relevant boundary-closed subcomplex, and propagates information
across dimensions before conditioning the planner. Its additional gains over GTool are therefore
consistent with the value of preserving the regional structures highlighted in Figure 1, rather than
flattening them into pairwise relations. The advantage persists across the 7B and 13B Vicuna and
CodeLlama families, suggesting that this structural interface complements backbone capacity.

RELATED WORK
Task planning in LLM-based agents aims to decompose natural-language requests into executable
sub-tasks. Most existing methods are prompt-based, including task decomposition (Wei et al. (2022)),
multi-plan search (Yao et al. (2024); Besta et al. (2024)), symbolic planning (Liu et al. (2023)), and
reflection-based refinement (Shinn et al. (2024)). These approaches have broadened the planning
ability of LLMs, but they usually assume sequential, tree-like, or DAG-like workflows, which makes
it difficult to capture richer dependency patterns such as loops, merges, and feedback structures.
Another line of work studies graph reasoning with LLMs. GPT4Graph and NLGraph evaluate how
pretrained LLMs process graph problems in natural language (Guo et al. (2023); Wang et al. (2023)),
while TalkLikeAGraph and Plan-over-Graph encode graphs as text and rely on the LLM to reason
over dependencies or derive execution orders (Fatemi et al. (2024); Zhang et al. (2025)). These results
suggest that LLMs can exploit graph-structured context, but text-only reasoning still lacks explicit
structural bias on more complex topological relations.
To address this limitation, several hybrid LLM+GNN methods inject graph representations into LLMs.
GraphGPT, GraphLLM, and GraphToken incorporate learned graph embeddings or graph tokens
into the language-model input on graph-centric tasks (Tang et al. (2024); Chai et al. (2023); Perozzi
et al. (2024)). In tool planning, GNN4TaskPlan (Wu et al. (2024)) uses an LLM to decompose user
goals and a GNN matcher to retrieve relevant sub-tasks, while USPilot (Chen et al. (2025)) combines
an LLM with an LLM-enhanced GNN, dynamic routing, and adapters. GTool (Chen et al. (2026))
encodes the tool-dependency graph with a node-edge GNN, pools it into a single graph token for the
planner LLM, and adds an auxiliary edge-existence objective. Despite their strength, these methods
still reason mainly over node-edge structure and do not explicitly elevate higher-order workflow
regions into the planning representation. Appendix I reviews topological deep learning.

CONCLUSION
In this work, we present TopoPlanner, a topology-consistent task planning framework that makes
loop, merge, and loop-merge regions explicit by lifting tool dependency graphs into cellular workflow
complexes. The lifted structure supports request-specific retrieval, multi-dimensional reasoning, and
topology-guided tool-sequence generation. We also construct topology-guided extensions of four
tool-planning benchmarks. Across the evaluated datasets and local LLM backbones, TopoPlanner
consistently outperforms the compared baselines; the ablations and structure-specific diagnostics
further support explicit regional modeling as an effective inductive bias within the evaluated scope.

PROOFS
A.1
PROOFS REGARDING PROPOSITION 1
Proof. Contractibility of the spanning tree. For the connected underlying undirected graph Gu,
a spanning tree T is connected and acyclic, hence contractible. A contractible subspace can be
continuously shrunk to a point.
Collapsing a contractible subspace. Consider the quotient map γ : Gu →Gu/T that identifies
all points of T with a single vertex v0. Because T is a contractible subcomplex of the graph, the
quotient map is a homotopy equivalence. Hence there exists a homotopy inverse s : Gu/T →Gu
such that s ◦γ ≃idGu and γ ◦s ≃idGu/T . Thus, Gu ≃Gu/T .
Induced isomorphism on first homology. Homotopy-equivalent spaces have isomorphic homology
groups. Hence the induced map γ∗: H1(Gu; Z) →H1(Gu/T ; Z) is an isomorphism.
Intuition in graph terms. The spanning tree T contains no cycles, so collapsing it does not remove
or merge any cycles in Gu. Each fundamental cycle in Gu (formed by a non-tree edge and the unique
tree path connecting its endpoints) becomes a loop based at the collapsed tree vertex. Thus, collapsing
T preserves the independent cycles measured by H1(Gu).
A.2
PROOF OF THE FUNDAMENTAL-CYCLE BASIS CLAIM
Proof. Let Gu = (V, E) be a finite connected undirected graph underlying a directed workflow
graph, and let T ⊂Gu be a spanning tree. Edge directions remain attributes for planning but do not
alter the cycle-space argument.
Existence and uniqueness of fundamental cycles. For each non-tree edge e = (u, v) ∈E \T , there
exists a unique simple path PT (u, v) in T connecting u and v, by acyclicity of T . Concatenating e
with PT (u, v) defines a unique simple cycle
Ce = e ∪PT (u, v) ⊂G.
Under the quotient map γ : Gu →Gu/T , the tree path PT (u, v) is mapped to that point, so Ce
becomes a nontrivial loop in Gu/T .
Spanning and independence in homology. The cyclomatic number of Gu is β1(Gu) = |E|−|V |+1,
which equals the number of non-tree edges. Thus there are exactly β1(G) fundamental cycles.
Any cycle in Gu is a linear combination of these fundamental cycles: whenever traversal encounters
a non-tree edge e, the corresponding Ce eliminates the associated tree segment.
These cycles are independent in H1(Gu): under γ, each fundamental cycle maps to a distinct loop in
Gu/T , and loops around different edges of a wedge of circles are homologically independent.
Hence the set
{[Ce] | e ∈E \ T }
forms a basis of H1(Gu) under the stated connected-graph assumption.
Topological summary. Different choices of spanning tree yield different fundamental-cycle edge
sets, but their homology classes always span H1(Gu). These loops therefore capture all independent
cycle-space relations of the underlying graph, while the retained edge directions preserve the workflow
semantics used by the planner.

THEORETICAL ROLE OF THE CELLULAR STRUCTURE
The homotopy and cycle-basis results above justify the lifting construction. We next clarify what
the lifted cells contribute to representation learning and retrieval. These statements characterize the
structural inductive bias of TopoPlanner; boundary closure alone does not certify runtime correctness.
Proposition 2 (Fixed-depth regional communication). Let R be a workflow region containing two
boundary elements whose graph distance exceeds L in the pairwise 1-skeleton. In an L-layer local
message-passing network, the representation of either element cannot depend on the other. If R is

represented by a shared 2-cell, every boundary element is incident to that cell, and information can be
exchanged across the region through a boundary-to-coface update followed by a coface-to-boundary
update, regardless of boundary length.
Proof. Local pairwise message passing expands a receptive field by at most one graph hop per
layer. Hence two elements at distance greater than L cannot influence one another after L layers.
After lifting R to a 2-cell, each boundary element is connected to the shared coface through an
incidence relation. A boundary-to-coface update followed by a coface-to-boundary update therefore
communicates across the region in constant depth. This is a representational shortcut rather than a
correctness guarantee; a suitably constructed hypergraph can provide a related shortcut, while the
cellular representation additionally retains graded boundary relations.

D
DATA CONSTRUCTION AND PROMPT DETAILS
To ensure reproducibility and facilitate future research, we describe the topology-guided data con-
struction workflow and provide the prompt template used by TopoPlanner during inference. The
data extension is not produced by a one-shot generation prompt. Instead, it is generated by exe-
cutable scripts developed through an agentic code-writing workflow, following the idea of code-action
agents (Wang et al. (2024b)) and agentic data construction (Mitra et al. (2024)).
D.1
AGENTIC TOPOLOGY-GUIDED CONSTRUCTION WORKFLOW
Given human-specified workflow-topology requirements, Codex is used as a code-executing agent
to implement and iterate the data construction pipeline. Algorithm 2 summarizes the process: it
mines valid motifs from the original tool graph, instantiates samples, generates requests under fixed
topology constraints, validates them, and creates leakage-controlled splits.
Structure constraints.
The construction code treats the original tool graph as the source of
structural validity. For each dataset, it loads the tool inventory and directed dependency links,
builds adjacency lists and an edge lookup table, and samples candidate motifs only from this graph.
We use three concrete paradigms: directed loop, such as A →B →C →A; merge, where two
dependency paths converge to a shared downstream tool, such as A →C and A →B →C; and
loop-merge, where a merge structure is further connected by a closing path back to an earlier tool. A
candidate is accepted only if all of its workflow dependencies are present in the original edge set, all
task nodes belong to the tool inventory, and the resulting task graph satisfies the intended topological
pattern. Thus, topology is fixed before request generation.
16


Codex workflow instructions.
Codex is not prompted to directly invent final samples. Instead,
it receives module-level implementation instructions and iteratively edits executable construction
programs. The main instructions used in the workflow are summarized below:
• Motif mining: “Sample valid directed-loop, merge, and loop-merge motifs from each tool graph.”
• Sample construction: “For each valid motif, construct task nodes, task links, and task steps in
the original dataset schema. Record motif statistics for later inspection.”
• Request generation: “Generate a natural request from the fixed, hidden tool topology.”
• Validation and merging: “Validate tools, edges, node-link consistency, identifiers, and motifs;
repair or reject errors before merging leakage-controlled splits.”

D.2
HUMAN EVALUATION PROTOCOL
We further conduct a human evaluation to assess the quality of the topology-guided data extension.
Following the evaluation dimensions used in TaskBench (Shen et al. (2024b)), each sample is rated
from 1 to 5 along three axes: Naturalness, which measures whether the user request is fluent and
realistic; Complexity, which measures whether the task requires non-trivial multi-step tool use; and
Alignment, which measures whether the request semantics match the annotated task graph.
To reduce single-perspective bias, we invite three human evaluators with different backgrounds: one
task-planning researcher who focuses on structural correctness, one domain practitioner who focuses
on tool semantics, and one user-facing reviewer who focuses on request naturalness. For each dataset,
evaluators inspect a randomly sampled subset of the augmented Cycle examples together with their
task nodes and task links. The final score is the average over evaluators and sampled examples.
These values are descriptive expert ratings rather than inferential population estimates. This expert
assessment is used only to rate benchmark instances; it does not involve crowdsourcing, collection of
personal data, or interventions on participants. The results are reported in Table 4.

D.3
MEASURES TO PREVENT DATA LEAKAGE
We strictly isolate the training and evaluation phases to prevent data contamination:
• Offline construction: The data extension is performed before model training and evaluation. No
generation-time construction state is exposed to the evaluated planner.
• Grouped splitting: Augmented samples are grouped by normalized request, ordered task nodes,
and dependency links before train-test assignment. This reduces the risk that near-duplicate
workflow graphs appear across training and testing splits.
• Format preservation: The augmented samples are merged into the original dataset schema, and
all evaluated methods use the same updated split.
D.4
MODEL OVERVIEW AND API-COMPATIBLE INTERFACE
Figure 2 in the main text summarizes the local open-source TopoPlanner pipeline. Here we clarify the
API deployment mode. When the backbone is accessed only through an API, the model exposes text
completions but not token embeddings, so the local graph-token interface in the main model cannot
be applied directly. We therefore keep the same cellular lifting, retrieval, and multi-dimensional
reasoning modules locally, use the API LLM only for textual proposal generation, and let the local
topology module perform the final topology-guided selection and graph-consistent decoding.

F
LIMITATIONS, BROADER IMPACTS, AND ASSET USE
Limitations.
Our empirical evaluation covers four benchmark-style tool-planning datasets; uncon-
strained real-world deployments remain unevaluated. The lifting mechanism targets loop, merge,
and loop-merge workflow regions, and λCI is selected per dataset. In the black-box API setting,
TopoPlanner uses topology-guided proposal selection and decoding because graph-token injection is
unavailable.
Broader impacts.
Topology-consistent planning can improve dependency preservation in work-
flows with feedback, verification, or branch merging. When connected to external APIs or action tools,
the same capability can increase execution risk; the released artifacts therefore comprise benchmark
data and research code rather than a turnkey autonomous agent.

WORKFLOW EXAMPLES AND STRUCTURAL INTERPRETATION
The following examples illustrate how loop, merge, and loop-merge workflows are represented
in the lifted complex. They explain the structural information available to TopoPlanner; because
per-example baseline predictions are not shown, they are not presented as qualitative evidence of
baseline failure.
H.1
CASE STUDY 1: HUGGINGFACE
Example ID: paper_cycle_huggingface_0005.
Request summary: extract information from a historical document, highlight key entities and dates,
convert the result to speech, enhance the audio, transcribe it back, and then revisit the original
document question answering step using the refined transcript.
Topology.
This
workflow
forms
a
loop-merge
pattern:
Document QA →Token
Classification →Text-to-Speech →Audio-to-Audio →ASR →
Document QA, with an additional direct edge Document QA →Text-to-Speech.
The direct edge matters because the speech synthesis stage must inherit both the initial extraction and
the entity-focused refinement, while the ASR output closes a feedback loop for iterative correction.
Structural interpretation.
The lifted representation places the direct merge path and the feedback
path on the boundary of the same cycle-level region. Boundary-closed retrieval therefore exposes
both relations to the planner rather than representing only a single linear path.

Request summary: transcribe a raw video interview, summarize it, generate relevant topics, rewrite

The region contains both the shortcut into Topic Generator and

ADDITIONAL RELATED WORK
Topological Deep Learning.
Topological deep learning (TDL) builds upon early advances in
Topological Signal Processing (TSP) (Barbarossa & Sardellitti (2020); Sardellitti et al. (2021)), which
emphasized that many real-world systems exhibit interactions that go beyond pairwise relations and
are more naturally described by high-dimensional topological structures. This insight has motivated
the generalization of classical graph-theoretic tools from pairwise graphs to richer topological
domains, such as simplicial complexes (SCs), which model interactions among fixed-size groups,
and cell complexes (CWs), which further generalize this representation to arbitrary polyhedral cells.
Along this line, extensions of the Weisfeiler–Lehman (WL) test to SCs and CWs (Bodnar et al.
(2021b;a)) have provided theoretical foundations for distinguishing and propagating high-dimensional
structural information, thereby enabling principled message passing beyond nodes and edges. Build-
ing on these ideas, convolutional architectures operate on SCs and CWs (Ebli et al. (2020); Yang et al.
(2022); Hajij et al. (2020); Yang & Isufi (2023); Hajij et al. (2022)), while attentional variants model
higher-dimensional neighborhoods (Goh et al.; Giusti et al. (2023)).
To unify these heterogeneous developments, the combinatorial complex (CC) framework (Hajij et al.
(2023)) was introduced as a general abstraction that subsumes SCs, CWs, and hypergraphs within a
single message-passing formalism, allowing a systematic treatment of multi-dimensional relational
structures. In parallel, Sheaf Neural Networks (SNNs) (Hansen & Ghrist (2019); Hansen & Gebhart
(2020); Bodnar et al. (2022); Battiloro et al. (2024); Barbero et al. (2022); Wei & Wei (2025)) have
explored a complementary perspective by modeling local consistency constraints via sheaf theory,
demonstrating strong performance in settings with heterophily and structured local interactions.
Recently, topological perspectives have been applied to analyze reasoning in large language models.
Minegishi et al. (2025) introduce reasoning graphs by clustering hidden-state representations across
25


reasoning steps and analyze their structural properties—such as cyclicity, diameter, and small-
world characteristics—on mathematical reasoning benchmarks. These developments suggest that
topological structure is increasingly relevant to LLM reasoning, but direct topology-aware planning
over tool workflows remains underexplored, which is the gap our work addresses.

COMPUTATIONAL COMPLEXITY ANALYSIS
This section characterizes the computational cost of TopoPlanner. The total computational overhead
can be decomposed into four stages aligned with the model design: cellular workflow lifting, cosheaf-
consistent cellular retrieval, multi-dimensional structural reasoning, and LLM tool planning.
J.1
THEORETICAL ANALYSIS
1. Topological Lifting.
The construction extracts a fundamental cycle basis from the underlying
undirected graph Gu = (V, E). For a connected graph, we first construct a spanning tree T in
O(|V | + |E|) time. Each of the β1 = |E| −|V | + 1 non-tree edges induces one fundamental cycle;
standard cycle-basis algorithms compute the basis in O(|V | · |E|) time.
• Efficiency: This lifting can be precomputed for each dataset/tool graph and cached together with
the graph features. During training and inference, TopoPlanner reads the precomputed cellular
structure and only applies request-dependent retrieval and encoding.
2. Cosheaf-Consistent Cellular Retrieval.
For each request, TopoPlanner scores cells by request
relevance and cosheaf-induced boundary compatibility. The practical implementation first applies
a cheap edge prefilter, then performs closure-preserving greedy selection. Its cost is linear in the
number of retained candidate cells after prefiltering; Table 17 reports the measured retrieval time
separately from LLM decoding.
3. Multi-Dimensional Structural Reasoning.
Our encoder employs a message-passing neural
network over the retrieved cell complex. Let X ∗
q denote the retrieved subcomplex and Eadj denote
the boundary and coface adjacency connections in the lifted complex.
• One reasoning layer costs O(|X ∗
q | · d2 + |Eadj| · d) for hidden dimension d.
• Linear scaling in retained incidences: Unlike global Transformer attention, which scales as
O(|X ∗
q |2), message passing is confined to boundary and coface relations. Its cost is therefore
linear in the retained incidence connections; for the dense Daily Life edge pool, retrieval first
applies the 512-edge prefilter in Table 17.
4. LLM Tool Planning Overhead.
The local open-source variant decodes autoregressively with a
frozen planner LLM and one injected graph token. The API variant uses topology to rerank proposals.
• LLM latency dominates, with approximate per-token complexity O(T · d2
model).
• Measured retrieval cost: Table 17 reports mean CPU times of 22.89–39.45 ms over 50 fixed
queries. These measurements exclude LLM generation and therefore do not establish end-to-end
latency overhead.
J.2
SCALABILITY DISCUSSION
Enumerating all simple cycles can be exponential, especially in dense graphs. TopoPlanner instead
constructs one fundamental cycle per non-tree edge, so the untruncated number of 2-cells equals the
cycle rank β1 = |E| −|V | + 1 for a connected graph. This remains linear in graph size even for
the dense Daily Life graph; its request-time retrieval additionally applies the deterministic 512-edge
prefilter reported in Table 17.
