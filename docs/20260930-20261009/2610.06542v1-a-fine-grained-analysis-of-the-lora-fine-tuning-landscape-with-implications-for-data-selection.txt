Abstract 

Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) has become a standard approach for parameter-efficient fine-tuning, yet a fundamental practical question remains unresolved: how should the adapter rank be chosen? An overly small rank may lead to a poorly conditioned optimization landscape, whereas an unnecessarily large rank sacrifices the efficiency that motivates LoRA in the first place. Existing theoretical analyses provide only limited guidance on this trade-off, and their guarantees are typically established under restrictive theoretical settings. We address this gap by developing a substantially sharper landscape theory for LoRA, building on modern results from nonconvex low-rank matrix sensing. Our central insight is that the appropriate adapter rank should depend on the quality of the data-induced optimization geometry, rather than on the model alone. To formalize this connection, we introduce LoRA-RIP, a data-dependent restricted-isometry metric that characterizes the conditioning of the cross-entropy (CE) objective along LoRA-relevant low-rank directions. We prove that sufficient rank over-parameterization, with the required rank explicitly determined by the LoRA-RIP constant, eliminates spurious local minima, thereby extending existing RIP-based guarantees beyond the classical 1/3 regime. This characterization further enables principled data selection under a fixed rank budget. Experiments across language and vision tasks support these theoretical predictions, showing that rank and data quality are two coupled resources that should be jointly considered for more efficient and reliable LoRA fine-tuning. 

# 1 Introduction 

Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) is a standard parameter-efficient method for fine-tuning large models (Hu et al., 2022). Given a pretrained weight matrix W0 ∈ Rn1×n2 , LoRA freezes W0 and learns an additive low-rank update W = W0 +γM , where M = U V ⊤, U ∈ Rn1×r, V ∈ Rn2×r, and γ is a scaling factor. With 

r ≪ min( n1, n 2), this factorization reduces the trainable parameter count for the adapted matrix from n1n2

to r(n1 +n2), lowering optimizer-state and memory requirements while often maintaining strong downstream performance. Realizing these efficiency gains, however, requires an appropriate choice of the adapter rank. Although larger ranks offer greater expressivity (Zeng and Lee, 2024), their practical value cannot be judged from representational capacity alone. In linearized fine-tuning experiments, Jang et al. (2024) observe that lower ranks converge more slowly even when they ultimately reach the same loss. Conversely, increasing rank need not improve downstream performance. For example, Albert et al. (2024) report that, when fine-tuning Phi3 on 15,000 examples, increasing the LoRA rank from 16 to 32 reduces the reported mean accuracy across eight commonsense reasoning tasks from 81 .8% to 80 .3% . These observations illustrate the cost of an unsuitable rank choice: an overly restrictive rank can slow optimization, whereas an unnecessarily large rank can consume additional resources without corresponding performance gains. Adaptive methods such

This leaves a complementary question: how should adapter rank be chosen to support effective fine-tuning without unnecessary parameter overhead? This question naturally leads us to nonconvex low-rank optimization: LoRA learns a factorized update, with the adapter rank r controlling its degree of parameterization (Burer and Monteiro, 2003). A long line of work has characterized the landscapes of factorized problems and established conditions excluding spurious local minima (Bhojanapalli et al., 2016; Ge et al., 2017; Zhu et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2021). In classical matrix sensing, the restricted isometry property (RIP) is a key metric that characterizes the optimization landscape, and it measures how uniformly the sensing data capture low-rank structure, making its constant a natural indicator of the underlying data geometry (Recht et al., 2010). More recent advances show that rank over-parameterization can improve the landscape by eliminating spurious local minima under weaker RIP conditions (Zhang, 2025a;b), directly connecting search rank with data-induced geometry. Classical sensing, however, assumes a fixed ℓ2 objective in matrix space, whereas LoRA settings have cross-entropy loss coupling with the base model structure. Its geometry therefore varies with the model’s responses to the data and the adapter update, making the extension of classical rank–RIP analysis nontrivial. Specifically for the LoRA fine-tuning landscape, Jang et al. (2024) establish no-spurious-minima guarantees in the neural tangent kernel (NTK) regime, while Liu et al. (2025) further relate adapter rank to local optimization geometry under 

ℓ2 loss. These results demonstrate the relevance of landscape theory, but their reliance on local linearization or squared-loss settings limits the guidance they provide for nonlinear cross-entropy fine-tuning. More recently, Kim et al. (2025) move beyond linearization by adapting established low-rank optimization arguments (Zhu et al., 2018; Ha et al., 2020), showing how RIP-type conditions still govern the landscapes in nonlinear LoRA settings and providing numerical evidence for their applicability. Their global-optimality guarantee in the analyzed region corresponds to a RIP constant below 1/3, while larger values allow a “fails-loudly” alternative. However, their analysis leaves two important aspects insufficiently characterized: the CE-specific structure of the RIP constant in LoRA, and how the adapter rank r shapes the optimization landscape beyond the small-RIP regime. Since RIP is strongly determined by the geometry of the fine-tuning data, these two questions are closely connected: understanding LoRA optimization requires relating data-induced RIP to the role of rank in shaping the landscape. Clarifying this relationship is therefore a key step toward a more principled theory of rank choice in LoRA fine-tuning. In this work, we build on theoretical tools from classical low-rank optimization and matrix sensing (Zhang et al., 2021; Zhang, 2025b; Yalçın et al., 2023) and extend them to develop a fine-grained landscape characterization of LoRA under cross-entropy loss. # 2 Related Work 

LoRA methods and theory. Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) (Hu et al., 2022) has become a central parameter-efficient fine-tuning method, and numerous variants have been built on its structure, including quantized adapters (Dettmers et al., 2023), adaptive rank allocation (Zhang et al., 2023), and modified factor updates (Hayou et al., 2024). Other work studies geometric updates and stabilization mechanisms (Zhang and Pilanci, 2024; Yen et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2026; Zhao et al., 2024). Recent theory has studied LoRA from complementary angles. Expressivity results characterize what a given adapter rank can represent (Zeng and Lee, 2024). From the view of optimization, NTK-regime analysis gives benign landscape guarantees for a linearized fine-tuning model (Jang et al., 2024). Other works analyze nonlinear LoRA landscapes or gradient-flow dynamics under structural assumptions (Liu et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025). 

Low-rank optimization. Low-rank matrix optimization provides a geometric foundation for understanding factorized objectives. A rank-constrained variable is often written as X = U V ⊤ by Burer-Monteiro factorization (Burer and Monteiro, 2003), which replaces the rank constraint by a nonconvex optimization problem over the factors. For matrix recovery and related objectives, restricted curvature or RIP-type conditions can imply benign landscape properties, such as the absence of spurious local minima or strict-saddle geometry (Ge et al., 2017; Zhu et al., 2018). Similar geometric analyses have also been developed for more general low-rank objectives (Zhang et al., 2021). A more recent direction studies rank over-parameterization: when the search rank exceeds the target rank r⋆, the required amount of over-parameterization can be expressed through RIP quantities (Zhang, 2025a;b). 

Data selection and training geometry. As innovations in model architecture hit a plateau, the strategic selection of training data becomes increasingly crucial. Existing work includes Shapley-based data valua-tion (Ghorbani and Zou, 2019; Wang et al., 2024), gradient-based data attribution and selection (Pruthi et al., 2020; Xia et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2026) and similarity-based data filtering (Sachdeva et al., 2026; Das et al., 2026). Meanwhile, recent theoretical work explores the statistical properties (Allouah et al., 2026; Rezaei et al., 2026). Complementary work studies training geometry through Hessian spectra and cross-entropy dynamics (Ghorbani et al., 2019; Garrod et al., 2025). # 7 Conclusion 

We studied how adapter rank and fine-tuning data jointly shape LoRA’s optimization landscape under cross-entropy loss. Under the stated assumptions, rank over-parameterization relative to the target rank rules out spurious local minima, with the required factor explicitly determined by our data-dependent LoRA-RIP constant δLoRA  

> k

. The guarantee also covers LoRA-RIP values above 1/3, quantifying how additional rank can compensate for less favorable geometry to achieve a benign landscape. Our framework further motivates data selection under a fixed rank budget, yielding principles based on latent-space coverage and pre-tuning softmax saturation. Experiments support these insights into the interplay between rank and data composition. However, most of the bounds presented in this work are sufficient, and an important future direction is to shorten their gap with necessary ones.

B.1 Stationarity notions 

Let g : Rm×n → R be twice-continuously differentiable. We say that Y ∈ Rm×n is a first-order stationary point if ∇g(Y ) = 0 . We say that Y ∈ Rm×n is a second-order stationary point (SOSP) if ∇g(Y ) = 0 and 

∇2g(Y )[ H, H ] ≥ 0 for any direction H ∈ Rm×n. We say that Y is a local minimum if there exists an open ball B containing Y such that g(Y ) ≤ g(Y ′) for every Y ′ ∈ B. Y is a global minimum if g(Y ) ≤ g(Y ′)

for every Y ′ ∈ Rm×n. A spurious local minimum is a local minimum that is not global. A standard result shows that gradient descent with random initialization avoids strict saddles almost surely; when the iterates converge, this makes SOSPs the relevant local optimality objects (Lee et al., 2016). In particular, every local minimum is an SOSP.

# E Landscape Diagnostics and Robustness Evaluations 

This section examines the optimization geometry and stability of LoRA fine-tuning through rank/checkpoint curvature, ambient matrix-space references, and multi-seed training. We also study the effects of data composition at a common reference point and along training trajectories, evaluate coverage across datasets, backbones, and adapter locations, and examine confidence-band selection on MNLI. The rank-sweep and MNLI data-intervention protocols are described in Appendix D. Together, these evaluations connect rank-dependent optimization gains to data-induced geometry and the practical effectiveness of landscape-guided selection.

In classical matrix sensing and low-rank optimization, RIP relates measurement geometry to recovery and landscape guarantees (Recht et al., 2010; Ge et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2021; Zhang, 2025b). Its primary value in our framework is likewise the relationship it establishes between data geometry and rank requirements, not an isolated numerical constant for a particular run. Exact certification is already computationally difficult for a fixed linear compressed-sensing matrix: the condition controls singular values across all supports of a prescribed size, and computing optimal RIP constants is NP-hard in general (Tillmann and Pfetsch, 2014). Hardness also extends to approximate RIP certification (Weed, 2018). For LoRA, the defining curvature bounds require uniform control over the continuous family of target-to-update segments and all admissible low-rank directions. Evaluating selected checkpoints in a diagnostic subspace does not compute these extrema. Likewise, determining r⋆ requires identifying a global minimizer of the nonlinear regularized matrix-space objective Fλ and certifying its rank. Local optimization does not establish global optimality or uniqueness; r⋆ refers to the low-rank target specified in our assumption. The matrix-space references and threshold or energy ranks reported below describe numerical solutions, not certified global targets. The informative consequence of the analysis is how these quantities interact: The sufficient condition permits larger LoRA-RIP values at larger ranks, while coverage, softmax weighting, and nonlinear residual curvature enter the data-side certificate in explicit ways. Our experiments examine these mechanisms through controlled rank and data interventions, attained objectives, projected GN and CE-Hessian spectra, and multi-seed performance comparisons. The projected spectra characterize local geometry, rather than the theorem’s uniform LoRA-RIP constant. Lower attained objectives at larger ranks and the changes in geometry and performance under coverage and confidence-band selection provide complementary, qualitative evidence for the rank–data relationship developed by the theory.

# F Limitations 

Our theoretical analysis focuses on single-matrix LoRA fine-tuning with cross-entropy loss under low-rank target and LoRA-RIP assumptions. The resulting rank bounds are sufficient rather than necessary, leaving room for tighter characterizations of the relationship between adapter rank and data-induced geometry. Our empirical evaluation examines this relationship through controlled classification experiments across datasets and backbones. Extending the theory to joint multi-layer adaptation and evaluating the data-selection principles in larger-scale generative fine-tuning are natural directions for future work. 50
