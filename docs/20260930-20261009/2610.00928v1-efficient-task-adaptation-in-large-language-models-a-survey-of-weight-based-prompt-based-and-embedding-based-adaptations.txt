Abstract 

As large language models are increasingly de-ployed across diverse downstream tasks, ef-ficient task adaptation has emerged as a cen-tral challenge. In response, a wide range of task adaptation methods have been proposed, spanning parameter-efficient fine-tuning, in-context learning, and embedding-injection ap-proaches. However, these lines of work have largely evolved within individual paradigms, leaving their cross-paradigm relationships and trade-offs underexplored, especially for re-cently emerging embedding-based adaptations. This survey presents a unified framework that categorizes task adaptation methods by where and how task information is encoded: model weights, input prompts, or injected task embed-dings. We provide a comprehensive taxonomy that integrates these paradigms, analyze their key strengths and limitations to explain how dif-ferent adaptation paradigms have evolved, clar-ify relationships across paradigms, and high-light open problems for future research. 1

1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed across a wide range of downstream tasks (Brown et al., 2020; Wei et al., 2022a; Mi-naee et al., 2024; Raza et al., 2025), which makes efficient and effective task adaptation a central chal-lenge for practical deployment. At the same time, as LLMs continue to scale in model size and train-ing cost (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022; Achiam et al., 2023; OpenAI, 2025; Comanici et al., 2025), retraining or fully fine-tuning these mod-els has become increasingly expensive and often infeasible, even when task-specific data are avail-able. This growing gap between the demand for task adaptation and the cost of full fine-tuning has driven sustained interest in methods that reduce

Consequently, a wide range of approaches have been proposed, including methods that limit the number of trainable parameters or perform adapta-tion without any parameter updates. In this work, we focus on efficient task adaptation , which we define as adapting pretrained or instruction-tuned LLMs to downstream tasks without full fine-tuning. Existing research has explored multiple paradigms for efficient task adaptation. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) (Houlsby et al., 2019; Lester et al., 2021; Hu et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2022a) adapts models by training a small number of task-specific parameters while keeping the backbone model largely frozen. In-context learning (ICL) (Brown et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2022b; Lu et al., 2022b; Zhou et al., 2022) enables training-free adaptation by specifying task information directly in prompts during inference. More recently, studies have shown that internal ac-tivations induced by ICL encode rich task-relevant information, giving rise to embedding-based adaptation that extracts and reuses explicit task representations during inference (Hendel et al., 2023; Todd et al., 2024). This line of work was originally developed in the context of mechanistic interpretability (Olah et al., 2020; Elhage et al., 2021), but has recently begun to be explored for practical deployment (Li et al., 2025d; Wang et al., 2025a; Park et al., 2026). These approaches can be broadly categorized by where task information is encoded. We define three task adaptation paradigms from this perspective. 

Weight-based adaptation encodes task information in model weights, including additional or modi-fied parameters introduced by weight-based PEFT.

Despite rapid progress, research on task adapta-tion has largely evolved independently within each paradigm, leaving the relationships among weight-based, prompt-based, and embedding-based meth-ods underexplored. While prior PEFT-only and ICL-only surveys provide useful taxonomies within their respective scopes (Dong et al., 2024; Sahoo et al., 2024; Vatsal and Dubey, 2024; Han et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025b; Mao et al., 2025), they do not jointly organize these paradigms under a common task-adaptation perspective, especially for emerging ICL-derived embedding-based methods. The following paragraphs further explain how these strengths and limitations are derived from the literature. 

Weight-Based Adaptation. Prior work has shown that (weight-based) PEFT methods can achieve strong and stable performance (Mahabadi et al., 2021; Mosbach et al., 2023; Weyssow et al., 2025), often comparable to full fine-tuning (Hu et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2022a). In addition, well-established PEFT libraries are widely available for various models, making these methods easily ac-cessible to practitioners (Mangrulkar et al., 2022). However, these methods require access to model parameters and involve training additional or par-tial weights, which incurs memory and computa-tion costs during training. They may also introduce adapter-management overhead in serving systems, since task-specific modules must be dynamically loaded and scheduled across requests (Sheng et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2024a). In particular, mixed-task batching can be challenging when prompts within the same batch correspond to different tasks and require activating different task-specific mod-ules. Serving such heterogeneous requests often requires specialized kernels, scheduling policies, and pipelined execution to mitigate latency (Wu et al., 2024a; Li et al., 2025b). Prompt-Based Adaptation. Prompt-based adap-tation interacts with the task-solving LLM only through its standard input-output interface and therefore does not require access to its parame-ters or internal activations. This makes it appli-cable to proprietary or API-only models such as GPT-5 (OpenAI, 2025) and GPT-6 (OpenAI, 2026). Natural-language prompts are also human-readable and readily editable, allowing users to directly spec-ify and modify task instructions or demonstrations. Beyond manual prompt design, prompts can be op-timized in a black-box manner, with an optimizer LLM deriving feedback from the task LLM’s eval-uation history and using it to iteratively refine the prompt (Zhou et al., 2022; Pryzant et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2024a). Recent work has extended this feedback-driven optimization framework be-yond task instructions to agent skills, which encode reusable procedural knowledge and tool-use strate-gies as textual artifacts (Alzubi et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026). However, prompt-based adaptation can incur substantial costs from repeated LLM in-ference during prompt optimization and from the additional prefill computation and KV-cache mem-ory required by long prompts at deployment (Choi et al., 2023; Liskavets et al., 2025; Hooper et al., 2025). Furthermore, because prompts serve as the primary means of task specification, perfor-mance can vary substantially with instruction for-mulation and demonstration selection or ordering, potentially requiring careful prompt construction or iterative prompt optimization to achieve reliable performance (Zhou et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2022b; Lu et al., 2022b). 

Embedding-Based Adaptation. Compared with weight-based approaches, embedding-based meth-ods typically require far fewer parameters and do not modify model weights. For example, several representative embedding-based methods encode task information using roughly 0.1K–130K parame-ters for Llama-3.1-8B (Todd et al., 2024; Peng et al., 2024b; Huang et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025d; Park et al., 2026), whereas LoRA adapters for the same model require more than 3M parameters. However, both ICL-derived and learned embedding meth-ods require access to internal model activations to extract or inject task embeddings. ICL-derived approaches typically require precomputing task embeddings and identifying appropriate injection locations for each task (Todd et al., 2024; Park et al., 2026). In contrast, learned embedding ap-proaches, including soft prompting methods, can suffer from optimization instability and sensitivity to embedding initialization and hyperparameter set-tings (Lester et al., 2021; Li and Liang, 2021; Chen et al., 2022). In addition, recent learned embedding methods often require thousands to tens of thou-sands of training iterations to converge (Peng et al., 2024b; Saglam et al., 2025; Kang et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025a). Overall, Table 1 highlights the key characteris-tics that distinguish these paradigms and provides insight into why different task adaptation strate-gies have emerged. In Sections 3–5, we examine representative methods within each paradigm and discuss how these methods have evolved in light of the perspective summarized in Table 1. As a sup-plementary reference, Appendix B.3 provides an illustrative quantitative snapshot in Table 2, based on values reported in prior work. 

3 Weight-Based Adaptation 

Weight-based adaptation encodes task-relevant in-formation in model weights or additional trainable modules. In this section, we categorize weight-based methods into single-task and multi-task adap-tations and describe their respective methodologies in detail. Single-task adaptation primarily focuses on improving training efficiency and task perfor-mance, while multi-task adaptation addresses chal-lenges in switching tasks during inference or per-forming batched inference where prompts in the same batch correspond to different tasks. 

3.1 Single-Task Adaptation LoRA Variants. LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) in-troduces trainable low-rank updates to pretrained weight matrices. Due to its strong and stable per-formance, negligible inference overhead, and sim-ple modular design, LoRA has inspired a large body of follow-up work that develops variants to improve expressivity, training dynamics, and efficiency. One line of work enhances the ex-pressivity of model updates by increasing the in-trinsic rank through alternative parametrizations, such as replacing standard matrix multiplication with Hadamard or Kronecker products (Nam et al., 2021; Edalati et al., 2022; Yeh et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2025). Another line focuses on improving training stability and convergence speed through better initialization strategies, such as leveraging singular value decomposition of pretrained weights or gradient matrices to initialize LoRA modules more effectively (Meng et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024a). A further line of work explores adaptive rank allocation, dynamically adjusting the effective rank across LoRA modules during training (Zhang et al., 2023b), or assigning different ranks to dif-ferent modules based on their importance (Ding et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024e). Other variants tar-get training-time memory efficiency, particularly for large-scale LLMs, by combining LoRA with low-bit quantization of pretrained weights during training (Dettmers et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2024b; Guo et al., 2024a). Complementary approaches further reduce the number of trainable parameters or storage requirements through parameter shar-ing across LoRA modules (Kopiczko et al., 2024; Renduchintala et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024a), high-lighting the flexibility of the LoRA framework. 

Other PEFT. Beyond LoRA, a variety of PEFT methods have been proposed. Adapters (Houlsby et al., 2019) insert small trainable modules between Transformer layers of a pretrained model, while Compacter (Mahabadi et al., 2021) reduces adapter parameters by replacing adapter linear layers with low-rank hypercomplex multiplication. Other ap-proaches reduce trainable parameters by selectively updating subsets of model weights. For example, FISH Mask (Sung et al., 2021) updates parame-ters based on their approximate Fisher Informa-tion (Fisher, 1922; Amari, 1996; Kirkpatrick et al., 2017), and BitFit (Zaken et al., 2022) fine-tunes only bias terms. Another line of work applies element-wise rescaling operations to internal activa-tions. (IA) 3 (Liu et al., 2022a) introduces learnable scaling vectors that rescales internal activations, RED (Wu et al., 2024b) extends this idea by jointly learning scaling and bias vectors, and PARA (Liu et al., 2024f) trains a lightweight vector genera-tor to dynamically produce scaling vectors. Some methods combine or extend PEFT techniques, such as DePT (Shi and Lipani, 2024), which integrates prompt tuning with LoRA-style updates on frozen word embeddings, and its variant ADePT (Tang et al., 2025a). Other approaches focus on improv-ing efficiency. LST (Sung et al., 2022) trains a small auxiliary network to reduce training-time memory requirements by avoiding backpropaga-tion through the backbone model, and RoAD (Liao and Monz, 2024) applies trainable rotations to lin-ear layer outputs to reduce the trainable parameters. 

3.2 Multi-Task Adaptation Multi-Task PEFT. Compared to prompt-based and embedding-based adaptation, weight-based methods typically incur higher overhead when switching tasks during inference or processing mixed-task batches, as task-specific modules must be dynamically loaded and scheduled across re-quests. To mitigate this limitation, several methods introduce routing-based mechanisms that maintain multiple adapters and use (soft) routers to select or combine task-relevant adapters, either at the task level (Pfeiffer et al., 2021; Huang et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023a) or at the instance level (Wang et al., 2023d; Feng et al., 2024; Tian et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2024a; Liu et al., 2025). These routers are typically implemented using learnable scalar weights or shallow neural networks. Other ap-proaches explore alternative strategies for multi-task inference. O-LoRA (Wang et al., 2023b) pro-poses a LoRA-based continual learning framework, while FLoRA (Wen and Chaudhuri, 2024) replaces LoRA updates with Hadamard-product-based up-dates, enabling efficient heterogeneous batching by avoiding expensive batched matrix multiplications. 

Model Merging. Model merging defines task vectors (Ilharco et al., 2023) as parameter differ-ences between fine-tuned models and a shared pre-trained model and combines multiple such vectors to enable multi-task inference without additional fine-tuning. Although model merging typically un-derperforms multi-task training, it is particularly useful when fine-tuned models are available but the corresponding training data cannot be accessed, for example due to data privacy or intellectual prop-erty constraints. Several methods aim to reduce interference between task-specific parameter up-dates during model merging (Yadav et al., 2023b; Yu et al., 2024; Gargiulo et al., 2025), while oth-ers propose fine-grained merging strategies that as-sign different merging coefficients across tasks, lay-ers, or parameters (Yang et al., 2024b; Zhou et al., 2024b; Xu et al., 2025; Yao et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2025). Another line of work focuses on perform-ing model merging only on PEFT updates (Zhang et al., 2023a; Tang et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2024). Among these, LoRA-LEGO (Zhao et al., 2024) introduces rank-wise LoRA merging by identify-ing row-column pairs of LoRA matrices as minimal semantic units, clustering them across tasks, and us-ing cluster centroids to construct merged adapters. 4 Prompt-Based Adaptation 

Prompt-based adaptation, commonly referred to as in-context learning (ICL), adapts LLMs by provid-ing task-relevant information directly in the input prompt at inference time, in the form of natural-language instructions and/or input-output demon-strations. Existing methods can be broadly catego-rized into instruction optimization , which focuses on refining task instructions, and demonstration optimization , which selects, orders, or generates input-output demonstrations. Collectively, these methods primarily aim to improve stability and performance of prompt-based adaptation. 

4.1 Instruction Optimization 

Instruction optimization focuses on refining task-specific instructions used in the prompt. These methods typically adopt a two-LLM framework, consisting of an optimizer LLM that updates instruc-tions and a task LLM that performs the downstream task using the updated instructions. In practice, strong proprietary models such as GPT-4o (Hurst et al., 2024) are often used as the optimizer LLM to enable more effective instruction optimization. A common strategy is to iteratively refine instructions using feedback generated by the optimizer LLM itself (Pryzant et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024b; Agarwal et al., 2025; Juneja et al., 2025; Yan et al., 2025). For example, ProTeGi (Pryzant et al., 2023) uses the optimizer LLM to analyze errors made un-der the current instruction and feeds the resulting feedback back into the optimizer LLM to update the instructions. Other approaches formulate in-struction optimization as an evolutionary process, iteratively improving instructions through mutation, selection, or related evolutionary operations (Guo et al., 2024c; Fernando et al., 2024; Agrawal et al., 2026). For example, GEPA (Agrawal et al., 2026) combines reflective feedback with evolutionary search, using natural-language reflection to pro-pose prompt updates and Pareto-based selection to retain promising candidates. Several methods lever-age carefully designed meta-prompts, i.e., prompts that instruct the optimizer LLM how to refine in-structions, to improve optimization stability and effectiveness (Ye et al., 2024; Tang et al., 2025b). In contrast, some approaches avoid directly opti-mizing instructions and instead optimize auxiliary components, such as soft prompts (Lester et al., 2021) provided to the optimizer LLM or the opti-mizer LLM itself (Chen et al., 2024b; Lin et al., 2024; Kwon et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2025). For instance, InstructZero (Chen et al., 2024b) and INSTINCT (Lin et al., 2024) optimize only soft prompts, while StablePrompt (Kwon et al., 2024) fine-tunes the optimizer LLM to improve instruc-tion generation. Beyond the optimization of task instructions, re-cent work on LLM agents has explored the auto-matic construction and refinement of agent skills ,which are reusable artifacts that encode procedural knowledge and tool-use strategies for a particular task family or domain (Mi et al., 2026; Alzubi et al., 2026; Ni et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026). From the perspective of prompt-based adaptation, methods that refine the textual content of agent skills are closely related to instruction optimization: both adapt the task LLM without updating its param-eters by modifying natural-language content pro-vided in the input prompt, often using feedback from black-box task evaluations. The distinction between them therefore lies primarily in the type of content being optimized: instruction optimization refines instructions specifying a task’s objective, constraints, expected output, or solution strategy, whereas agent-skill methods construct or refine reusable procedural knowledge that guides how an agent acts over multiple steps, interacts with tools, and responds to intermediate outcomes. 

4.2 Demonstration Optimization 

Including a small number of input-output demon-strations in the prompt can effectively convey task information to LLMs (Brown et al., 2020). How-ever, task performance is highly sensitive to both which demonstrations are selected and how they are ordered within the prompt (Zhao et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2022b; Lu et al., 2022b). As a result, a large body of work has explored methods for optimizing demonstration selection and ordering. KATE (Liu et al., 2022b) retrieves demonstrations by embedding candidate examples and selecting nearest neighbors for each test input, and subse-quent methods such as MDL (Wu et al., 2023) and ConE (Peng et al., 2024a) adopt this retrieval step to first narrow the candidate pool before apply-ing more refined selection strategies. Another line of work explicitly promotes diversity among se-lected demonstrations using determinantal point processes (DPPs) (Kulesza et al., 2012; Ye et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2023). Beyond selecting which demonstrations to include, several methods also consider demonstration ordering. Some approaches treat an ordered sequence of demonstrations as the basic unit of selection rather than scoring demon-strations independently (Lu et al., 2022b; Wu et al., 2023, 2024e; Guo et al., 2024b), while others formulate demonstration selection as a sequen-tial decision-making process optimized with rein-forcement learning (Zhang et al., 2022; Scarlatos and Lan, 2023; Wang et al., 2025e). For exam-ple, AES (Zhang et al., 2022) formulates demon-stration selection as a Markov Decision Process, where the policy state consists of all previously selected demonstrations, and trains an offline Q-learning policy (Mnih et al., 2013) to select each new demonstration conditioned on this history. In settings where only unlabeled data are avail-able, several methods selectively annotate demon-strations to support in-context learning (Su et al., 2022; Mavromatis et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024b). For example, Vote-K (Su et al., 2022) annotates diverse unlabeled examples using a k-nearest neighbor graph constructed in the Sentence-BERT (Reimers and Gurevych, 2019) embedding space, while IDEAL (Zhang et al., 2024b) extends this approach with influence-driven selection to better approximate the underlying data distribu-tion. More recently, LLM-generated demonstra-tions have emerged as an effective alternative to human-curated examples (Long et al., 2024; Yehu-dai et al., 2024; Nadas et al., 2025). Some ap-proaches generate synthetic input-output pairs di-rectly (Chen et al., 2023; Su et al., 2024; Wan et al., 2025), while others augment demonstrations with LLM-generated reasoning paths (Shao et al., 2023; He et al., 2024b; Honda and Oka, 2025), enabling chain-of-thought (CoT) (Wei et al., 2022b) reason-ing during inference. When a large number of demonstrations are available, scaling the number of demonstrations from a few to many, a setting commonly referred to as many-shot ICL , can fur-ther improve task performance and robustness to demonstration selection and ordering (Zhang et al., 2025a; Bertsch et al., 2025). However, these gains come at the cost of increased memory and compu-tation that scale with the number of demonstrations, as well as attention dispersion (Yuan et al., 2024) in long prompts, which can degrade performance. 

5 Embedding-Based Adaptation 

Embedding-based adaptation represents task in-formation as explicit vectors, referred to as task embeddings , which are injected into model acti-vations during inference, typically via addition or concatenation. Existing methods can be broadly divided into two classes based on how these em-beddings are obtained: ICL-derived task embed-dings and learned task embeddings . The former extracts task embeddings from internal activations induced by ICL, while the latter directly optimizes task embeddings through gradient-based training. Although these two classes are respectively related to ICL and PEFT, both aim to improve downstream task performance without relying on task-specific prompts during inference. 

5.1 ICL-Derived Task Embeddings Non-Contrastively Derived. Early work, no-tably Task Vectors (Hendel et al., 2023) and Func-tion Vectors (Todd et al., 2024), showed that last-token internal activations produced during few-shot inference encode rich task-relevant information, which can be extracted and injected into model ac-tivations to enable task execution in a zero-shot set-ting. Building on this observation, non-contrastive embedding-based adaptation methods aggregate such activations across multiple few-shot prompts and aim to identify effective injection locations that maximize task performance. Injection locations are determined using various strategies, including validation-based sweeps over layers (Hendel et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024a; Wang et al., 2025a), reinforcement-learning-based optimization (Huang et al., 2024; Cai et al., 2025), and gradient-based optimization that softly controls task injection (Li et al., 2025d; Park et al., 2026). While most approaches operate at the layer level, some ex-plore finer-grained injection at the attention-head level (Huang et al., 2024; Park et al., 2026). Recent results indicate that these methods can achieve per-formance comparable to few-shot ICL; however, evaluations have largely focused on relatively sim-ple tasks, motivating broader evaluation on more complex reasoning and generation tasks. 

Contrastively Derived. Contrastively derived task embeddings originate from Inference-Time In-tervention (ITI) (Li et al., 2023b), which showed that differences between last-token activations in-duced by contrastive prompt pairs encode direc-tional information that can be injected into model activations to steer model behavior. ITI and subse-quent work have leveraged such contrastive prompt pairs primarily for behavior steering, including im-proving truthfulness or safety (Liu et al., 2024b; Rimsky et al., 2024; Qiu et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025c; Zhan et al., 2026). More recent methods extend this paradigm to task adaptation by con-structing contrastive pairs where one prompt con-tains correct task demonstrations and the other contains incorrect or missing demonstrations, en-abling task execution via the resulting difference vectors (Wang et al., 2025d; Liu and Deng, 2025). Using contrastively derived embeddings for gen-eral task adaptation is relatively underexplored, and systematic comparisons with non-contrastive ap-proaches remain an open area for further study. 

5.2 Learned Task Embeddings Soft Prompting. Prompt Tuning (Lester et al., 2021) adapts models by prepending learnable continuous embeddings, known as soft prompts ,to the input embedding sequence and optimiz-ing only these embeddings. Although highly parameter-efficient, prompt tuning is often un-stable and typically underperforms weight-based PEFT methods such as LoRA (Hu et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2022a), motivating subsequent im-provements. Some approaches dynamically adapt task-specific soft prompts to produce instance-specific soft prompts, leading to improved perfor-mance (Asai et al., 2022; Jain et al., 2024). Another line of work explores transfer learning by lever-aging soft prompts from multiple source tasks to initialize or compose target-task prompts, thereby transferring task knowledge and improving adapta-tion (Vu et al., 2022; Asai et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023f; Belanec et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025b). For example, SPoT (Vu et al., 2022), MPT (Wang et al., 2023f), and Task Prompt Vectors (Belanec et al., 2025) initialize target-task soft prompts using soft prompts learned from related source tasks. 

Other Learned Embeddings. Other learned-embedding approaches adapt models by optimiz-ing task embeddings via gradient-based training, where the embeddings are injected into inter-nal activations, typically via additive interven-tion. LIVE (Peng et al., 2024b) and M 2IV (Li et al., 2025c) introduce layer-wise learnable vec-tors with associated scaling factors that are trained end-to-end. LTV (Saglam et al., 2025) extracts attention-head outputs from few-shot inference and constructs layer-wise task embeddings as learned weighted combinations of heads within each layer. However, these methods often require a large num-ber of training iterations, sometimes exceeding those of standard PEFT approaches (Peng et al., 2024b; Saglam et al., 2025; Kang et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025a). Moreover, these methods are often compared with ICL-derived embedding ap-proaches, while their conceptual similarity to soft prompting methods is often overlooked. 

6 Open Problems and Discussion 

In this section, we present four open research problems that cut across all three task adaptation paradigms for LLMs. Additional discussions are provided in Appendix B, including comparisons between our taxonomy and prior PEFT-only or ICL-only survey taxonomies, the distinction be-tween weight-based and embedding-based adapta-tions, the rationale for classifying soft prompting as embedding-based adaptation, and a limited quanti-tative analysis. 

Hybrid and Compositional Task Adaptation. 

Beyond relying on a single adaptation paradigm, an important research problem is to jointly lever-age multiple forms of task information, such as learned embeddings, prompts, and trained mod-ules, to strengthen task adaptation. As a representa-tive example, Instruction Prompt Tuning (Singhal et al., 2023) prepends a shared soft prompt to task-specific instructions and demonstrations, enabling complementary task signals to jointly guide model behavior and better align an instruction-tuned LLM with domain-specific instruction semantics, thereby improving the safety, grounding, and completeness of long-form generations. Despite such initial ef-forts, how best to combine different task adaptation paradigms remains underexplored, presenting an important direction for future research. 

Data Efficiency and Synthetic Supervision. 

Most task adaptation methods require a nontriv-ial number of labeled demonstrations to achieve strong performance (Lester et al., 2021; Hu et al., 2022; Agarwal et al., 2024). To reduce reliance on human-curated supervision, LLM-generated data have emerged as a scalable alternative and are in-creasingly adopted for task adaptation (Long et al., 2024; Yehudai et al., 2024; Nadas et al., 2025). Prior work shows that synthetic demonstrations can differ from human-curated ones in fidelity, bias, and diversity (Li et al., 2024b; Nadas et al., 2025), yet how these differences affect different task adap-tation paradigms has not been thoroughly studied. Addressing this question could provide useful in-sights into how synthetic data should be used across different task adaptation paradigms. 

Benchmarks for Evaluating Task Adaptation. 

Existing task adaptation methods have been evalu-ated on a broad range of established benchmarks, summarized in Tables 3-6. These benchmarks cover diverse categories, including natural lan-guage understanding, reasoning, mathematics, cod-ing, summarization, and safety and trustworthiness. While such benchmarks have served as controlled testbeds for comparing adaptation methods, re-cent developments in LLM research suggest the need to expand evaluations to more challenging settings. These include generating long-form re-sponses (Wu et al., 2024d; Bai et al., 2024; Que et al., 2024), solving complex multi-step reason-ing problems (He et al., 2024a; Glazer et al., 2024; Phan et al., 2025; Art of Problem Solving, 2025; Lin et al., 2025; Balunovi´ c et al., 2025), and main-taining coherent behavior in long-horizon or inter-active environments (Shridhar et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2022; Yao et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2023; Wei et al., 2025). Evaluating adaptation methods under such settings may provide deeper insights into their robustness, error accumulation, and long-range rea-soning capabilities. 

Beyond Task Adaptation: Encoding Behavioral and Contextual Information. Beyond task adaptation, these techniques have also been ex-plored for encoding diverse behavioral and contex-tual signals in LLMs, including safety/truthfulness, persona, and long-context knowledge. Prompting offers a simple and flexible interface for specify-ing such signals in natural language, but its effec-tiveness can be limited for some attributes (e.g., safety or persona) (Zheng et al., 2024a,b), and long prompts may introduce additional inference over-head. To address these limitations, recent methods encode such information directly into model pa-rameters or embeddings. For example, SHINE (Liu et al., 2026) and Doc-to-LoRA (Charakorn et al., 2026) generate LoRA adapters that internalize information from long-contexts. More broadly, embedding-based approaches have been used to encode various types of information, including safety/truthfulness (Li et al., 2023b; Wang et al., 2025c), persona (Potertì et al., 2025; Feng et al., 2026), and long-context knowledge (Eyuboglu et al., 2025), suggesting that expanding the range of information encoded through such adaptation techniques is an interesting research direction. 

7 Conclusion 

This survey presents a unified overview of task adaptation in LLMs by organizing existing meth-ods according to where task information is encoded. Through this framework, we connect weight-based, prompt-based, and embedding-based adaptations and discuss their relationships, trade-offs, and shared open problems. We hope this survey serves as a useful reference for understanding the indepen-dently evolving landscape of LLM task adaptation. 

Limitations 

This survey provides a unified overview of task adaptation techniques for large language models, but it has several limitations. First, given the rapid pace of recent progress in this area, the taxonomy and comparisons presented here may not fully cap-ture the most recent developments. Second, the sur-vey emphasizes high-level methodological distinc-tions and qualitative trade-offs rather than exhaus-tive empirical re-evaluation of individual methods. This focus is intended to highlight the key strengths and limitations of different adaptation paradigms, thereby contextualizing existing approaches and motivating future research. Finally, although many task adaptation techniques for LLMs may be ap-plicable to other types of models, such as mul-timodal LLMs or visual generative models, this survey focuses exclusively on text-based LLMs, leaving such extensions for future work.

B Discussion 

B.1 Comparison with Previous PEFT-only and ICL-only Taxonomies 

This work proposes a unified taxonomy that covers weight-based, prompt-based, and embedding-based task adaptation techniques. While most embedding-based adaptation methods, except for soft prompt-ing (Lester et al., 2021; Li and Liang, 2021), have only recently emerged and therefore lack dedi-cated surveys, several PEFT-only and ICL-only surveys have previously proposed taxonomies for weight-based and prompt-based adaptation meth-ods (Lialin et al., 2023; Dong et al., 2024; Sahoo et al., 2024; Vatsal and Dubey, 2024; Han et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025b; Mao et al., 2025). In this section, we compare our taxonomy with those pro-posed in representative PEFT-only and ICL-only surveys. We select representative surveys based on citation impact, relevance to each subfield, and pub-lication or arXiv release year. The selected surveys are listed below: 

PEFT-only surveys 

• P1. Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning for Large Models: A Comprehensive Survey (Han et al., 2024) • P2. Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning in Large Models: A Survey of Methodologies (Wang et al., 2025b) • P3. A Survey on LoRA of Large Language Models (Mao et al., 2025) 

ICL-only surveys 

• I1. A Survey on In-context Learning (Dong et al., 2024) • I2. A Systematic Survey of Prompt Engineer-ing in Large Language Models: Techniques and Applications (Sahoo et al., 2024) • I3. A Survey of Prompt Engineering Methods in Large Language Models for Different NLP Tasks (Vatsal and Dubey, 2024) For ease of discussion, we refer to these sur-veys as P1–P3 for PEFT surveys and I1–I3 for ICL surveys. It is important to note that taxonomies proposed in different surveys naturally reflect the scope and objectives of the respective works. Our goal here is therefore not to claim that our taxon-omy is generally preferable to previous ones, but to clarify how the coverage and perspective adopted in our taxonomy differ from prior PEFT-only and ICL-only taxonomies and how they align with the goal of contextualizing a broad range of methods under the theme of task adaptation in LLMs. Compared with previous single-field surveys, our taxonomy differs in two main aspects. First, our taxonomy spans weight-based, prompt-based, and embedding-based adaptation within a single framework. This broader coverage provides a uni-fied perspective on task adaptation in LLMs and en-ables a contextualized view of different approaches based on how task information is encoded and uti-lized. Second, most existing PEFT-only and ICL-only surveys primarily cover works published up to around 2024, whereas our survey additionally includes more recent methods published in 2025 and 2026. Considering that several directions, such as instruction optimization, have recently evolved, introducing a new taxonomy helps better reflect the current landscape of task adaptation methods. Be-low, we further compare our taxonomy with previ-ous ones for weight-based and prompt-based adap-tation, respectively. 

Detailed Comparison with PEFT-only Tax-onomies. The PEFT-only surveys P1 and P2 categorize methods primarily according to fine-grained technical design choices, such as adapter-based methods, selective parameter up-dates, reparameterization-based fine-tuning, and quantization-based approaches. Similarly, P3 fo-cuses on detailed categorization of LoRA variants. These taxonomies provide fine-grained method-ological classifications that help organize the large body of PEFT research. In contrast, our taxon-omy places PEFT methods within the broader cat-egory of weight-based adaptation . This category additionally includes approaches such as model merging and multi-task adaptation, which are not typically emphasized in previous PEFT-only tax-onomies. Detailed Comparison with ICL-only Tax-onomies. Previous ICL surveys adopt differ-ent perspectives in organizing prompt-based meth-ods. Our taxonomy organizes prompt-based adap-tation from a different perspective by focusing on how task information is encoded and on the main challenges addressed in task adaptation. B.2 Detailed Comparison of Weight-Based and Embedding-Based Adaptations 

In our taxonomy, we distinguish between weight-based and embedding-based adaptations. A concep-tual comparison of these paradigms is summarized in Table 1 of Section 2. In this section, we further clarify the differences between these two adapta-tion paradigms. Following terminology commonly used in prior literature (Bishop and Nasrabadi, 2006; Goodfel-low et al., 2016), we first clarify the meanings of 

weights and embeddings (or activations) . Concep-tually, weights are parameters that define the trans-formations applied to activations, typically involv-ing multiplicative operations. In contrast, embed-dings or activations refer to the outputs produced by these transformations. Based on this distinction, we categorize methods that encode task information into model weights, including additional weights or rescaling coefficients, as weight-based adaptation .In contrast, methods that encode task information directly in activation-space representations are cat-egorized as embedding-based adaptation . This in-cludes approaches that encode task information in soft prompts within the input embedding space or in injected activation vectors within the activation space of intermediate layers. At first glance, this distinction may appear unfa-miliar because weight-based PEFT methods such as LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) are often discussed within the broader PEFT literature together with soft prompting methods. In our taxonomy, however, weight-based PEFT methods are categorized as weight-based adaptation because they encode task information in model weights, whereas soft prompt-ing methods are categorized as embedding-based adaptation because task information is encoded in the embedding space. We introduce this catego-rization because it more clearly reflects where task information is encoded in the model. This perspective also helps clarify relationships between different adaptation approaches. For in-stance, recently proposed learned embedding meth-ods (Peng et al., 2024b; Li et al., 2025c; Saglam et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025a; Kang et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025), such as LTV (Saglam et al., 2025), share conceptual similarities with soft prompting approaches because both encode task informa-tion in activation-space representations optimized through gradient-based training. However, this connection is often overlooked in previous stud-ies (Peng et al., 2024b; Saglam et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025a; Yang et al., 2025), which rarely dis-cuss or compare these methods with soft prompting approaches. By organizing methods according to where and how task information is encoded, our taxonomy highlights these conceptual connections and provides a unified framework for systemati-cally analyzing and comparing a broad range of task adaptation techniques. 

B.3 Quantitative Meta-Analysis 

The values summarized in Table 2 are derived from the results reported in prior work (Park et al., 2026). We provide this focused quantitative comparison to complement the qualitative analysis in Section 2 by illustrating differences in task performance, train-able parameter count, and runtime among represen-tative methods. The Big-Bench Hard scores and trainable param-eter counts illustrate the trade-off between task per-formance and parameter efficiency. Weight-based adaptation remains a strong baseline, with LoRA and (IA) 3 achieving the highest scores among the compared methods. In contrast, 10-shot ICL re-quires no trainable parameters but performs lower than these PEFT methods, reflecting the trade-off between training-free adaptation and task perfor-mance. Embedding-based methods use substan-tially fewer trainable parameters than weight-based methods. Among ICL-derived embedding meth-ods, SITE achieves the strongest performance, out-performing 10-shot ICL while using only 1.02K trainable parameters. This suggests that ICL-derived task embeddings have the potential to pro-vide a compact alternative to repeatedly includ-ing demonstrations in the input prompt. However, the low scores of FV and LIVE also indicate that embedding-based adaptation does not uniformly improve performance, and its effectiveness depends strongly on how task embeddings are constructed and injected. The runtime results should be interpreted sep-arately from the Big-Bench Hard scores. While the Big-Bench Hard scores are reported on Big-Bench Hard, the total runtime is measured sep-arately on 10,000 AG_News test queries using Llama-3.1-8B on a single NVIDIA A6000 GPU. Under this setting, 10-shot ICL requires longer runtime than LoRA and SITE, mainly due to the inference overhead from repeatedly processing long demonstration-containing prompts. SITE, a recently proposed ICL-derived embedding-based method, shows runtime comparable to LoRA, whereas FV requires substantially longer runtime due to the additional search procedure for select-ing intervention heads. For methods requiring task-specific training, the reported runtime includes both training and evaluation time. Importantly, this analysis should not be inter-preted as a general ranking of adaptation paradigms. The reported scores and runtimes are based on a limited set of representative methods, a single back-bone model, selected benchmarks, and specific ex-perimental settings. Therefore, they may not fully reflect general trends across different model fami-lies, model scales, tasks, implementations, or hard-ware environments. Rather, this comparison pro-vides a limited empirical snapshot that illustrates the broader qualitative trade-offs discussed in this survey.
