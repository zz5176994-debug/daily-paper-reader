## Abstract 

We consider the stability of multi-step reasoning processes, which have extensive applications in language models, including chain-of-thought and algorithmic rea-soning. While longer sequences of reasoning can improve a model’s generation capability at test time, the errors due to intermediate reasoning steps can accumu-late in autoregressive generation, and thus grow substantially at the end. In this paper, we ask: What are the key factors determining the stability of multi-step reasoning? First, we show an inference error bound governed by the product of 

spectral norms of the Jacobians taken through the input space across generation steps. This product can be viewed as an error amplification factor, which could scale exponentially with the number of reasoning steps, serving as a quantitative measure of reasoning stability. Second, we analyze this measure in transformer models trained to predict simple tasks like linear and quadratic functions. We theoretically prove that the transformer model converges to a solution where the stability measure decays, thus yielding nearly zero inference loss over (arbitrarily) long steps. Finally, the stability analysis leads to several algorithmic implications for controlling the stability, through (i) chain-of-thought length compression that reduces the sensitivity of each step, and (ii) quantization-aware training that regular-izes the input Jacobian norms. We validate the proposed algorithms by fine-tuning language models on graph-algorithmic reasoning tasks and symbolic state-tracking tasks. Across seven evaluations, our algorithms improve over baseline comparisons by 3.5% on average, and by 8.2% for longer-length inputs. Ablation analysis validates that the stability measure is drastically reduced by 3-8 ×, confirming the regularization effect on the spectral norms of the (input space) Jacobians. 

## 1 Introduction 

Multi-step reasoning enhances language model capabilities by generating intermediate steps before producing a final answer. Specifically, for an input x, a model fW generates a reasoning sequence 

(x, ˆy(1) , . . . , ˆy(T )) autoregressively. As the error in previous outputs propagates over the steps, this can destabilize long reasoning and cause severe final prediction errors [38, 28]. However, standard training like supervised fine-tuning does not explicitly control this error accumulation. In this work, we theoretically analyze such error amplification across steps, aiming to identify the key factors governing reasoning stability and design training methods that control accumulated errors.

Understanding such error amplification is valuable in many practical problems, especially for scaling test-time computation [6, 8]. One concrete setting is algorithmic reasoning, where models perform the step-by-step computation of classical algorithms like Bellman-Ford [20]. Models are expected to learn these underlying algorithms to generalize and extrapolate to larger, unseen inputs. However, a mistake in the early steps can corrupt the procedure. In our own experiments, we observe that the test error in a fine-tuned language model (e.g., Qwen-1.5B and Gemma-2B) can scale super-linearly with steps, as shown in Figure 1a. Empirical methods, such as training models to generate fewer steps [5] or reason in the continuous feature space [10], have been proposed to reduce these errors. Yet, the reason for their empirical benefits remains underexplored. Recent theoretical work has presented convergence and generalization guarantees of transformers trained on specific reasoning tasks, including linear regression [12, 40], state tracking [14], and path-finding in trees [34]. Their analyses focus on the task-specific transformer structures, leaving open the general cases. Studies based on PAC learning theory [1, 15] yield VC-dimension generalization bounds dependent on the entire hypothesis class. This motivates us to conduct a general analysis of multi-step reasoning with computable measures on data and fine-tuned models. We begin by analyzing the inference error amplification of multi-step reasoning. Our key finding is that the inference loss is governed by the products of the spectral norms of Jacobians taken through the input space across generation steps, which control reasoning stability. The idea is to bound the gap between the T -step inference loss, ℓ(fW ([ x, ˆy(1) , . . . , ˆy(T −1) ]) , y (T )), and the training loss, ℓ(fW ([ x, y (1) , . . . , y (T −1) ]) , y (T )), conditioned on the correct steps. Motivated by this analysis, we propose a training method to control accumulated error by regularizing the Jacobian-based error amplification factor. Our method incorporates chain-of-thought length compression that downsamples the reasoning steps during training, and quantization-aware training 2[18, 19] that trains weights with gradients computed on the quantized model. We show that our method reduces the Jacobian spectral norms by regularizing the Hessian trace of the model loss during quantized training. We evaluate this method on five algorithmic reasoning tasks [20] and two state-tracking tasks from LEGO [39]. Summary of contributions. First, our analysis reveals that the inference error in multi-step reasoning scales with the product of spectral norms of Jacobians taken through the input space. Second, we theoretically show that transformers trained on linear and quadratic functions converge to a regime where these norms are strictly less than one, yielding near-zero inference error. Finally, we propose a training method that regularizes these input Jacobian norms, empirically enhancing test performance and length generalization across diverse reasoning tasks.

## 3 Theoretical Analysis 

This section presents a theoretical analysis of multi-step reasoning in the above setup. First, we present a bound on the inference loss relative to the ground truth generation. We show that this bound depends on an error amplification factor defined by the product of spectral norms of Jacobian matrices measured at the input space. Second, we instantiate this bound in in-context prediction tasks for linear and quadratic functions. We show that transformer models converge to a regime where the error amplification factor decays across steps, thus yielding nearly zero inference loss.

Comparison with prior work. Prior analyses of transformers trained on multi-step reasoning differ from ours in their attention mechanisms, tasks, and tracked quantities. Previous works have examined one-head softmax attention on state-tracking tasks to demonstrate implicit biases and length generalization [13, 14]. Huang et al. [12] study one-head linear attention on in-context linear regression, where the trained transformer implements multi-step gradient descent. Yang et al. [34] study multi-head softmax attention on tree path-finding, where different heads specialize and coordinate across subtasks. In contrast, we study multi-head non-linear attention on quadratic regression. We identify the explicit form of Jacobians taken through the inputs at each step at convergence and connect it with the stability of the inference loss. 

## 4 Proposed Methods 

Our analysis suggests two strategies to mitigate accumulated errors: reducing the sequence length to limit the error amplification factor PT −1 

> t=1

ρ(T ) 

> t

and regularizing the input Jacobian spectral norms 

∥J(t) 

> i

∥. Motivated by these insights, we design a training method combining two techniques. 

(1) Chain-of-thought length compression. First, we compress the reasoning sequence by sub-sampling intermediate steps. For a training example (x, y (1) , . . . , y (T )) and a sampling factor 

λ ∈ (0 , 1] , we uniformly sample T ′ = ⌊λ · T ⌋ intermediate steps to form a compressed sequence 

(x, y (1) , . . . , y (T ′)), and minimize the training loss over the sampled sequence. As shown in Figures 4a and 4b, models trained on subsampled sequences consistently outperform those trained on full steps. Intermediate numbers of steps achieve the lowest inference loss on the Dijkstra and Prim tasks. Notably, subsampling steps reduces the error amplification factor by 92 ×

and 181 × compared to training with all steps. Subsampling steps reduces the amplification factor but increases the per-step errors εt. Thus, the best λ is the one that balances these two effects. 

(2) Regularization through quantization-aware training. Second, we incorporate quantization during supervised fine-tuning to explicitly regularize the Jacobian spectral norms. This is inspired by observations that quantized training smooths the loss landscape and provides a regularization effect [18, 19]. ## 5 Experiments 

We evaluate our proposed method in a range of algorithmic reasoning tasks. First, our method outperforms baselines by an average of 3.5 % across five graph algorithmic and two symbolic reasoning tasks. Second, our method significantly enhances out-of-distribution length generalization, yielding an average 8.2 % improvement. Third, empirical analysis confirms that our approach reduces the error amplification factor (measured via Jacobian spectral norms) by up to 8×. Lastly, we also conduct ablation studies for various design choices of our algorithm.

## 6 Related Work 

Theoretical understanding of multi-step reasoning. Studying the training dynamics of transformers is key to understanding how multi-step reasoning improves generalization. Prior work analyzes single-head transformers trained on parity-check tasks [13, 30], and state tracking tasks [14], while [34] studies multi-head specialization on path-finding in binary trees. For the in-context weight prediction task, [12] establishes convergence for linear targets using single-head linear transformers. We extend the analysis to nonlinear multi-head transformers and quadratic functions. Complementary theoretical analyses include entropy-calibration analyses of error accumulation in autoregressive generation [4] and information-theoretic frameworks that quantify per-step information gain in multi-step reasoning [28]. Joshi et al. [15] study learning general function classes of next-token generators with CoTs in the realizable setting, showing that the sample complexity scales with the VC dimension of the hypothesis class and log( T ) of the CoT length T . Altabaa et al. [1] further improve the bound with a notion of CoT-information, which achieves faster rates than applying uniform convergence to training loss on chain-of-thought sequences. Prior work has also examined the expressiveness of transformers with chain-of-thought. Li et al. [22] show that CoT enables transformers to perform computations beyond the expressiveness limits of models without CoT. Expressiveness has been further characterized through circuit complexity under logarithmic, linear, and polynomial generation lengths [25]. Increasing CoT length has been shown to outperform parallel scaling of multiple chains on graph connectivity tasks [27]. 

Generalization of language models. Recent work derives generalization bounds for LLMs via compression and subspace training [23, 24], with tighter martingale-based extensions that explain empirical pretraining scaling laws [7]. A separate line of work derives PAC-Bayes bounds for fine-tuning that depend on the loss Hessian [17, 16]. 

Optimization methods for multi-step reasoning. Several recent works aim to improve multi-step reasoning beyond supervised fine-tuning. Yao et al. [35] frame this process as a latent-variable problem, minimizing gradient variance to efficiently allocate sampling budgets. A complementary line of work explores implicit reasoning. Implicit chain-of-thought [5] gradually removes intermediate steps to internalize computation. Continuous chain-of-thought [10] shifts reasoning into a continuous latent space. Semantic chain-of-thought [11] aligns implicit and explicit steps via contrastive learning. Empirically, Yeo et al. [36] investigate how long reasoning chains emerge in large language models, highlighting training computation and reward shaping as key factors. More broadly, multi-step reasoning traces also appear in experimental design settings [33, 32], where trajectory-level data is revealed over time. 

## 7 Conclusion 

This work provides an analysis of multi-step reasoning by bounding its inference errors via the product of input Jacobian spectral norms across generation steps. We prove that transformers trained for in-context weight prediction converge to a multi-head construction where this error amplification factor decays, achieving polynomially small test loss. We introduce a training method that explicitly controls this factor using reasoning length compression and quantization-aware training. Across seven algorithmic tasks, our approach consistently improves test accuracy and length generalization, with reduced empirical amplification factors in fine-tuned models. 10 Acknowledgement

B.2 Sample Complexity Comparison 

We compare models fine-tuned with a varied number of training examples between 1000 , 2000 , and 

5000 . We also evaluate a model fine-tuned without intermediate steps. After training, we evaluate the noise stability using the fine-tuned model. We inject isotropic Gaussian noise with standard deviation 

σ = 0 .005 into the model weights and measure the increase in the inference loss. We compute the average loss increase with 10 random seeds. We evaluate the noise stability of models fine-tuned with chain-of-thought on a range of algorithmic reasoning tasks, including DFS, Dijkstra, Prim, and DAG shortest path. First, we find that increasing the number of training samples leads to improved noise stability across all tasks. This suggests a strong correlation between generalization performance and robustness to weight noise. For the algorithms of the same task, we noted a difference in noise stability. Specifically, models fine-tuned with the Dijkstra algorithm have more than 2× lower Hessian traces than the Bellman-Ford algorithm. Additionally, models fine-tuned by Kruskal’s algorithm yield 15 × lower Hessian trace values than Prim’s. Second, we study the effect of intermediate steps on the sample complexity. We randomly subsample the intermediate steps T to 20 , 10 , 5, 2, and 0 where T = 0 indicates training without intermediate steps. We find that a larger T substantially reduces sample complexity. Using intermediate steps consistently achieves better sample complexity compared to training without intermediate steps.

B.3 Discussions 

Our formulation targets supervised fine-tuning on ground-truth steps. Reinforcement learning methods like Group Relative Policy Optimization or Proximal Policy Optimization optimize over the model’s own rollouts. Our analysis extends to reinforcement learning in two key ways. First, because test-time generation is independent of the training objective, our stability analysis applies equally to models trained with reinforcement learning. Second, our work can be applied to study whether 36 policy-gradient training implicitly regularizes input Jacobian norms along high-reward trajectories. We leave the formal extension of our bounds to the reinforcement learning setting for future work. Lastly, we discuss several limitations of this work. First, our method regularizes these Jacobian norms during training. Future work could develop test-time regularization strategies that adapt to each input example during inference. Second, for black-box models, where gradients and Jacobians are not directly accessible, proxy stability measures based on noise perturbations may provide a practical alternative. Finally, extending the Jacobian-based error accumulation analysis to broader language-model tasks, including tool use, generation, and question answering, remains an interesting direction. 37
