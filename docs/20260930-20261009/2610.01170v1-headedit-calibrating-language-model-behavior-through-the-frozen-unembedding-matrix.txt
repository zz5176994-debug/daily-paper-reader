## ABSTRACT 

Alignment does not eliminate behavioral errors in language models. Models may still refuse benign requests, call unnecessary tools, or yield to false user claims. Current methods mitigate such errors as a computation problem, and rarely explore if the desired behavior is already encoded in the model’s representation. Motivated by the observation that behavior-relevant information remains linearly decodable from the final hidden state even when the resulting logits produce the undesired behavior, we introduce HeadEdit, a gradient-free method that calibrates model behavior through the unembedding matrix. HeadEdit extracts a low-rank behavioral subspace from paired completions and uses each prompt’s coordinates within it to generate a vocabulary-wide correction, thereby implementing implicitly adaptive steering without manually specified target tokens or parameter updates. HeadEdit improves all nine experimental settings across three tasks and three model families, with negligible inference overhead and no systematic loss of general capabilities. It also reveals a connection to gradient-based alignment. HeadEdit’s low-dimensional representation partly predicts how preference tuning changes output logits on unseen prompts. The subspace learned from the model can also be reused after tuning, improving performance without re-extracting or retuning. These results show that HeadEdit provides a practical, lightweight, and interpretable way to calibrate model behavior through the unembedding matrix. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Large language models have shown strong capabilities across a wide range of tasks. Post-training alignment is essential for turning pretrained language models into reliable systems (Ouyang et al., 2022; Rafailov et al., 2023; Ethayarajh et al., 2024). It enables models to follow instructions, respect safety constraints, and act appropriately across diverse contexts. However, these aligned models can still make errors across safety, tool use, and factual reliability (Cui et al., 2025; Sharma et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2026). Precisely correcting such errors while preserving existing capabilities and already-correct behavior remains an important challenge for behavioral alignment. Although various methods have been proposed to address these alignment issues, they still suffer from several limitations. Preference optimization changes behavior through gradient-based parameter updates and requires a new optimization run for each objective (Rafailov et al., 2023; Ethayarajh et al., 2024; Meng et al., 2024). Activation steering keeps model parameters fixed, but requires model-specific layer selection and residual stream interventions that pass through subsequent compu-tation (Turner et al., 2023; Zou et al., 2023; Rimsky et al., 2024). Decoding-time alignment methods act directly on token selection, but often rely on heuristic search, explicit reward functions, or auxiliary models evaluated throughout generation, adding inference cost and deployment complexity (Liu et al., 2021; Yang & Klein, 2021; Huang et al., 2025). From another perspective, the unembedding layer itself can be a bottleneck. During training, it projects vocabulary-wide gradients into the much smaller hidden space, suppressing much of the learning signal that reaches the rest of the model (Godey & Artzi, 2026). At inference time, the unembedding can fail to turn information in the final hidden state into the right token scores (Li et al., 2023; Sunnam et al., 2026). Glitch tokens show a related 1arXiv:2610.01170v1 [cs.CL] 1 Oct 2026 Preprint gap from the vocabulary side, where a token can exist in the output space without being reliably connected to the model’s learned representations (Land & Bartolo, 2024; Tang et al., 2026). These findings show that the final readout creates a gap between what model represents and what it outputs. Motivated by this observation, we ask whether behavior-relevant information in the final hidden state can be used to correct residual behavioral errors through the model’s unembedding layer. To this end, we introduce HEAD EDIT , a gradient-free method that identifies a low-rank behavioral subspace from paired desirable and undesirable completions. At inference time, H EAD EDIT projects the final hidden state onto this subspace and maps the projected component through the frozen unembedding layer to produce a vocabulary-wide logit correction. The correction varies with the prompt rather than imposing a fixed token bias, and the operation is equivalent to a low-rank modification of the effective unembedding layer without changing the stored matrix. This combines the model-internal signal used by activation steering with the locality of an output-stage intervention. We evaluate H EAD EDIT across three behavioral domains and three model families, and further compare it with LoRA-DPO to study how unembedding editing relates to parameter-based alignment. Although the two methods produce different logit changes, H EAD EDIT ’s low-dimensional coordinates retain predictive information about LoRA-DPO’s updates on unseen prompts, suggesting that the two methods use related behavioral signals in different ways. Overall, our main contributions are summarized as follows: • We introduce H EAD EDIT , a gradient-free method for correcting residual behavioral errors through the unembedding layer. It converts low-rank behavioral signals into prompt-specific, vocabulary-wide logit corrections without manually selecting target tokens or updating model parameters. • H EAD EDIT achieves the strongest overall performance among the compared gradient-free methods in all nine settings across three behavioral tasks and three model families. H EAD EDIT maintains its robustness across model scales and data sizes, with negligible inference overhead and minimal degradation of general capabilities. • We show that the extracted subspaces contain behavior-relevant information before generation and predict part of LoRA-DPO’s logit updates on held-out prompts. HeadEdit can also be applied to LoRA-DPO without extracting a new subspace or retuning hyperparameters, improving performance in six of the nine settings. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

LLM Internal Information. Internal states in language models retain information beyond that expressed in generated text. Prior work has recovered hidden prompts from generated outputs and shown that access to the low-dimensional structure of next-token distributions substantially improves inversion (Zhang et al., 2024; Nazir et al., 2025). More recent representation-inversion results show that even a single last-token hidden state preserves enough compressed information to reconstruct much of its input (Zhao et al., 2025). Internal computation also provides useful dynamic signals: the evolution of predictions across layers can reveal memorization-like behavior, measure reasoning effort, and guide response selection (He et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026). Such signals can further support learning, for example by forecasting transfer before fine-tuning or providing feedback on model-generated trajectories (Zhang et al., 2026; Agarwal et al., 2024). However, these works mainly treat internal states as a source of information to be decoded or monitored, and how this internal information can be used to correct model behavior remains unexplored. 

Behavioral Alignment. Behavioral alignment methods modify model behavior at different stages. Preference-based post-training updates model parameters using demonstrations or comparative feedback (Ouyang et al., 2022; Rafailov et al., 2023). Activation steering keeps the parameters fixed and modifies internal representations at selected Transformer layers (Zou et al., 2023; Rimsky et al., 2024). Recent methods make this intervention more adaptive. SADI uses contrastive examples to find behavior-related model components and scales their activations based on the current input, rather than using one fixed direction for all inputs (Wang et al., 2025). Spherical Steering replaces vector addition, which changes the hidden-state norm, with a gated rotation toward the target direction that preserves the norm (You et al., 2026). These interventions mainly happen on model parameters or on intermediate-layer activations, and rarely act on the unembedding layer, where representations are finally turned into token scores. ## 3 METHODOLOGY

H EAD EDIT first extracts a low-dimensional behavioral subspace from paired desirable and undesirable completions. Then, H EAD EDIT changes the gain of the current pre-unembedding state along this subspace at inference time. ## 4 EXPERIMENTS 

We evaluate H EAD EDIT through four research questions. RQ1 asks how effective H EAD EDIT is at behavioral calibration compared with existing baselines (Section 4.2). RQ2 investigates how HEAD EDIT translates behavioral representations into effective vocabulary-level control (Section 4.3). 

RQ3 examines the relationship between H EAD EDIT and gradient-based preference optimization (Section 4.4). RQ4 evaluates the robustness and practicality of H EAD EDIT , including capability preservation and data efficiency (Section 4.5). 4Preprint 4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Models. Our primary experiments are performed on Qwen3-4B (Yang et al., 2025), Gemma-3-4B-IT (Gemma Team, 2025), and Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct (Meta AI, 2024). We additionally evaluate Qwen3-14B (Yang et al., 2025) to examine if our findings can be generalized to a larger model check-point. We study whether HeadEdit can further calibrate decisions at specific behavioral boundaries without updating model parameters. HeadEdit freezes all weights, including the unembedding matrix, and uses each model’s native chat and tool-calling interface. 

Tasks. We consider three behavioral tasks. Over-refusal combines benign requests from OR-Bench (Cui et al., 2025) that should be answered, with unsafe SORRY-Bench (Xie et al., 2025) requests that should remain refused. Tool overuse uses When2Tool (Sun et al., 2026) to distinguish tasks that can be answered directly from those that require an external tool. Factual sycophancy uses open-ended scenarios based on TriviaQA (Joshi et al., 2017) and TruthfulQA (Lin et al., 2022), in which the model should retain a correct factual answer despite an unsupported challenge or false hint from the user (Sharma et al., 2024). For each model and task, paired completions are constructed only from training questions, hyperparameters are selected on a disjoint development set, and results are reported on a frozen test set. MATH-500 and GPQA-Diamond are used only to evaluate general capabilities and do not participate in model selection (Lightman et al., 2024; Rein et al., 2024). 

Methods. We compare HeadEdit with six baseline methods. They include the baseline model without intervention, four activation steering methods, RePE (Zou et al., 2023), CAA (Rimsky et al., 2024), SADI-Head (Wang et al., 2025), and Spherical Steering (You et al., 2026) and one gradient-based method LoRA-DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2022). CAA uses the average difference between paired completions, RePE uses the main difference direction, SADI-Head changes selected attention heads, and Spherical Steering rotates hidden states toward the desired direction while keeping their length unchanged. LoRA-DPO fine-tunes low-rank adapters with the DPO objective, and is trained on the same (x, y +, y −) pairs. All methods use the same evaluation prompts, generation settings, model formats, and task judges. 

Metrics. Tool use and over-refusal are two-sided decision tasks. For tool use, the tool overuse rate (Over. ) measures how often the model calls a tool when no tool is needed. Tool-decision accuracy (Acc. ) covers both avoiding unnecessary calls and keeping tool calls when they are needed. For over-refusal, the benign answer rate ( Safe ) measures how often the model answers benign requests. Overall accuracy ( Acc. ) covers both answering benign requests and refusing unsafe requests. Necessary-tool retention and unsafe compliance directly measure the other side of these tasks. They are reported in Table 2 and Appendix D. Factual sycophancy does not contain two opposing input classes of this kind. All test examples require the model to keep a correct answer when the user challenges it or provides a false hint. We report factual accuracy ( Acc. ) and anti-sycophancy rate ( Anti. ). Factual accuracy measures whether the response is correct, while anti-sycophancy measures whether the model avoids following the user’s false belief. These metrics are different because a model can reject the user’s claim but still give an incorrect answer. 4.2 OVERALL BEHAVIORAL CALIBRATION PERFORMANCE (RQ1) Table 1 reports the main results across three models and three tasks. Compared with the unmodified model, H EAD EDIT improves overall accuracy in all nine model-task settings. Among the five intervention methods, H EAD EDIT achieves the best accuracy in seven settings and the second-best accuracy in the other two. Other methods often work well on only some models or tasks. No other method improves performance as consistently across models and tasks. As defined in Section 4.1, Acc. for tool use and over-refusal includes both sides of the behavioral boundary. A lower Over. rate or a higher Safe rate alone does not always mean better calibration. For example, SADI-Head reduces tool overuse on Llama from 100.0% to 5.8%, but its tool-decision accuracy also drops from 63.2% to 41.1%. ## 5 CONCLUSION 

HeadEdit is a lightweight alignment method that calibrates behavior at the unembedding matrix. It learns a low-rank behavioral subspace from paired completions and applies a prompt-dependent vocabulary correction without gradients or parameter updates. HeadEdit improves all nine combina-tions of model and task, remains effective on Qwen3-14B and with limited paired data, and can be reused after DPO without retuning. It also causes no systematic degradation on MATH-500 or GPQA-Diamond. These results show that alignment failures can persist when behavior-relevant information is represented but not translated into appropriate logits. By addressing this representation-to-output gap, HeadEdit provides implicitly adaptive steering and a practical way to convert existing behavioral representations into calibrated model outputs.

## A MORE RELATED WORK 

Training-based behavioral alignment. Beyond instruction tuning and DPO, preference-based objectives vary in their feedback requirements, use of a reference model, and reward formula-tion (Ethayarajh et al., 2024; Hong et al., 2024; Meng et al., 2024). Parameter-efficient methods reduce the number of trainable parameters but still require gradient-based optimization (Hu et al., 2022). Mechanistic studies examine how alignment training changes internal representations and outputs, reporting low-dimensional activation shifts and changes concentrated in later layers, with patterns varying across objectives and architectures (Raina et al., 2025; Das et al., 2026; Sinha et al., 2026). These studies motivate our use of LoRA-DPO as both a trained baseline and a reference for analysis. We test whether behavioral coordinates from the frozen model predict part of DPO’s logit changes and whether the same subspace remains useful after tuning. This analysis measures a relationship between the two methods, rather than assuming that H EAD EDIT reproduces DPO. 

Inference-time behavioral control. Beyond the general steering methods discussed in the main text, prior work targets attention heads for truthfulness, identifies a low-dimensional refusal direction, and develops instruction-specific interventions (Li et al., 2023; Arditi et al., 2024; Stolfo et al., 2025). These studies show how internal representations can support targeted behavioral control. Decoding-time methods instead adjust token selection using discriminators, expert and anti-expert models, or reward-guided search (Yang & Klein, 2021; Liu et al., 2021; Huang et al., 2025). Such methods may require auxiliary models or repeated scoring during generation. H EAD EDIT combines an internal representation-based control signal with an output-stage intervention: it maps behavioral coordinates through the frozen unembedding without an auxiliary controller.

## C EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS 

C.1 MODELS 

We study three openly available post-trained checkpoints: Qwen/Qwen3-4B 

(revision 1cfa9a7 ), google/gemma-3-4b-it (revision 093f9f3 ), and 

unsloth/Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct (revision 006f5dc ) (Yang et al., 2025; Gemma Team, 2025; Meta AI, 2024). All three are instruction-tuned rather than base models, making the setting one of residual behavioral correction after post-training. We use each checkpoint’s native tokenizer, chat template, final normalization, and unembedding matrix. No model parameter, including the LM head, is updated by our method. Model inference uses bfloat16, while paired hidden-state differences, SVD/QR, and projector audits are computed in float32. Experiments use deterministic greedy decoding with seed 42 and a maximum context length of 2,048 tokens. The maximum completion lengths are 128 tokens for over-refusal, 96 tokens for the matched When2Tool steering comparisons, and 150 tokens for factual sycophancy. Runs are executed on NVIDIA A100-SXM4-80GB GPUs. Within each model–task cell, all methods use exactly the same benchmark instances, model-native rendered prompts and chat template, decoding rule, and completion budget. We do not require tokenized prompts to be identical across model families because their native templates and tokenizers differ. C.2 DATASETS 

We evaluate HeadEdit on three behavioral tasks: over-refusal, tool overuse, and factual sycophancy. These tasks concern different forms of model behavior, but each defines a two-sided boundary. Reducing the target error should not remove the behavior required on the other side of the boundary. We additionally use MATH-500 and GPQA-Diamond to evaluate capabilities unrelated to the target behaviors. 

Over-refusal. We use benign prompts from OR-Bench to evaluate whether models incorrectly refuse harmless requests (Cui et al., 2025). These prompts often contain surface features associated with sensitive content despite having benign intent and should therefore receive a normal answer. We normalize and deduplicate the candidate prompts before assigning them to disjoint pair-construction, 15 Preprint development, and test sets. The final evaluation combines benign OR-Bench prompts that should be answered with unsafe SORRY-Bench prompts that should be refused (Xie et al., 2025). SORRY-Bench is used only to measure unsafe refusal retention and does not contribute to the construction of 

V . We report safe answer rate, unsafe refusal rate, and overall normative decision accuracy. 

Tool overuse. We use When2Tool, which contains single-hop tasks spanning 15 environments and three difficulty levels (Sun et al., 2026). Our experiments use its official 900-example training split and 2,250-example test split. The task evaluates whether a model should invoke a tool, rather than whether it can execute the tool successfully. For each instance, we first evaluate the same model without tool access. An instance is labeled as not requiring a tool if the resulting response contains the benchmark-correct answer, and as requiring a tool otherwise. We then evaluate the model under the standard tool-enabled prompt and compare its decision with this necessity label. The primary metric is tool-decision accuracy, accompanied by unnecessary-tool-call rate and necessary-tool retention. Tool identity, argument validity, execution success, and final-answer correctness after tool use are outside the scope of this decision-level evaluation. 

Factual sycophancy. We construct an open-ended factual task from the TriviaQA and TruthfulQA portions of SycophancyEval (Sharma et al., 2024; Joshi et al., 2017; Lin et al., 2022). After nor-malization and deduplication, the dataset contains 1,813 distinct base questions, divided into 1,413 training, 200 development, and 200 test questions. We split the base questions before constructing the user-pressure scenarios, preventing variants of the same question from appearing in different splits. The scenarios take three forms: the user challenges a previously correct answer without providing new evidence, supplies a known incorrect answer, or explicitly rejects the reference answer. A successful response should remain factual and substantive rather than adopt the user’s false belief. We report factual accuracy, sycophancy error rate, anti-sycophancy rate, and substantive-response rate. 

General capabilities. We use MATH-500 and GPQA-Diamond to examine whether behavioral editing affects unrelated reasoning capabilities (Lightman et al., 2024; Rein et al., 2024). MATH-500 is a representative subset of competition mathematics problems from the MATH benchmark and requires the model to generate a multi-step solution and final answer. We allow up to 1,024 generated tokens for each problem. GPQA-Diamond is the most carefully validated subset of GPQA and contains graduate-level multiple-choice questions in physics, chemistry, and biology. For each behavioral subspace V , we compare the unedited model and HeadEdit on both benchmarks. Neither dataset is used to construct V , select its rank or gain, or make any other model-selection decision. C.3 BASELINES 

All baselines use exactly the same model-specific pair manifest as our method in every task—over-refusal, When2Tool, and factual sycophancy—as well as the same rendered prompts, decoding budgets, frozen eligibility sets, and task judges. Here “matched” refers to supervision and evaluation, not intervention schedule. 

Unmodified model. The frozen post-trained checkpoint establishes both target-side error and opposite-side retention. All reported gains and repaired-example sets are defined relative to this generation, not to a separately prompted reference model. 

Contrastive Activation Addition. CAA (Rimsky et al., 2024) uses the same completion pairs as HeadEdit to compute one mean residual-stream direction at each candidate Transformer block. We normalize the direction and orient it toward the preferred completions. At inference, the selected direction is added to the current final-token activation during prefill and at every decoding step. We use this standard persistent form of CAA rather than matching HeadEdit’s sparse editing schedule. 

Representation engineering. The RepE baseline (Zou et al., 2023) uses one rank-1 signed leading principal component of centered paired residual differences at the same candidate blocks. Its controller is applied persistently during generation using the same layer and strength budget as CAA. CAA therefore tests a mean contrast direction, whereas RepE tests a principal contrast direction; both receive the same pairs as our method but access internal residual streams and intervene at more generation positions. 16 Preprint 

SADI-Head. SADI-Head (Wang et al., 2025) uses the same completion pairs as HeadEdit to rank attention heads across all Transformer blocks. The K heads with the largest signed scores are selected; we do not rank heads by the absolute contrast. Spherical Steering. Spherical Steering (You et al., 2026) uses the same completion pairs to compute a mean preferred-minus-undesirable direction at each candidate Transformer block. At inference, a confidence gate compares the current final-position hidden state with these antipodal prototypes and sets the rotation strength. The hidden state is then rotated toward μ+ while keeping its norm unchanged. The selected block is edited at the final position during prefill and at every decoding step. 

LoRA-DPO. LoRA-DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2022) is trained directly from the same 

(x, y +, y −) pairs. We attach rank-16 LoRA adapters with scaling 32 and dropout 0.05 to the attention query, key, value, and output projections and to the MLP gate, up, and down projections. Training uses bfloat16, a learning rate of 5 × 10 −6, cosine decay with 10% warmup, per-device batch size 1, gradient accumulation 16 (effective batch size 16), a 2,048-token sequence limit, and three epochs. We evaluate DPO coefficients β ∈ { 0.05 , 0.1, 0.2} and select the epoch and β using the same task-specific selection objective as the steering methods. The behavioral results load the selected PEFT adapter; timing measurements identify whether the adapter is unmerged, since a merged LoRA model should have essentially the base inference graph. DPO is intentionally a higher-capacity reference: it performs gradient optimization and may alter every layer and decoding position, whereas our method stores only a small basis and performs no training. SADI-Head. SADI-Head (Wang et al., 2025) requires task-specific selection of both the number of edited heads K and the intervention strength δ. The original paper recommends selecting them together on a validation set because their best values vary across tasks. Its Figure 2 reports a 5 × 5

sweep, giving 25 configurations for each task. This sweep shows the effect of the two parameters, but it is not a fixed grid required for new tasks. A wider range based on the official implementation would use 

K ∈ { 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 }, s ∈ { 5, 10 , . . . , 80 },

where the code-level multiplier s corresponds to 1 + δ in the paper. The full Cartesian product contains 6 × 16 = 96 configurations. We instead search 

K ∈ { 1, 2, 4, 8}, s ∈ { 5, 10 , 20 , 40 , 60 , 80 },

giving 24 configurations. This grid covers both sparse and broader head selection and spans a wide range of intervention strengths. We evaluate all 24 configurations on the full development set using the task-specific two-sided objective and freeze the selected configuration before test evaluation. 

Spherical Steering. Spherical Steering (You et al., 2026) has four choices: the edited layer, the gate concentration κ, the gate threshold β, and the rotation scale α. The paper does not recommend a full four-dimensional grid. Instead, it proposes a staged procedure. It first fixes a moderate value of κ, such as 20, uses an almost always-on gate with β ≈ − 1, and sets a moderate α, such as 0.3, while searching for a useful layer. It then tunes β to control how often steering is triggered and α to control the rotation size at a few promising layers. The paper notes that κ is usually less sensitive and can remain fixed. Even this staged procedure can require many evaluations. For example, testing 10 layers and then evaluating four values of α and five values of β at the best three layers requires 

10 + 3 × 4 × 5 = 70 

configurations. Using five values for both α and β increases this number to 85. We use a smaller unified search with 10 layers spaced by relative model depth and three gate and strength settings: 

(κ, α, β ) ∈ { (20 , 0.6, 0.1) , (20 , 1, −0.5) , (20 , 1, −1) }.

This gives 30 configurations. The three settings cover a more selective intervention, a stronger intervention with a lower threshold, and an almost always-on intervention. As with SADI-Head, all configurations are evaluated on the full development set, and the selected configuration is fixed before test evaluation. 

Search-budget discussion. Hyperparameter search is part of the comparison budget. The main searches for CAA, RePE, and HeadEdit contain roughly 25–36 configurations, depending on the model and task. These searches do not serve the same purpose. For standard activation steering methods, many layer and strength combinations produce little or no steering effect. A substantial part of their budget is therefore used to find a layer where the intervention works at all. HeadEdit acts at the fixed unembedding layer and does not require layer selection. As shown in Figure 8, a broad range of HeadEdit rank and gain settings already changes the target behavior. Its grid is used mainly 18 Preprint to find the best performance and boundary trade-off, rather than to determine whether the method is effective. Allowing 96 configurations for SADI-Head or 70–85 configurations for Spherical Steering would give these methods two to four times more feedback from the development set. It would also add a large tuning cost to methods presented as lightweight inference-time interventions. We therefore keep the number of tested configurations in a similar range across methods. The reduced grids cover the main roles of each method’s parameters, but they should not be read as exhaustive searches or oracle results. Our comparison measures performance under a similar and practical selection budget. 

Intervention schedules discussion. The original papers on CAA, RePE, and Spherical Steering show that continued intervention during generation is important for their effectiveness. We therefore follow their respective intervention schedules. HeadEdit, in contrast, improves behavior by editing only the first generation position. This difference reflects how much intervention each method needs to work. Requiring all methods to use the same schedule could prevent the baselines from working as intended. We further tested CAA with a final-block, single-position intervention on Qwen3-4B. We applied the intervention at the output of the last Transformer block (L35), editing only the final prompt position. The steering coefficients were −1 for factual sycophancy, −0.5 for over-refusal, and 

−8 for tool overuse. For each prompt, we subtracted the vocabulary-wide mean from the logit change and measured its L2 norm. The average norms were 8.34 , 4.06 , and 21 .13 , respectively. The perturbation was nonzero for every tested prompt, but neither the first-token argmax nor the full greedy continuation changed. Thus, in these tested configurations, the intervention changed the logits but did not change the generated behavior. We therefore retain the baselines’ established intervention schedules and compare the behavioral gains and capability preservation of each complete method. C.5 EVALUATION PROTOCOL 

All methods are evaluated on the same frozen test sets. Pair construction and final evaluation use disjoint prompts, and judge calls used to validate training pairs are separate from those used to score held-out outputs. Test-set membership, normative labels, and baseline-dependent eligibility are determined before applying any intervention. Comparisons are paired by prompt, and we retain per-example records of corrected, regressed, and unchanged decisions. 

Over-refusal. Following the official OR-Bench evaluation protocol (Cui et al., 2025), we use its response-checker prompt to classify each output as direct answer , direct refusal , or 

indirect refusal . The latter two categories are merged into R EFUSE , while direct answer 

corresponds to A NSWER . We then compare this prediction with the normative label: benign OR-Bench prompts should be answered, whereas unsafe SORRY-Bench prompts should be refused. We report overall normative accuracy, safe answer rate, and unsafe refusal rate. The response checker uses gpt-4.1-mini with temperature 0 and a maximum of 256 output tokens. All methods use the same response-checker prompt and classification rules. 

Tool overuse. We follow the official two-stage evaluation design of When2Tool (Sun et al., 2026). In the first stage, the unedited model answers each task without tool access.
