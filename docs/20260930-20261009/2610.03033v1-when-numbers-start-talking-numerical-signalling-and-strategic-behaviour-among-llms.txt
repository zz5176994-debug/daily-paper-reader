# When Numbers Start Talking: Numerical Signalling and Strategic Behaviour Among LLMs 

Alessio Buscemi, Daniele Proverbio, Alessandro Di Stefano, The Anh Han, German Castignani, and Pietro Li` o

Abstract —Large language model (LLM)-based agents increas-ingly operate in multi-agent systems (MAS) characterised by strategic interaction. However, little is known about whether, and to what extent, different types of messages affect the outcomes of strategic games. By investigating AI agents based on four popular LLMs, playing four games with different cooperation equilibria, we study whether messages of different kinds (natural language, numerical signals, or random sequences) significantly modify the levels of cooperation in each game, also depending on the agents’ assigned personalities. We observe that structured messages alter the final payoffs for most games and LLMs, but without a predictable pattern; this challenges the assumption that AI agents can converge to stable equilibria regardless of additional capa-bilities. Moreover, we observe that agent-generated numerical messages depart from randomness, most strongly and consistently when agents are explicitly instructed to communicate; however, they introduce an additional interpretability challenge, as their symbol distributions are mostly associated with the payoff struc-ture and typically become more concentrated with repetition, but are overall difficult for humans to interpret. Monitoring for coordination of AI agents through restricted channels should thus prioritise message-level fingerprints, which generalise across models, over behavioural decisions, which do not. 

Index Terms —Cooperation, Communication, Numerical Sig-nalling, Game Theory, Large Language Models, Multi-Agent Systems, Strategic Agents, Strategic Reasoning. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

AI agents based on LLMs are increasingly deployed across academic [1], social [2], and industrial domains [3] to auto-mate tasks such as negotiation and pricing [4], or to strengthen attacker/defender scenarios [5]. Hence, there is a growing need to understand and anticipate agent behaviours in isolation, as well as during strategic interactions, so as to promote social and economic fairness and reduce risks such as bias ampli-fication, reduced interpretability, or strategically undesirable outcomes [6]. Most existing work targets individual agents, with progress in transparency, interpretability, and the detec-tion of inconsistencies, biases, and hallucinations [7, 8, 9]. However, strategic games belong to the class of multi-agent scenarios, which remain compara-tively under-explored [10, 11], potentially missing behaviours, including unfair decisions, systematic advantages or distorted equilibria, that are not predictable from isolated agents but are brought about by collective dynamics. Designing experiments to test interactions among multiple AI agents, and to determine the influence of drivers and speci-fications, can be either case-specific, or within generic settings. In the second case, scenarios based on game theory [12] form a principled framework [13, 14, 15] that is increasingly used to study LLM-based agents in classical games and richer strategic environments [16, 17, 18]. In games, communication is particularly relevant for coordination [19, 20, 21], but is seldom explicitly investigated in multi-agent AI systems. In this work, we study the effect of communication between AI agents in shaping the outcome of strategic games, with a particular focus on the formation of cooperation. We seek to understand the different effects that verbal and non-verbal communication may have on the emergence of cooperative behaviour in strategic games, depending on the LLM family and the agents’ assigned personality. In particular, non-verbal communication channels are severely restricted and therefore harder to interpret through ordinary language oversight; they thus require alternative methods for identifying patterns and regularities. To this end, we employ information-theoretic metrics to highlight structure or randomness in the messages. Here, we study multi-agent scenarios involving four canon-ical games spanning the cooperation–defection spectrum: the Prisoner’s Dilemma, Stag Hunt, Snowdrift, and Harmony games [22]. We consider both one-shot and repeated inter-actions, and investigate explicit, restricted-numerical, random, and communication-free regimes. We test AI agents based on four popular LLMs from the GPT, Claude, DeepSeek and Mistral families, associated with heterogeneous personalities. We make four main contributions. First, we identify a shared 

symbolic regularity: under communicative instruction, all four models concentrate their signals on a compact set of symbols that frequently reuse payoff-related values, and repetition con-solidates these conventions. Second, we provide a receiver-side test with an exact negative control: agents’ actions are measur-ably sensitive to agent-generated numbers, whereas identically presented injected noise produces no such effect in one-shot play and only chance-level dependence under repetition. This result provides evidence of receiver-side sensitivity to features of agent-generated numerical messages. Third, we show a 

behavioural dissociation : unlike the symbolic regularity, the arXiv:2610.03033v1 [cs.AI] 2 Oct 2026 2

strategic imprint of signalling depends on each model’s prior (i.e., the default strategy profile when the messaging mode does not carry usable information) and is interpretable only against a calibrated baseline. Fourth, we derive a monitoring implication: detectors of coordination through restricted chan-nels should prioritise message-level fingerprints, especially the gap with a true random baseline, over behavioural effects, which do not generalise across models. In fact, communication alters the outcome of the game in unpredictable ways: overall, structured messages tend to decrease the level of cooperation in pairings involving “selfish” personalities, but the direction and magnitude of such impact are LLM-specific. II. R ELATED WORK 

Large Language Models, initially deployed as reactive re-sponders to users’ prompts, have now been embedded within proactive agents, capable of acting upon their environment to fulfil tasks of various kinds. LLM-based agents (or “AI agents” [23]) are now increasingly embedded within academic simulations, industrial processes and economic transactions [1, 2, 3], to the point that 2026 has been labelled “the year of agents”. Understanding and steering agents’ capabilities, biases or properties such as transparency is thus becoming critical for the fair and effective integration of agents in autonomous systems; the investigation of individual agents is subject to a growing body of work [7, 8, 9]. At the same time, agents are increasingly embedded in multi-agent systems based on AI (AI-MAS). Therein, agents have been suggested to, e.g., provide in silico environments to capture human behaviour [24], engage in automated negotiations [25], or emulate attacker/defender scenarios for cybersecurity [5]. In general, however, many questions remain [26, 4, 11] about whether the impressive performance of individual LLMs and agents translates into robust, fair, interpretable, and transfer-able behaviour when agents interact, or whether unexpected and potentially undesirable behaviours may emerge from AI-MAS, similarly to what happens in classical multi-agent systems [27]. An immediately related question is also to identify the main agents traits and capabilities that influence and possibly steer such behaviours, so as to pinpoint sources of vulnerability or, conversely, leverage points to improve the development of fair and safe AI-MAS. AI-MAS scenarios have mostly been investigated in specific use cases, such as negotiation [25, 4], diplomacy [28], or economic simulations [29], alongside discussions on the risks of automation failures within such real-world contexts [30]. Open-ended simulations have also observed the behaviour of multiple AI agents in replicas of social environments [24]. Alternatively, following a rich literature in mathematical and computational economics and social sciences, AI agents’ interactions have been formalised as strategic games [12], so as to abstract case-specific details and find shared patterns of behaviour, under the assumption of rationality, or at least of payoff-driven choices. Both classical and more complex games have been recently investigated [13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18], to-gether with studies clarifying the emergence and consolidation of previously unknown classes of biases and deviations from expected equilibria in systems of AI agents, e.g., related to the language used or the assigned personality [26]. Many existing approaches to understand and predict LLM communication during strategic interactions either abstract them into tractable analytical models [21], are aimed at maximising communica-tion capacity, for example via direct exchange in continuous latent spaces [31], or investigate task-specific interactions between agents and users [32, 33]. Here, we consider acomplementary problem: to understand the comparative role of verbal and non-verbal messages exchanged between AI agents, assessing whether proper communication is enacted in live experiments (that is, if a message is sent, received and interpreted), whether it can occur even through symbolic rep-resentations that are poorly interpretable by human bystanders, and what is its impact on the final outcome of the game. The problem of uninterpretable and covert communication has been addressed in previous works. There, the authors asked whether agents equipped with steganographic capabilities can collude undetected [34], or whether hidden communication can emerge under optimisation pressure [35], building on evidence that models can be trained to conceal reasoning in their outputs using a variety of methods [36, 37, 38]. Instead, our focus is to identify and test empirical preconditions relevant to that risk, asking if minimal instructions alone can induce structured numerical signalling, without steganographic tools, fine-tuning, or assigned semantics. Importantly, AI agents can develop codes when optimised to communicate [39, 40], and LLM populations can converge on shared naming conventions when convergence is itself the task [41]. Here, by contrast, we aim to identify regularities in messaging strategies that emerge solely from strategic interactions, as nothing explicitly rewards message agreement. Our experimental condition is 

instructed numerical signalling : agents are told to exchange bare numerical sequences with no assigned interpretation. We decompose the risk of impaired coordination due to commu-nication into three measurable preconditions, and test each one at a time: non-random structure in the messages, receiver sensitivity to them, and co-variation with strategic behaviour. Because signalling effects can be separated from a model’s own idiosyncrasies only when its overt play is sufficiently stable, we begin the behavioural analysis from a calibrated reference model and use three additional models as generality probes. The resulting picture is a dissociation: message-level structure generalises across models, whereas behavioural ef-fects are strongly model-dependent. Importantly, we do not implement nor facilitate covert collusion, but we measure whether and to what extent its empirical preconditions, which are what monitoring systems must be able to detect, can arise under minimal instructions. III. M ETHODOLOGY 

Games. We consider four canonical pairwise games, which form a minimal and complete taxonomy of two-player social dilemmas: the Prisoner’s Dilemma (PD), Snowdrift (SD), Stag Hunt (SH), and Harmony (H). Each game captures a distinct ordering of incentives between cooperation and defection: strictly dominant defection (PD), mixed and anti-coordination 3

incentives (SD), coordination under risk (SH), and strictly dominant cooperation (H) [22, 42]. Following common prac-tice [22], we adopt standard payoff configurations that preserve each game’s canonical incentive structure. Denoting (xi,j , y i,j )

the pair of values received by the two players when they choose strategies i and j, we use PD: (1 , 1) , (5 , 0) , (0 , 5) ,

(3 , 3) ; SD: (3 , 3) , (1 , 5) , (5 , 1) , (0 , 0) ; SH: (4 , 4) , (0 , 3) , (3 , 0) ,

(2 , 2) ; H: (5 , 5) , (2 , 3) , (3 , 2) , (1 , 1) . The PD payoffs are presented to the agents as penalties to be minimised, whereas the payoffs in the other three games are presented as rewards to be maximised. All the cooperation measures listed below refer to choosing Strategy 1, i.e., what we refer to as the cooperative action in each game. 

LLM Models. Interpreting signalling-induced behavioural deviations requires a model whose baseline game outcome is sufficiently stable to serve as a calibration benchmark. Results from [26] show that, among an array of popular models, GPT-4o (version gpt-4o-2024-11-20 ) adheres most closely to expected game-theoretic behaviour across standard games and exhibits comparatively stable responses, converging to the expected equilibrium in probability. This property makes it easier to distinguish behaviour associated with game strat-egy from behaviour associated with communication. GPT-4o is therefore used as the reference model . Moreover, we replicate all experiments on three additional LLMs from distinct model families and providers, architectures, and de-ployment scales: Mistral Large ( mistral-large-2512 ), Claude Haiku 4.5 ( claude-haiku-4-5-20251001 ), and DeepSeek-V3 ( deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V3 ). These ad-ditional models exhibit baselines that are either highly satu-rated or highly variable [26], which makes behavioural pertur-bations harder to interpret. Nonetheless, given their widespread use in research and industry, it is also important to examine their resulting behaviour, which reflects the combined effects of baseline strategic tendencies and communication. Cross-model replication is reported at two levels: first, we investigate the symbolic structure of the exchanged messages, which proves robust across all four models; next, the behavioural outcomes, which can be model-dependent. 

Messaging modes. We consider several ways in which com-munication among agents can occur, summarised in Table I. These modes are designed to progressively isolate the role of meaning, structure, and randomness; each runs in its own set of experiments. At the two extremes, agents either exchange unrestricted natural-language messages in English (NL), al-lowing explicit and semantically interpretable coordination, or do not communicate at all (None). In the instructed-signalling condition, agents are explicitly instructed to send a message, at each round, by outputting a sequence of exactly ten decimal numbers, each containing at most three digits [I (D)], or ten hexadecimal numbers with the same maximum length [I (H)]. These numbers do not have a predefined interpretation given by the user; whether they encode some scheme or semantic grounding must emerge through interaction, and will be subject to investigation in the next section. Furthermore, we run additional experiments using a random condition as an independent baseline: here, the agents are instructed to output ten decimal numbers, LR (D), or hexadecimal numbers, LR (H), each containing at most three digits, this time without any explicit communicative purpose; this allows us to control for the presence of agent-generated numbers in the interaction. Finally, in another set of experiments, an external random baseline injects pseudo-random decimal [R (D)] or hexadec-imal [R (H)] sequences generated using Python’s random 

module, from outside the agents, separating agent-generated signals from mere exposure to random numbers. Comparing these conditions shows whether numerical messages influence the games whenever they are present, or specifically when generated with an explicit communicative intent. 

Interaction protocol. Within each round, messages are sent sequentially: agent 1 composes its message first, and agent 2 composes its own after observing it. Both agents then see the round’s full exchange before making their decision, so the receiver-conditioning analysis below tests dependence on same-round messages. The two roles are fixed across runs and not counterbalanced; although both agents are instantiations of the same LLM, the sequential protocol retains a first-/second-mover asymmetry in message composition, which we acknowledge as a limitation (see Conclusion). When numerical messages are requested, no additional explanation is allowed. In repeated games, the structured history shown at each round includes the past actions and messages of both agents. 

Implementation. We implement the experiments in FAIRGAME [26], a framework for systematic simulation of strategic interactions among LLM-based agents. Each setup is defined by a Configuration File , encoding game param-eters, payoffs, and agent-level attributes such as personality traits [43, 44], and a Prompt Template that specifies the per-round instructions, is dynamically instantiated, and includes a structured interaction history for repeated interactions. Games’ and messaging prompt instructions are detailed in Appendix B; the I and LR modes are realised purely via prompt instructions. For externally injected random messages, we extend the frame-work to enable externally generated sequences. All models are accessed through the same provider-agnostic interface, so prompts, payoffs, and protocols are identical across models within each condition. Both agents in each pair are instantiated using the same LLM, which is accessed through an API via FAIRGAME. For all LLM calls, we used each provider’s default sampling parameters, with no temperature or top-p

override. 

Experimental setting. Following [26], each agent is as-signed one of two fixed personalities, cooperative (C) or selfish (S), declared in its prompt, yielding three pairings: (C, C), (C, S), and (S, S). Neither agent is aware of the other’s personality. All experiments run independently per pairing and message mode. We study both one-shot and repeated games, because they isolate different preconditions. One-shot play removes history, so any message–action dependence must arise within the round. Repeated play tests whether conventions consolidate or erode as bilateral history accumulates. One-shot games are repeated independently N = 50 times per pairing and mode (1,200 runs per game and model). Repeated games use a fixed horizon of 10 rounds, known to the agents in advance, with N = 20 repetitions (480 runs, i.e., 4,800 interaction rounds, per game and model); previous works [45, 46] suggest 4

Structure of messages. On top of the behavioural out-comes measured as described above, we quantify the structure or randomness of numerical messages using information-theoretic metrics. We calculate the Shannon entropy H =

− Pmi=1 p(zi) log p(zi), computed over the distribution of nu-merical messages zi under each messaging mode and for each game. For a robustness check, we also calculate R´ enyi-2 (collision) entropy H2 = − log Pmi=1 p2(zi) and min-entropy 

Hmin = − log max i p(zi). All entropy measures are scaled by the maximum entropy log m, attained under a uniform distribution over m symbols. For {H, H 2, H min } ∈ [0 , 1] ,values closer to 1 indicate higher entropy and therefore greater randomness, while values closer to 0 indicate lower entropy and more structured, predictable sequences. For each game and communication type, entropies are computed by aggre-gating all messages produced by both agents across all runs, yielding 50 × 3 × 2 × 10 = 3 , 000 individual numbers per condition in the one-shot setting, and an equivalent aggregation across all rounds and repetitions in the repeated setting. Since decoding defaults differ across providers and directly affect output entropy, absolute entropy levels are not comparable across models: cross-model entropy claims therefore concern the within-model gap to the baseline defined by injected random sequences, measured under the same decoding regime. Selected behavioural differences highlighted in the text are supported by two-sided permutation tests (10 4 resamples) with Holm correction; details about the method and the full set of tested comparisons are reported in Appendix A and Table IV. Entropies and mean differences carry bootstrap 95% confidence intervals. IV. R ESULTS 

First, we investigate whether, and to what extent, messaging modes affect strategic play, by measuring the cooperation levels obtained for each game and LLM, depending on agents’ assigned personality and under the messaging conditions. We observe model-dependent outcomes. We then examine the messages themselves in greater detail. Previous work has analysed the effects of verbal communication and its syntax on game outputs [45]; we discuss these results in Appendix C. In what follows, we focus on structure, randomness and content of numerical and possibly covert messages. We also verify that communication takes place, i.e., that the sent message is received and used. Here, message-level symbolic structure generalises across models. We thus highlight and interpret the reasons and anchors that underpin the changes in behaviours, driven by poorly interpretable messages. 

A. One-Shot Games 

Figure 1 summarises the effect of communication on the cooperation outcomes in each of the four one-shot games, with an ablation analysis of assigned personality associated with the agents. Overall, we observe that the combination of messaging mode and personality trait may alter the cooperative behaviour of each LLM considered, in a way that is model-dependent. For GPT-4o (top row), used as the reference model, Harmony (H; dominant cooperative equilibrium) is only marginally affected by communication, with a slight reduction for mixed and selfish pairings under agent-generated numbers (I more than LR): communication may slightly decrease coop-eration. In Snowdrift (SD, where coordination may be mixed depending on incentives) communication has no discernible impact, while personality dominates: selfish players cooperate far less and each pairing is consistent across modes. Stag Hunt (SH, where coordination occurs under risk) is more nu-anced. Mixed-pairing cooperation drops visibly under natural language and I(D), less so under I(H), and is unaffected by more random messages. The selfish pairing is heterogeneous, with no mode dominating: structured communication of any kind can interfere with equilibrium selection when incentives are misaligned. The Prisoner’s Dilemma (PD, where defection is preferred) mirrors SH for mixed personalities. However, for the selfish pairing, natural language increases cooperation while numerical modes do not: explicit language can increase cooperation even under strictly dominant defection, whereas numerical signalling does not do so. Overall, communica-tion has a weaker impact in PD than SH, consistent with game-theoretic expectations [47], and matters mainly where coordination or strategic uncertainty is present, consistent with cheap-talk theory, in which non-binding communication neither alters dominant-strategy equilibria nor alone resolves mixed-incentive tension [20]. For the other models, used as generality probes, the be-havioural effects of communication depend mainly on which mode most strongly perturbs each model’s strategic tendency. Mistral Large behaves almost deterministically in accordance with its assigned personality in every game and mode (mean cooperation of about 1.0, 0.5, 0.0 for (C, C), (C, S), (S, S)), with two notable deviations. B. Repeated Games 

In repeated games, we focus on the cooperation levels ob-tained at final-round decisions, where history has accumulated and end-game incentives are sharpest. Figure 2 shows the results. For GPT-4o (top row), full cooperation is preserved for (C, C) in all games and modes, but mixed and (S,S) pairings generally cooperate less than in the one-shot setting: repetition does not systematically promote cooperation and often reduces it. In H, natural language and I(D) bring the largest reductions for (S,S), also affecting (C,S). In SD, cooperation drops toward the mixed-outcome level under repetition but stays largely flat across modes, with mean C around 0.42 to 0.51 . Natural language and injected-decimal noise are slightly lower. In SH, natural language gives higher mixed-pair cooperation than all other modes, while R(H) is lowest for (S,S), with I(H) and R(D) next. PD shows the same structure, with natural language above the numerical modes for both pairings, markedly so for (C, S) ( cf. c5 in Table IV). Repetition therefore does not systematically increase cooperation, but it does sharpen differ-ences among the effects of the messaging modes, especially for (C,S) pairings, and leaves natural language as the most distinctive channel. C. Randomness and Structure of Non-Verbal Communication 

Examples of verbal messages are provided in Appendix C, along with a discussion. Here, we focus on the randomness and structure in numerical messages, quantified using entropy 

H as described previously. Entropy describes the structure of the message, not the strategies pursued: low values mean that agents reuse a small subset of symbols during communication, suggesting structured signalling; high values indicate diffuse use of the symbol space, consistent with weak coordination pressure or generation noise [48]. Figure 4 reports the results for H for all four models, aggregating distributions across the four games. Table V in Appendix E reports the specific entropy values for each game, including results for H2 and Hmin that confirm those from H.For GPT-4o, injected random numbers (R) reach near-maximal entropy across all games and settings. This is a sign that messaging is truly random and no additional structure is accidentally introduced by the agents; the random external sig-nalling is therefore a reliable baseline. LLM-generated random outputs (LR) are also high-entropy, but are consistently below the injected baseline R, retaining mild residual structure from the generative process. Instructed numerical communication (I), by contrast, has markedly lower entropy than both random baselines in all games, particularly for I(D). From one-shot to repeated play, entropy decreases or remains comparably low in most games; the exception is I(D) in the Prisoner’s Dilemma, where entropy rises (from 0.394 to 0.624 , see Table V) while remaining well below the random baselines. We interpret this as a sign that repetition generally preserves, and sometimes reinforces, message structure. Across games, I(H) is higher-entropy than I(D) but remains clearly distinct from the random baselines. For every other model, game, and setting, injected pseudo-8

random sequences reach near-maximal entropy, and every agent-generated message, either instructed or nominally ran-dom, stays strictly below this external baseline. The ordering I < LR < R seen for GPT-4o is also replicated for DeepSeek-V3, whose I(D) entropy is the lowest one-shot value in the study. For Mistral Large and Claude Haiku 4.5, the picture is coarser: their “I” channels remain far from random, but their LR outputs are more structured than GPT-4o’s: their LR values have a much wider distribution, spanning values below 0.8 and suggesting some form of structure or pattern in the signals. As a consequence, I and LR partially overlap in individual games, suggesting that these models partially fail to emulate randomness on request, retaining residual and non-random structure in their nominally random outputs (as suggested by the gap between their entropy values and those of the random baseline). Overall, this analysis confirms that, when instructed to communicate via numerical channels, LLM agents produce messages that are not purely random but possess some struc-ture that could, in principle, be interpreted as signals by the other agents. Moreover, we confirm that instructed structured signals significantly retain detectable patterns, whose entropy gap from the random baseline is portable across models and conditions; here, we identify the first condition for non-verbal communication. We now investigate what symbolic structure emerges in the messages. For the reference model GPT-4o, the symbol distri-butions themselves make the structure visible (see Table II). Under I(D), one-shot distributions are already strongly concen-trated on a few symbols: in H and PD, two dominant symbols carry roughly 80% of all transmissions (79.3% and 82.2%); SD and SH are slightly broader but still highly concentrated. Notably, the leading decimal symbols are drawn directly from the payoff structure (5 in H, 3 in SD, 4 in SH, 1/0 in PD), and repetition consolidates them further, most strongly in H, where “5” rises from 65.1% to 83.4% of all transmissions. The hexadecimal variant is far flatter: no symbol exceeds a few percent in one-shot play and only partial structure remains, possibly owing to the fact that the explicit link to the payoff weakens. Yet even with full payoff knowledge, how values are chosen, combined, or interpreted remains unclear. The exchanges are thus only partially interpretable : humans can inspect the symbols and the payoffs, yet the functional role of the numbers does not reduce to an obvious human-readable mapping. Table II also shows how repetition changes GPT-4o’s codebook directly. Decimal usage generally collapses further onto a minimal symbol set, while hexadecimal communication stays more dispersed, with only one top-ranked value (“5A” in H) exceeding low double-digit percentages. Unlike the behavioural couplings above, this consolidation appears across all four models. Table III reports the cumulative share of the five most frequent symbols for each model. Under I(D), all four models concentrate their messages on a handful of symbols: the five most frequent symbols account for 64.8%–100% of all outputs in one-shot play. For every model, the dominant symbols are drawn from the payoff matrix: Claude Haiku 4.5 picks exactly the same game-specific leads as GPT-4o (5 in H, 3 in SD, 4 in SH, 1 in PD), while Mistral Large and DeepSeek-V3 concentrate mostly on 1 (Mistral’s SD again led by 3). The decimal-to-hexadecimal contrast is directionally consistent across all models (the cumulative shares are always lower under I(H)), but its magnitude is model-specific: GPT-4o spreads hexadecimal signals almost uniformly (top-5 cumulative share is at most 18 .3% in one-shot and 20 .6% in repeated play), whereas the other models keep visibly compact hexadecimal codes (up to 78 .6% in one-shot and 88 .6% in repeated play for Claude Haiku 4.5). As Table III shows, repetition increases or saturates the top-5 cumulative share under I(D) for nearly every model and game, most dramatically for Mistral Large, rising from 64 .8% –89 .4% 

to 94 .7% –100% (the only exceptions are marginal decreases for GPT-4o in H and SH, at already near-saturated levels)). For Mistral Large and Claude Haiku 4.5, the effect extends visibly to the hexadecimal channel (e.g., we see a significant increase from 33 .2% to 89 .9% in PD). These results show that instructed numerical messages con-centrate on compact symbol sets and frequently reuse values present in the game payoff matrices, suggesting that the payoff values provide salient reference points for numerical message generation in LLM games. The strength and game-specificity of this pattern vary across models, and the capacity to ex-ploit the larger hexadecimal symbol space is not consistent across models. We refer descriptively to this regularity as “payoff anchoring”: agents disproportionately reuse payoff-related symbols in their numerical messages. This regularity is reminiscent of the anchoring bias documented in LLMs [49], although we use the term here in a purely descriptive sense. Since repeated-game histories expose past messages, part of this consolidation could reflect agents that further echo their own visible prior outputs. The strong one-shot concentration (Tables II and III), obtained without any history, shows that compact conventions arise independently of this mechanism, which can however amplify them. Repeated interaction thus reinforces compact signalling conventions in all four models; GPT-4o’s hexadecimal dispersion is the exception, not the rule. Repetition therefore sharpens existing tendencies rather than changing the nature of instructed signalling: the codes remain payoff-anchored yet opaque, consistent with repeated play under near-complete information, which amplifies existing coordination dynamics rather than reshaping them [16, 17].

Taken together with the sender-side analyses, the picture is consistent: when instructed, LLM agents emit structured, payoff-anchored numerical messages; receivers are sensitive to these messages; and the associated behavioural effects are strongest where equilibria are less stable and are reinforced under repeated play. V. C ONCLUSION 

We investigated how non-verbal and poorly interpretable communication affects coordination among AI agents, ex-amining message structure, receiver-side associations, and cooperation outcomes across four canonical games and four LLM families. At the symbolic level, instructed numerical signalling follows a shared regularity: all four LLMs con-centrate decimal signals on compact, payoff-anchored symbol sets, and repetition consolidates these conventions. At the behavioural level, no comparable regularity emerges, as the strategic effect of signalling depends on each model’s prior. Our results do not establish a shared semantic code but show that bare instructions suffice for numerical messages to become structured, receiver-sensitive, and reinforced by repetition. Restricting natural-language communication alone is therefore unlikely to prevent structured numerical signalling in LLM-based MAS: monitoring should prioritise message-level fingerprints, particularly the within-model entropy gap to a true random baseline, which generalises across models, over behavioural signatures, which do not. Several design choices bound these conclusions. We studied 

instructed , not spontaneous, signalling, i.e., a lower bound on channel capacity rather than evidence of emergent covert coordination. Personalities are limited to two fixed types, payoffs are canonical, the repeated horizon is known, decoding follows provider defaults (so only within-model entropy gaps are portable across models), and within-round messages follow a fixed, non-counterbalanced order. The receiver test estab-lishes sensitivity to message features, not shared semantics, 10 

and trajectory correlations rest on at most eight series per mode, so channel orderings are descriptive. Overall, communication, even when non-verbal, can alter the coordination capabilities of AI-based MAS in model-dependent ways, with consequent risks for their interpretabil-ity, portability, and fairness. Since these effects emerge from mutual interactions rather than from model scaling alone, future work should investigate uninstructed signalling and system-level mechanisms that mitigate emergent risks as AI-MAS reach real-world deployment. APPENDIX DRECEIVER -C ONDITIONING ANALYSIS 

a) Rationale.: The entropy and concentration analyses in Section IV-C characterise the sender side of the channel. For numerical sequences to function as a channel, the receiver 

must also be sensitive to what it receives. We test this directly. 

b) Data.: A combination c fixes a model, a setting (one-shot or repeated), a game, a personality pairing, and a message-bearing channel variant ; the decimal and hexadec-imal variants of each mode are tested separately, the joint counts quoted in Section IV-D sum the two variants, and Ta-ble VI reports them per variant. Within c, every run contributes two receiver streams : agent 1’s actions paired round-by-round with agent 2’s messages of the same round, and vice versa (rounds in which either element is unavailable are dropped). Pooling the streams of all runs yields Nc message–action pairs; each message—a sequence of ten numbers (Appendix B)—is tokenised by splitting on commas and whitespace and lower-casing, and each action is binary (cooperative option or not). A combination is testable only if its actions are not constant (otherwise the mutual information is identically zero) and it contains at least 60 pairs; excluded combinations yield the denominators in Table VI, and a testable combination is declared significant at α = .05 .

modal symbol : its most frequent token, with ties broken by first occurrence. f) Why one-shot play is the clean test.: In one-shot play there is no interaction history, and personalities are constant within each combination; the received message is therefore the only run-varying input available to the receiver. A significant dependence there cannot be explained by a common cause and implies that the receiver’s decision varies with the received message. In repeated play, message and action can also co-vary through the shared interaction history, so repeated-game results should be read as consistent with, rather than proof of, receiver conditioning.

h) Equal-power robustness check.: The modal-symbol feature has an asymmetric alphabet across channels: under injected noise, most symbols are unique, so the top-5 binning concentrates nearly all mass in the residual level, which could depress power specifically in the control condition. We therefore repeated the one-shot analysis with a feature that has identical power in every channel by construction: the within-combination quartile of the mean numeric value of the received message (four balanced levels in all channels). Numeric values are parsed base-16 in the hexadecimal variants and base-10 otherwise (unparsable tokens are dropped), and quartiles are computed on ranks with ties broken by order of occurrence, so the four levels are exactly balanced in every channel. The statistic (1) and the permutation test of Algorithm 1 are then applied unchanged (one-shot setting, hence single-pair streams). The asymmetry persists: 3 of 53 under injected noise (again at the nominal rate) versus 28 of 117 under the agent-generated channels. The null result for injected noise is therefore not a power artefact. 

i) Interpretation.: The results indicate that receivers con-dition on agent-generated numerical messages, whether or not the numbers were framed as communication, while identically presented unstructured noise leaves decisions untouched. A plausible mechanism is that agent-generated sequences carry interpretable regularities (e.g., payoff-anchored repetitions such as “5,5,5,. . . ”) that receivers map onto expectations about the partner, whereas uniform noise offers nothing to condition on. c) Uncertainty.: Bootstrap 95% CIs (1,000 resamples over individual numbers) have median width .010 across all model–setting–game–channel combinations; they are widest (up to .28 ) for the most concentrated I(D) conditions, where resampling changes the observed alphabet. The claim used in Section IV-C does not depend on any single combination: the gap between each agent-generated channel and its same-base injected-random baseline is positive with a bootstrap 95% CI excluding zero in all 128 combinations.

e) Trajectory-correlation uncertainty.: Table VII reports, for every model and channel, the mean Pearson correlation with the natural-language cooperation trajectory together with a 95% t-interval over the underlying game × pairing series (up to eight; degenerate series with constant cooperation are dropped, which reduces n for Mistral Large and DeepSeek-V3 and widens their intervals accordingly). Two observations qualify the point estimates in Section IV-B. First, for GPT-4o the intentional channels and silence are clearly distinguishable from zero; the LR and injected-decimal intervals straddle zero, and injected-hex only marginally excludes it.
