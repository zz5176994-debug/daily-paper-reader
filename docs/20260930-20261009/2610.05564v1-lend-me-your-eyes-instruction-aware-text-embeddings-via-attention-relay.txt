## ABSTRACT 

Text embedding models trained with contrastive learning learn to follow task instructions from instruction-paired data, while instruction-tuned LLMs already know how to follow them. We show that this instruction-following ability can carry over from an LLM to a Transformer-based embedder without any training. We propose Attention Relay, which passes the attention weights an LLM pro-duces to the embedder’s own attention. Across six instruction-tuned LLMs from the Qwen3, Llama 3.1 and OLMo 3 families and ten widely used embedding mod-els that differ in tokenizer, size and pooling type, Attention Relay makes nearly every combination instruction-aware. Experiments that break the method down into its parts show that the LLM’s attention weights track the instruction in its later layers and come largely from instruction tuning. They also show that relay-ing these weights selects which content in the text matters: it makes the aspect of the text that the instruction asks about dominant in the embedding, or restores that aspect where averaging had diluted it. ## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Text embeddings are central to search, retrieval-augmented generation, clustering and classifica-tion (Muennighoff et al., 2023), and the success of Large Language Models (LLMs) has made them stronger: the best embedding models are now built on LLM backbones (Wang et al., 2024; BehnamGhader et al., 2024; Muennighoff et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025c). What makes two texts similar, however, can depend on the task: news articles can be grouped by topic or by where they happened, customer messages by how the customer feels or by what the customer needs. We call any model or readout that turns a text into one vector by pooling its token states an embedder ; trained embedding models are the most common kind. An embedder trained without instructions cannot follow one, even when the instruction is placed in its input. Most recent embedders are therefore trained with instructions: they take an instruction as part of their input and learn to use it by contrastive training on texts paired with instructions, from InstructOR (Su et al., 2023) to Qwen3-Embedding (Zhang et al., 2025c), which takes curated instruction data and a round of training for each embedder, to teach what the LLMs they are built on already do (Wei et al., 2022; Ouyang et al., 2022). An instruction-tuned LLM follows such instructions readily, but it is no substitute for an embedder. Its hidden states were never shaped for similarity, and embeddings read from them without training, such as PromptEOL (Jiang et al., 2024), Echo embeddings (Springer et al., 2025) and Value Ag-gregation (Zhang et al., 2026), remain well behind trained embedders. Nor does the LLM’s grasp of an instruction obviously carry over to an embedder, which differs from it in size, tokenizer and representation space. We show that one part of an instruction-tuned LLM’s instruction following does carry over, without any training: where the LLM reads a text when it is about to answer, the attention it pays to the text’s tokens, which we call its reading weights (Figure 1). We propose Attention Relay , which hands these weights to the pooling of an instruction-agnostic embedder. ## 2 MOTIVATION 

In this section, we show that an instruction-agnostic embedder holds what an instruction asks about but reads every text the same way, while an instruction-tuned LLM reads a text differently as the question changes. 2.1 AN EMBEDDER HOLDS WHAT AN INSTRUCTION ASKS ABOUT BUT CANNOT WEIGHT IT 

An embedding is a weighted average over a text’s tokens. Mean pooling gives every token the same weight, and CLS or last-token pooling lets one token attend to the others with attention the embedder learned for similarity in general. In an embedder trained without instructions, nothing in these weights responds to a question, and a question placed in the input is only more text. Asked whether a customer message is closer to one that shares its emotion or one that shares its intent, such an embedder follows the question in about half of the cases, as often as one that ignores it, while Qwen3-Embedding, trained with instructions, follows it in nearly all (Figure 2). A news article about a business deal in Japan belongs with business news when articles are grouped by topic and with news from Japan when they are grouped by country, yet such an embedder gives it one vector for both, although generic embeddings do encode what an instruction asks about (GSTransform; Feng et al., 2025). The embedder holds what an instruction asks about but has no way to weight it by the instruction. 2.2 AN INSTRUCTION -TUNED LLM READS THE TEXT FOR THE QUESTION 

An instruction-tuned LLM given a text followed by a question computes every state of the text before it sees the question. Because of the causal mask, the text’s states are the same under every question, and the question can act on the text only through where later positions attend. At the position where the LLM is about to answer, its attention over the text, summed over heads and renormalized over the text, is what we call its reading weights (Figure 1). Only the weights can cross. Of what an instruction-tuned LLM computes while it reads a text under an instruction, an embedder could be handed either vectors or weights. Vectors, whether hidden states, values or the representation of an answer, live in the LLM’s own coordinates, with their own dimension and geometry, and cannot enter another model without a learned map. Weights over the text have no coordinates: they are a distribution over the text itself.

The weights carry the instruction. They are also where the instruction acts. In a Transformer, attention is the only operation that moves information between positions, and with the text before the instruction, the text’s values are the same under every instruction. The text’s share of a head’s output at the answer position, Pnj=1 αj (x, q ) vj (x), therefore depends on the instruction only through its weights, so passing the weights passes everything the instruction changes in how the LLM reads the text, and nothing else. 

The embedder already reads through weights. An embedder pools its text with a distribution over its tokens: uniform in mean pooling, its pooling token’s attention in CLS or last-token pooling. Once the two tokenizations are aligned through characters, the LLM’s weights mean the same thing in the embedder, how much each part of the text matters for the question, and they enter the embedder where its own weights already are. Which parts of a text matter for a question is a property of the text and the question, not of the model that reads them; how to represent those parts stays with the embedder. 

## 4 EXPERIMENTS 

We evaluate Attention Relay across LLMs, embedders and kinds of instruction, then change one ingredient at a time and ask what the relay passes, what it can select and what it changes. 4.1 SETUP 

Models. We take the reading weights of six instruction-tuned LLMs: Qwen3 at 0.6B, 1.7B, 4B and 8B (Yang et al., 2025a), Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (Grattafiori et al., 2024) and OLMo-3-7B-Instruct (Team Olmo, 2025). Four of their base checkpoints serve as controls. We relay the weights into ten embedders that take no instruction: Qwen3-Embedding in its document mode (Zhang et al., 2025c), BGE (Xiao et al., 2024b), GTE (Li et al., 2023), E5 (Wang et al., 2022) and two sentence-transformers models (Reimers & Gurevych, 2019) (Appendix A.1). They span tokenizers, sizes and three kinds of pooling: the last token, [CLS] and the mean. We read each LLM at one block, chosen without labels where its weights move most with the question, and keep one setting per pair on every task. Unless stated otherwise, we relay Qwen3-1.7B’s weights. 

Tasks. We use InBedder’s evaluation sets (Peng et al., 2024). NYT holds 2,144 news articles, labeled by topic and by the country where the news happened. We embed every article once under the topic question and once under the location question, cluster each set of embeddings by the aspect its question asks about, and report the harmonic mean of the two V-measures. IntentEmotion (IE) holds customer messages, labeled by how the customer feels and by what the customer needs, and arranges them in triplets. Each triplet asks whether a message is closer to one that shares its emotion or one that shares its intent; an embedding that ignores the question scores 100, and one that always follows it scores 200. Four more sets carry instructions of other kinds. FewRel, FewNerd and FewEvent ask every text for the type of a marked relation, entity or event, and InstructSTSB gives every pair of sentences its own instruction. We run them with Qwen3-1.7B here and with Llama and OLMo in Appendix C.1. Every gain comes with a 95% paired bootstrap interval (Appendix B). 4.2 MAIN RESULTS

What the relay passes. The gain comes from the words of each text that the LLM reads (Figure 8). Weights with the LLM’s shape but the wrong words keep at most about a third of it, whether they are shuffled within the text, taken from another text or reduced to a profile of positions. Removing the tenth of words the LLM weights most takes away most of the gain, while removing a random tenth takes at most a quarter. 

What the relay can select. The relay gains more when the text names what the question asks about. We split NYT’s articles into those that name one of the ten countries or its people and those that do not, and cluster each set on its own. Where an article names the country, the location gain is two to six times larger than where the place is only implied, for four of the five embedders (Figure 9). Qwen3-Embedding, the one embedder built on an LLM, gains about as much in both. 

What the relay changes. Relaying makes the asked aspect count in one of two ways: it surfaces an aspect the embedding held but did not cluster by, or makes an aspect more readable in the first place. A linear probe reads whether an embedding holds an aspect at all, and the V-measure whether the embedding clusters by it. An NYT article runs to hundreds of words, and most articles name the country or its people. Every embedding alone already holds the location, which a probe reads with 94 to 96% accuracy; relaying leaves the probe within a point but raises location V-measure by 15 to 27 points. A FewNerd text is one sentence of about 30 words, and its label is the type of one marked entity, often among entities of other types. Pooled over the whole sentence, that entity takes a small share of the embedding; relaying moves the pooling onto it and raises the probe’s accuracy by 3 to 11 points. ## 5 RELATED WORK 

Instruction-aware text embeddings. A text can be grouped or compared in several valid ways, as studied in alternative clustering (Gondek & Hofmann, 2004; Niu et al., 2014) and conditional simi-larity (Veit et al., 2017), as in C-STS (Deshpande et al., 2023). Instruction-aware embedders let an instruction choose the way and learn to follow it in training: InstructOR (Su et al., 2023), E5-Mistral (Wang et al., 2024), GritLM (Muennighoff et al., 2025) and Qwen3-Embedding (Zhang et al., 2025c) through contrastive training with instructions, InBedder by learning to answer a question about the text (Peng et al., 2024), and GSTransform through a transformation of a generic embedder’s outputs, trained for each instruction (Feng et al., 2025). Even so, retrieval models trained with instructions use them mostly as keywords (FollowIR; Weller et al., 2025). Attention Relay trains nothing: the instruction following comes from an LLM at inference, and the embedder is used as released. 

Training-free embeddings from LLMs. An LLM’s hidden state at the end of a prompt that asks for a text in one word serves as the text’s embedding (PromptEOL; Jiang et al., 2024); later methods average several prompts (MetaEOL; Lei et al., 2024) or generated rewrites (GenEOL; Thirukoval-luru & Dhingra, 2025), repeat the text (Echo embeddings; Springer et al., 2025), or pool the value vectors (Value Aggregation; Zhang et al., 2026). With a condition in the prompt, such readouts become conditional (PonTE; Yamada & Zhang, 2025), and self-contrastive steering improves them (Focusing Condition; Cheng et al., 2026). All of them represent the text in the LLM’s own space; Attention Relay takes only the LLM’s attention and keeps the space the embedder was trained for. 

Attention as a question-conditioned signal. At the last position, attention heads fetch what a prediction needs from earlier tokens (Geva et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2025). As a score, this attention re-ranks passages in ICR (Chen et al., 2025) and QRHead (Zhang et al., 2025b), ranks documents once calibrated for position (Found in the Middle; Hsieh et al., 2024), decides which cached tokens to keep (SnapKV; Li et al., 2024b), and, taken from a small model, which tokens a larger model reads (SpecPrefill; Liu et al., 2025). Attention weights are not explanations in general (Jain & Wallace, 2019; Wiegreffe & Pinter, 2019), so we test the reading weights against shuffled, other-text and uniform weights. These uses stay within one model or one tokenizer family; Attention Relay carries the weights through characters into a different model. 8Preprint 

Changing attention at inference. Attention steering scales a model’s attention on one span, marked by the user (PASTA; Zhang et al., 2024a), found by the model (AutoPASTA; Zhang et al., 2024b) or holding the instruction, as in SpotLight (Venkateswaran & Contractor, 2026) and InstA-Boost (Guardieiro et al., 2025), by one factor. Image editing, as in Prompt-to-Prompt (Hertz et al., 2023) and Plug-and-Play (Tumanyan et al., 2023), injects the attention maps of one generation into another, which keeps its own values, and attention transfer (Zagoruyko & Komodakis, 2017; Li et al., 2024a) and MiniLM (Wang et al., 2020) train a student with a teacher’s attention maps, also across tokenizers through character offsets (MATT; Haltiuk & Smywi´ nski-Pohl, 2025).

Each relayed output is thus a convex combination of vectors the embedder computed itself: the instruction decides which of the embedder’s content dominates, and only a distribution over the text, never a vector, passes from the LLM. What the embedder does not compute cannot be relayed: knowledge the LLM draws on to answer, such as the country a city lies in, and anything an instruction asks beyond which tokens matter, such as how two entities relate. 

A shared backbone. Many embedders built on an LLM keep its weights and add a small fine-tuning change, often a LoRA adapter (Hu et al., 2022). If the LLM’s instruction tuning were an adapter too, one backbone could serve as both models: with one adapter it would read the text for the question, and with the other it would embed it. The two would share their tokens, their heads and a single copy of the weights. Modern post-training, however, changes every weight of the model rather than training an adapter. 

Open questions. The relay needs attention whose weights form a distribution over the text, as softmax attention does. Sparse attention, such as NSA (Yuan et al., 2025), reads only part of the text at full resolution, and linear attention, such as Gated DeltaNet (Yang et al., 2025b), keeps a state instead of weights; whether their implicit attention (Ali et al., 2025) can be relayed, from such an LLM or into such an embedder, is open. Nothing in the method is specific to text either: a vision-language model’s attention over an image follows the question (Zhang et al., 2025a), and aligning image patches by area instead of characters would relay it into an image encoder’s pooling; whether this makes image embeddings instruction-aware, and embeddings of other modalities after them, is the next test. A.4 CONTROLS 

Figure 8 asks whether the gain needs the LLM’s weights on the text’s own words. Three controls keep the shape of the weights but cut their tie to the words. Shuffled within the text permutes the 16 Preprint text’s weights at random over its own tokens. Another text’s weights gives the text, token by token, the weights of a random text of the same length, or the nearest length. A profile of positions gives each token the LLM’s average weight at the same relative position, under the same question, over all the other texts, with relative position split into twenty bins. It keeps where in a text the LLM tends to look, but not which words this text holds. The shuffled and other-text controls average three random draws. Two more controls remove words from each text before the embedder reads it: the tenth of its words that the LLM weights most under the question, or a random tenth. On the shortened texts, we relay the LLM’s weights on the remaining words and compare with the embedder alone on the same texts.

C.3 ON THE TEXT SHARE 

We compare the mass-preserving bias with the plain bias defined in §3.1. What the LLM contributes is where each head reads within the text, not how much of its attention goes to the text. The mass-preserving bias holds each head’s attention on the text fixed, while the plain bias also lets it grow in the heads that agree with the LLM and shrink in the others; switching between them changes either score by about a point, a small fraction of the gain (Figure 12).
