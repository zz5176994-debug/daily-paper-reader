## Abstract 

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) has substantially improved the mathematical reasoning capabilities of large language models. Recent work introduces search into RLVR rollouts to increase trajectory diversity, but diversity alone does not ensure that the search-induced rollout policy improves upon the current policy. To address this gap, we propose APIVIS, a training-time framework that adapts finite-budget Gumbel search to chunk-level mathematical reasoning. APIVIS combines direct and searched responses within each rollout group, allowing improvements found by search to produce informative relative rewards. It further applies selective supervision to search-improved tokens, preserving a learning signal when uniform group rewards render GRPO ineffective. We show that exact value-guided selection improves the expected verifier reward at each searched state and that this guarantee extends to the complete rollout policy, with a corresponding approximate guarantee under bounded value-estimation error. Experiments on widely recognized mathematical reasoning benchmarks and different model scales demonstrate substantial improvements over competitive search-based methods, validating the effectiveness of APIVIS. 

## 1 Introduction 

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) improves mathematical reasoning by op-timizing groups of responses scored by an exact answer checker [Shao et al., 2024, DeepSeek-AI, 2025]. While GRPO and its relatives dominate current RLVR pipelines, their learning signal is largely determined by rollout diversity. Standard pipelines draw several complete responses independently from the current policy, yet token-level randomness often produces surface variations of the same rea-soning path. This limits the reasoning strategies available for learning; when the responses also share the same final return, their group-relative advantages vanish altogether. RLVR therefore faces both an exploration problem and a learnability problem: its rollout operator must improve the trajectory distribution and expose a preference that the policy can learn. A natural remedy is to make trajectory generation principled rather than rely on independent sampling. At inference time, process-reward and value-guided search already provide effective test-time scaling by selecting among candidate continuations [Lightman et al., 2024, Wang et al., 2025]. This success has motivated training-time search for rollout diversity or learning targets, but existing methods improve returned responses rather than establish an improved rollout policy (Appendix A). Greater diversity may restore relative contrast, and extra search may improve correctness, but neither guarantees that the search-induced policy dominates the current policy. Gumbel MuZero offers an inspiration but not a direct solution. Its central idea is to define a searched policy by monotonically reweighting the prior policy with completed action values, ensuring that the expected value under the searched distribution is no lower than under the prior; Gumbel action sampling and sequential halving approximate this operator under a limited simulation budget [Danihelka et al., 2022]. This construction assumes a finite, enumerable action set whose prior probabilities and action values refer to the same decision. In language reasoning, actions are variable-length chunks, the candidate set is itself sampled from the policy, and values are estimated probabilities of reaching correct answers given the response prefixes. Search may commit several chunks within one completion. Translating the improvement operator therefore requires defining the policy induced jointly by candidate generation and value-based selection, proving that the local inequality survives random candidate sets and composes across a complete trajectory, and quantifying the effect of value error. The resulting local preference must then be transferred to the language model even when identical final returns remove the group-relative signal. We introduce APIVIS, which treats reasoning chunks as search actions and uses value-guided Gumbel search as an improvement operator inside online RLVR. Each rollout group mixes direct and searched responses, while a selective auxiliary loss trains only on search-improved tokens. The former converts better search outcomes into group-relative contrast; the latter preserves an improvement-aligned signal when GRPO group returns are uninformative, provided that the value-improvement filter admits searched tokens. In the reported configuration, successful and failed trajectories have the same InfoSFT weight, so an all-failure group can receive InfoSFT supervision even though its group-relative GRPO gradient is zero. We summarize our contributions as follows: • We formulate value-guided selection over sampled reasoning chunks and prove statewise and complete-response policy improvement, with an explicit degradation bound under value-estimation error. • We propose APIVIS, which combines finite-budget chunk search, mixed-policy rollout groups, and selective supervision to distill accepted search-improved decisions, including uniform-return groups for which the group-relative gradient vanishes. • We evaluate APIVIS at Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-4B scales, obtaining consistent improve-ments over direct RLVR and search-based baselines. 

## 2 Value-guided informative search 

Chunk-level reasoning process. For problem x, state s contains the prompt and committed reasoning, and action a is the next variable-length reasoning chunk. The transition appends the chunk, 

s′ = s ◦ a, and the only reward is terminal correctness r(x, y ) ∈ { 0, 1}. We write 

Qπ (s, a ) = E[r | s, a, then follow π], (1) 2where π is the current language-model policy. Chunks keep the tree shallow and align with partial solutions scored by token-level value models [Wang et al., 2025]; Appendix B specifies the operational chunk boundaries. 

Value-guided chunk search. At fixed chunk boundaries, π proposes C candidates and a frozen value model scores their prefixes (Figure 1a). Gumbel sequential halving allocates S simulations by repeatedly pruning weak roots and expanding survivors. The rollout commits the final principal chunk and resumes decoding; the completed response receives the usual terminal verifier reward. Concretely, root candidates are sampled independently from the current policy and assigned independent Gumbel variables. Simulations are divided across sequential-halving phases. Each simulation descends from its assigned root through least-visited children with deterministic tie breaking, evaluates an unexpanded or terminal leaf with the frozen value model, expands a nonterminal leaf with policy-sampled chunks, and backs the leaf value up through visit counts, value sums, and their mean. At each phase boundary, the lower-scoring half of the roots is removed. After the final phase, the highest-scoring surviving root and its highest-value principal continuation are committed; discarded branches never enter the training batch. 

Mixed-policy rollout groups. Each six-response group contains three direct samples from π and three from the search-induced policy μ (Figure 1b). GRPO normalizes all six returns together, so improvements found by search create within-group contrast while direct responses anchor learning to the current policy.

## 3 Experiments 

3.1 Setup 

We evaluate Qwen3-1.7B-Base and Qwen3-4B-Base. Every run uses the same ordered Open-RS training data [Dang and Ngo, 2025], verifier, optimizer, and data schedule: 64 prompts per step, six completions per prompt, and 100 training steps. At both scales we compare the initial policy, GRPO, LATR, and APIVIS; the 4B study additionally includes TS-LLM, while the 1.7B study includes a matched MCTS-alpha + GRPO baseline. LATR retains its native branching rule. TS-LLM and the matched MCTS-alpha baseline use MCTS with the same per-search simulation budget as the Gumbel search in APIVIS. Search placement in APIVIS is fixed across trajectories. Each APIVIS group contains three directly sampled completions and three search-generated completions. All mathematical training results use one training seed. Search methods additionally incur candidate-generation and value-model inference cost. Over the archived 4B runs, APIVIS uses 20.06 logged training hours (160.51 GPU-hours), compared with 6.32 hours for GRPO, 38.64 for LATR, and 40.16 for TS-LLM; Appendix C.1 gives the full accounting. We evaluate direct policy decoding on MATH-500 [Hendrycks et al., 2021], AIME-2024, AIME-2025, and OlympiadBench [He et al., 2024]. For each problem, Avg@16 is the mean verifier accuracy over 16 sampled completions at temperature 0.7 and top-p = 0 .95 . The validation artifact is a deterministic 0.5% split of Open-RS generated with seed 42 (35 prompts). Each validation point uses four direct-policy samples per prompt. For every headline model-comparison row, we select the saved checkpoint ranked first by validation mean@4, with earlier-step tie breaking, and only then run the four full test benchmarks. The reported APIVIS runs use fixed 3-direct/3-search in training rollouts; Appendix C lists all hyperparameters. 

3.2 Main Results 

Table 1 compares the rollout and search strategies at both model scales. With Qwen3-4B-Base, APIVIS achieves 40 .02 average Avg@16, exceeding GRPO, LATR, and TS-LLM by 3.20 , 3.48 ,and 3.87 percentage points, respectively, and performs best on all four benchmarks. Neither LATR (36 .54 ) nor TS-LLM ( 36 .15 ) improves the average over GRPO ( 36 .82 ). Search-based generation alone therefore does not automatically translate into a stronger directly decoded policy under this protocol. Relative to GRPO, APIVIS gains 2.32 points on MATH-500, 2.71 on AIME-2024, 4.17 on AIME-2025, and 3.59 on OlympiadBench. Relative to the LATR baseline, the gains are 1.41 , 4.17 , 3.96 , and 

4.37 points, respectively. With Qwen3-1.7B-Base, APIVIS reaches 27 .79 , compared with 25 .26 for GRPO, 22 .59 for LATR, and 25 .10 for MCTS-alpha + GRPO: gains of 2.53 , 5.20 , and 2.69 points, respectively.

## 4 Discussion 

Limitations. The value model is frozen throughout training. This keeps the search signal stable and makes the comparison interpretable, but it also prevents the value function from adapting as the policy improves; stale or miscalibrated values can misrank chunks, and the approximate guarantee degrades with value error. Moreover, finite-budget sequential halving only approximates the ideal improved distribution, and our experiments consider single-turn mathematical completions with terminal verification. The experiments cover two model scales but use one training seed per configuration, and the ablations do not separately isolate the effect of mixed direct/search group construction. 

Future work. A natural next step is to co-train the value model and policy while controlling critic drift, so that search quality can track the evolving policy without destabilizing the improvement operator. Monitoring candidate-ranking quality may provide a practical signal that the value model has plateaued and should undergo another round of training. RLVR and rollout diversity. GRPO normalizes verifier rewards within responses sampled for one prompt, removing the need for a learned critic [Shao et al., 2024]; DeepSeek-R1 demonstrates the effectiveness of this recipe for mathematical reasoning [DeepSeek-AI, 2025]. Its update, however, depends on the responses exposing useful return differences. When ancestral samples follow similar reasoning paths or receive the same return, group-relative learning supplies little information. This motivates changing the rollout operator rather than only the optimizer. 

Search for inference-time reasoning. Process-reward models rank intermediate solutions and support best-of-n or tree-style test-time scaling [Lightman et al., 2024]. Value-guided search extends this idea to partial reasoning traces scored by a token-level value model [Wang et al., 2025]. These methods improve the answer returned for a fixed prompt, but they need not define a searched distribution that improves the policy used for subsequent training. 

Search for training-time exploration. LATR branches and prunes during RLVR generation to increase trajectory diversity [Xing et al., 2025], while ROSE resamples from uncertain positions and assigns tree-structured credit [Zhao et al., 2026]. TS-LLM and ReST-MCTS ∗ instead use tree search to construct learning targets [Wan et al., 2024, Zhang et al., 2024]. These approaches establish the utility of search during training, but do not guarantee that the search-induced rollout policy dominates the current language-model policy. 

Context-guided exploration and informative sampling. Search is not the only way to change the rollout distribution. NudgeRL conditions generation on lightweight strategy contexts and distills the discovered behavior back into the base policy [Lee et al., 2026], while weak-model guidance uses prefixes from a weaker policy to move rollouts outside the current policy’s typical trajectories [Shen et al., 2026]. DAPO dynamic sampling instead resamples prompts to increase the supply of groups with nonzero relative advantages [Yu et al., 2025]. These approaches address closely related exploration or learnability failures without online value-guided tree search. Our experiments do not compare them under matched total compute. Complementarily, GAUGE shows that LLM-as-a-judge evaluation of user-simulated task-oriented agents can be poorly anchored to grounded task success, motivating verifiable rewards when they are available [Bodhwani et al., 2026]. 

Search as policy improvement. Gumbel MuZero combines Gumbel action sampling and sequential halving to approximate a value-improved policy under a small simulation budget [Danihelka et al., 2022]. APIVIS adapts this principle to sampled, variable-length reasoning chunks, then couples searched continuations to both group-relative learning and selective supervision. The distinction is that search is treated as an explicit improvement operator, not only as a source of diverse or high-reward trajectories. 

## B Reasoning Chunk Definition 

We factor each completion into variable-length chunks rather than individual tokens. A state contains the prompt and all chunks committed so far; an action is the next chunk, and the transition appends that chunk to the response prefix. Operationally, generation of one chunk stops at the first of • a decoded blank line "\n\n" , which marks a reasoning-step boundary; • a decoded "</answer>" tag, which closes the final answer; or • the EOS token. These boundaries are additionally subject to the remaining response budget and a safety token limit that prevents a pathological single chunk when no boundary fires. Truncation is applied directly to token IDs, so the retained text is always the exact decoded prefix.
