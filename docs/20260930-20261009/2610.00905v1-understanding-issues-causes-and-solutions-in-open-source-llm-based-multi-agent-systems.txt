A B S T R A C T 

With the advancement of LLM-based multi-agent systems (MAS), an increasing number of open-source projects are adopting multi-agent architectures as the foundation of their core functionality. Although research and practice on MAS have attracted considerable attention, limited studies have explored the challenges faced by practitioners of open-source LLM-based MAS, the causes of these challenges, and potential solutions. To address this gap, we conducted an empirical study to understand the issues that practitioners encounter when developing and using open-source LLM-based MAS, the possible causes of these issues, and potential solutions. We collected 22,848 closed issues from 21 open-source LLM-based MAS and applied a mixed automated and manual filtering approach to reduce the dataset to 944 issues related to LLM-based MAS. We then analyzed these issues to understand the frequent issues encountered by practitioners, their underlying causes, and potential solutions. Our study results show that (1) Orchestration & Execution Issue is the most common issue faced by practitioners, (2) Workflow Problem , Tool Integration Problem , and Memory Problem are identified as the most frequent causes of the issues, and (3) Optimize Workflow is the predominant solution to the issues. Based on the study results, we derive empirically grounded implications for practitioners and researchers aimed at improving orchestration, tool integration, and memory mechanisms in LLM-based MAS. 

## 1. Introduction 

Large Language Models (LLMs) are transforming the landscape of software development, moving us beyond sim-ple coding copilots to a future where specialized LLM-powered agents actively collaborate like a digital team (Hou et al., 2024). Since the release of ChatGPT in 2022 (Ope-nAI, 2025), research on LLMs has grown rapidly (Zhao et al., 2023a). The software engineering (SE) community quickly recognized their potential and began applying LLMs throughout the software development lifecycle (Hou et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024). Early studies examined single-agent scenarios, where an LLM assists developers with code generation, code review, software testing, and requirements documentation (Fan et al., 2023). Recent advances in multi-agent frameworks have en-abled the development of LLM-based multi-agent systems (MAS), in which multiple specialized LLM-powered agents communicate, coordinate, and collaborate to accomplish complex, sequential software engineering tasks that were previously too difficult for a single LLM agent (He et al., 2025b; Liu et al., 2026). causes of these issues, as well as possible solutions . With this aim in mind, we formulate three Research Questions (RQs): • RQ1 : What issues do practitioners face when working on LLM-based MAS? • RQ2 : What are the underlying causes of the issues in LLM-based MAS? • RQ3 : What are the potential solutions to address the issues in LLM-based MAS? To answer RQ1–RQ3, we analyzed the filtered set of 944 GitHub issues to identify the issues reported by practitioners, examine their underlying causes, and the solutions adopted in practice. Workflow Problem , Tool Integration Problem , and Memory Problem are identified as the most frequent causes of the issues, and (3) Optimize Workflow and Adjust Configuration 

are the predominant solutions to the issues in LLM-based MAS. The main contributions of this work are threefold: (1) We identify the key issues that occur during the development of LLM-based MAS projects using the dataset (Rehman et al., 2026) and organize them into a two-level taxonomy to provide a hierarchical understanding of their character-istics. (2) We identify the root causes of these issues and document the corresponding solutions proposed and adopted by practitioners, organizing both into taxonomies for causes and solutions. (3) We establish and analyze the mapping re-lationships between the identified issues and their underlying causes, as well as between the issues and their associated solutions, to better understand how issues are triggered and addressed in practice. The rest of this paper is structured as follows: Section 2 presents the Research Questions (RQs) and the research methodology employed in this study. Section 3 presents the study results. Section 4 discusses the findings and impli-cations based on the study results. Section 5 discusses the threats to validity. Section 6 reviews the related work, and Section 7 concludes this work along with the directions for future research. 

## 2. Methodology 

This study follows a systematic, multi-stage research methodology to investigate the practitioner-reported issues, underlying causes, and corresponding solutions in open-source LLM-based MAS. Based on this objective, we formu-late three Research Questions (RQs) to guide the subsequent phases of the study and ensure a structured and repro-ducible analysis process, as shown in Figure 2. The research process comprises the formulation of RQs in Section 2.1, data collection in Section 2.2, data filtering and labeling in Section 2.3, data extraction in Section 2.4, and data analysis in Section 2.5.

2.2. Data Collection 

We collected data for our study from GitHub repositories that clearly mention MAS in their documentation. GitHub is currently the largest platform for hosting OSS projects. In addition, every project on GitHub has an “Issues” page, which provides a platform for users and developers of the project to report issues experienced during development and usage, and to discuss the identification, analysis, and resolution of these issues with other users and developers. In order to thoroughly explore the causes of issues and their potential solutions, we collected all “closed” issues from the repositories of the selected LLM-based MAS. To ensure comprehensive data collection, we did not set a start date for the data source. The date we performed the data search was November 16, 2025, and we collected all the closed issues created before this date. The first author utilized a keyword-based search approach, using the search terms “Multi-Agent System”, “Multi-Agent LLM”, and “AI Agents” to search across GitHub. Any project that contained these search terms (e.g., in the project description or tags) was retrieved. After obtaining the search results, we found that although many projects include the terms “Multi-Agent System”, “Multi-Agent LLM”, or “AI Agents”, they do not actually implement multi-agent architectures or utilize multiple LLM-based agents as core components. 2.3. Data Filtering and Labeling 

We collected closed issues from 21 open-source LLM-based MAS and observed that not all reported issues were relevant to MAS-related problems. For example, some prac-titioners used issue trackers for community interaction or general inquiries, such as requesting access to a project’s Discord channel Agency-swarm #37, without describing any technical problem in the MAS projects. We also identified issues that were purely single-agent in nature and did not involve multi-agent collaboration, despite being reported in MAS project repositories (e.g., Sdk-python #1435). In addition, we found that the issue labels were often too generic, and relying solely on existing labels could lead to missing relevant information. For example, the multi-agent 

Orchestration Issue reported in Autogen #6819 was assigned only the generic Bug label, making it difficult to identify through label-based filtering alone. To address these challenges in filtering and labeling MAS-related issues, we adopted a two-stage mixed ap-proach, combining automated and manual filtering meth-ods to construct a high-quality dataset. This process fo-cused on identifying closed issues that explicitly describe practitioner-reported problems related to MAS. Based on the pilot and formal filtering results in Section 2.3.1 and Sec-tion 2.3.2, the final dataset was constructed and subsequently used for data extraction and analysis.

We conducted pilot data filtering by randomly select-ing 100 closed issues from the collected dataset. The first and second authors independently labeled these issues to minimize potential personal bias. We observed that multi-agent issues were rare only 11 issues were related to real MAS issues, whereas non-multi-agent issues accounted for a relatively large proportion. Therefore, we adopted a mixed approach, combining automated and manual methods for

Automated Filtering : We applied a pilot automated fil-tering approach (He et al., 2025a), following the method-ology of prior studies demonstrating the effectiveness of LLM-based evaluation and automated assessment in soft-ware engineering tasks (Zhou et al., 2025a; Crupi et al., 2025). We randomly selected 500 issues from the col-lected 22,848 closed issues and processed them with the 

DeepSeek-R1 model deployed locally via Ollama, with care-fully crafted prompt instructions and iterative refinement to identify MAS-related issues (Montes et al., 2025). Using this approach, we identified 110 MAS-related issues. 

Manual Filtering : To ensure dataset quality, we did not fully rely on the LLM; therefore, manual filtering was also conducted. Two human coders, the first and second authors, were involved in this process. They labeled the same sample of 500 issues independently, which contained information related to problems encountered during the development or use of open-source LLM-based MAS. Inter-rater agreement between the two authors was measured using Cohen’s Kappa coefficient (Cohen, 1960), yielding a value of 0.82, which indicates a very high level of labeling consistency between the two authors. Any disagreements in the pilot labeling results were resolved through discussion involving the fourth author to reach a consensus. Finally, 102 out of the 500 closed issues were considered real multi-agent issues. 

Evaluation of Automated Filtering : To evaluate the effectiveness and accuracy of the DeepSeek-R1 model, we compared the automated filtering results with those of the manual filtering. Out of the 500 sampled issues, manual filtering labeled 102 multi-agent-related issues, while auto-mated filtering labeled 110 such issues. The comparison be-tween manual and automated labels yielded 452 agreements, where both methods produced consistent labels (i.e., auto=0, manual=0 or auto=1, manual=1), and 48 disagreements, where the labels differed (i.e., auto=1, manual=0 or auto=0, manual=1). We measured the agreement between the au-tomated filtering results and the human annotations. Using this measure, the automated filtering approach achieved an agreement rate of 90.4%. To examine the 48 disagreements, we reviewed the cases where automated filtering labeled an issue as 0 but manual filtering labeled it as 1. In these cases, MAS concepts were not explicitly mentioned, and the relevance to MAS could only be determined from the issue context rather than explicit keywords. As a result, the LLM did not filter these issues as MAS-related issues. For example, Agentscope #489 does not explicitly describe a multi-agent issue, but its relevance to MAS is only clear from the context, not from explicit keywords. Conversely, in cases where automated filtering labeled an issue as 1 but manual filtering labeled it as 0, the issues often contained MAS-related terminology or project components, leading the LLM to filter them as MAS-related issues. 2.4. Data Extraction 

To answer the three RQs presented in Section 2.1, we defined a set of data items for data extraction, as shown in Table 5. The data items D1–D3 are used to extract infor-mation about issues, underlying causes, and solutions that address these issues, corresponding to RQ1–RQ3, respec-tively. These data items are extracted from different parts of the collected closed issues, including the title, description, and discussion. To ensure the accuracy of the data extraction results and the validity of the subsequent data analysis, we decided to conduct the data extraction manually. In addition, the dataset size is relatively small, making manual data extraction feasible. The details of the pilot and formal data extraction procedures are presented in Section 2.4.1 and Section 2.4.2, respectively. 

2.4.1. Pilot Data Extraction 

We randomly selected 100 closed issues from our filtered dataset. The first author then independently performed pilot data extraction on these issues. The results indicate that all three data items (see Table 5) can be extracted from our dataset. Based on the pilot extraction results, we reached a consensus and defined the following rules for formal data extraction. (1) In principle, only one issue is extracted from each closed issue; if multiple issues can be extracted, we record the first issue discussed. (2) If there are multiple causes mentioned in a closed issue, we record all the causes of the issue extracted in the former step, which are identified by the reporter and the development team. (3) Similarly, if there are multiple solutions mentioned in a closed issue, we record all the solutions identified by the reporter and the development team that can address the issue extracted in the former step.

In some cases, the discussion was too vague to identify specific causes or solutions, while in others, issues were automatically closed due to inactivity, low priority, or high complexity, making it difficult to extract information. 

2.5. Data Analysis 

After completing the data extraction, we performed data analysis to address the three RQs formulated in Section 2.1. We applied the Open Coding and Constant Comparison methods, which are widely used in Grounded Theory for qualitative analysis in software engineering research (Stol et al., 2016). Open Coding represents the initial stage of anal-ysis, where researchers systematically examine the text to identify key concepts and categories, assigning descriptive codes to each element. Constant Comparison follows as a refinement stage, in which researchers iteratively compare new data with previously coded data to adjust and integrate categories based on their similarities and differences. The steps in our data analysis are as follows: (1) The first author meticulously read through the text of each closed issue and assigned descriptive codes that succinctly summa-rize the core themes according to the three RQs (see Table 5). For example, Smolagents #1353 was coded as “Agent Tool Registration Failure” , which indicates an error caused by improper agent integration. 3.1.2. Memory & State Management Issue 

(14.7%) Memory & State Management Issue refers to issues in storing, updating, and propagating memory, state, or intermediate information across agents during execution. Practitioners encounter these challenges during the develop-ment and use of LLM-based multi-agent OSS. This category encompasses the following six types. • CONTEXT LOSS refers to issues where persistent state or shared memory is not properly maintained or retrieved across agents during execution. Contextual information stored at higher levels is not consistently propagated or is reinitialized instead of being reused, causing agents to lose access to previously established states and leading to inconsistent or incorrect behavior across execution steps. For example, a practitioner reported that “ team shared context is lost whenever moving on to subsequent agent ” (Agno #3254). • STATE INCONSISTENCY refers to issues where shared state or intermediate outputs are not consistently vis-ible, synchronized, or aligned across agents during execution. State updates are not reliably propagated, or different agents access different versions of the same state due to timing or synchronization issues, causing agents to operate on inconsistent state views, leading to unreliable or divergent execution behavior. For example, a practitioner reported that “ the second agent is often unable to see items stored in the state of the previous agent ” (Adk-python #1119). • STATE PERSISTENCE FAILURE refers to issues where stored state or session-level information is not correctly initialized, preserved, or made accessible across agents during execution. State is not properly bound to session identifiers, or stored information becomes unavailable during runtime despite being ex-plicitly defined, preventing agents from retrieving pre-viously stored session state and leading to incomplete or incorrect execution. For example, a practitioner reported that “ team_session_state not initialized when session_id is provided ” (Agno #3799). • RETRIEVAL FAILURE refers to issues where previ-ously stored state, memory entries, or checkpoints cannot be reliably accessed during execution, even if they exist in the system. Agents fail to retrieve the required previous state, leading to incomplete work-flow progression or reduced system reliability. For example, a practitioner reported that “ in a sequential agent workflow, the system randomly fails to retrieve stored checkpoints ” (Flowise #3859). • CONTEXT OVERFLOW refers to issues where accu-mulated contextual information exceeds the effective input or memory capacity of the system during multi-agent execution. Multiple agents continuously con-tribute information without filtering or compression, making the input context excessively large and leading to degraded performance or loss of relevant signals during reasoning. For example, a practitioner reported that “ a single agent needs to store information from multiple agents, which can lead to excessively long to-ken lengths in the input for large models ” (Agentscope #637). • STATE LOSS refers to issues where parts of the generated or updated state are not retained in the final execution outcome. In multi-agent or parallel workflows, intermediate state updates are overwritten, dropped, or not properly merged during aggregation, causing some agent contributions to be missing from the final system state and resulting in incomplete or partial execution results. For example, a practitioner reported that “ state updates from parallel agent calls were not correctly aggregated, causing some agent contributions to be missing from the final session state ” (Adk-python #1153). 

Interpretation : The high proportion of Memory & State Management Issue indicates that state handling is a critical challenge in LLM-based MAS. Context Loss (42) and State Inconsistency (36) are the most frequently reported issues, showing a consistent pattern of unreliable state propagation across agents. There is a clear gap between the designed memory abstractions and their actual behavior in execution. This highlights the need for reliable state management and memory propagation mechanisms in future MAS.

## 4. Implications 

In this section, we discuss the implications of the study results presented in Section 3 for users, developers, and researchers of open-source LLM-based MAS. 

4.1. Implications for Users of LLM-based MAS   

> Implication 1 . Users can address the Orchestration & Execution Issue by designing explicit prompt-based hand-off mechanisms and implementing appropriate execution controls.

Orchestration & Execution Issue (22.0%) ranks first among all 17 issue categories, and within this category, 

Coordination & Handoff Issue is the dominant type, indi-cating that users frequently face difficulties in coordinating task transfers and managing execution flow in LLM-based MAS. A practitioner reported that “ the sub-agent could not correctly transfer control back to the root agent when calling an MCP tool ” (Adk-python #1039), suggesting that unclear handoff specifications can lead to agents coordi-nation failures. Several practices can improve coordination among agents and enhance the reliability of task execution in LLM-based MAS. For example, users can define explicit handoff prompts or utilize built-in agent handoff mecha-nisms provided by frameworks such as OpenAI Agents SDK (OpenAI, 2025) to clearly specify transfer conditions and task responsibilities between agents. Furthermore, users can configure framework-level execution controls, such as retry limits, timeout settings, and logging mechanisms, to reduce infinite loops, premature termination, and unexpected ex-ecution behaviors in LLM-based MAS. In addition, many LLM-based multi-agent frameworks provide orchestration and monitoring capabilities that support workflow design, inspection, and debugging, enabling early detection of co-ordination issues and improving collaboration reliability between agents (Dibia et al., 2024). For example, cite-cemri2025multi conducted a comprehensive empirical study on multi-agent failure modes and found that inter-agent mis-alignment, including communication breakdowns, context loss during handoffs, and conflicting outputs, accounts for 36.9% of failures across seven MAS frameworks. Conse-quently, users should prioritize explicit handoff design and systematic execution monitoring to address Orchestration & Execution Issue , the most common issue category in LLM-based MAS.

> Implication 2 . Users can leverage LLM-based multi-agent development frameworks for software development tasks while remaining mindful of the potential issues and limita-tions of LLMs.

One-third of the open-source LLM-based MAS inves-tigated in this study are frameworks for developing soft-ware applications by leveraging multiple collaborating LLM agents (see Table 3). These frameworks lower the barrier for users to build applications using LLM-based MAS. For example, AutoGen is an open-source framework that enables users to build LLM applications using multiple agents that can communicate with each other to accomplish tasks (Wu et al., 2024). It also allows users to configure agents with dif-ferent commercial and open-source LLMs, according to their application requirements, enabling users to select a model that best fits their needs. While such frameworks provide useful capabilities for software development, our findings show that LLM-based MAS also face several problems. Specifically, Orchestration & Execution Issue , Memory & State Management Issue , Tool Issue , and Integration Issue 

account for 22.0%, 14.7%, 9.2%, and 6.7% of the issues, respectively. These findings suggest that using open-source LLM-based MAS for software development may introduce various challenges. Users should therefore monitor agent workflows, manage agent memory and state, verify tool usage, and configure explicit agent communication mecha-nisms when deploying and using LLM-based MAS. 

4.2. Implications for Developers of LLM-based MAS 

> Implication 3 . Developers of open-source LLM-based MAS should possess a foundational understanding of multi-agent system design principles.

According to our study results, the following knowledge is required to develop and use open-source LLM-based MAS: (1) Developers need knowledge of designing and managing agent workflows and orchestration in LLM-based MAS. Our findings show that Workflow Problem (WP) ac-counts for 29.4% and ranks first among all identified causes. This indicates that developers need solid knowledge of agent coordination, handoff design, and execution flow manage-ment when developing LLM-based MAS. For example, a contributor reported that “ after invoking transfer_to_agent, the target agent did not respond and produced no output due to incomplete transfer of task-related information ” (Adk-python #2073), highlighting the importance of proper hand-off design while developing LLM-based MAS. (2) Devel-opers need to understand tool integration and execution in LLM-based MAS. According to our study results, Tool Integration Problem (TIP) accounts for 11.7% of all causes. This indicates that developers require knowledge of tool reg-istration, schema validation, and concurrent tool access man-agement when developing LLM-based MAS. For example, a developer reported that a “ tool deadlock occurred because the swarm could not access tools already locked by an active Claude Code session ” (Claude-flow #59), highlighting that knowledge of proper tool lifecycle management is essential when developing LLM-based MAS. (3) Developers need to understand memory management and state persistence in LLM-based MAS. Memory Problem (MP) accounts for 11.1% of all causes. For example, a developer explained that “external memory failed to store new memories in Mem0 because the agent ID exceeded the allowed length ” (CrewAI #2985), indicating that knowledge of session persistence and state consistency is essential for developing reliable LLM-based MAS. 

> Implication 4 . Developers can enhance the reliability of tool integration through schema validation and robust exe-cution controls.

Tool Integration Problem (TIP) accounts for 11.7% of all identified causes, with Tool Execution Problem and MCP In-tegration Problem being the most frequently observed types. A typical failure occurs when tools encounter deadlocks, resource contention, or schema mismatches at runtime in LLM-based MAS. For example, a practitioner reported that “MCP tool calls failed during asynchronous GroupChat execution because the event loop was already running ”(Ag2 #1763), while another reported that “ VertexAI Search Tool failed when combined with a sub-agent because mul-tiple tool types were not supported together ” (Adk-Python #2822). To avoid such failures, developers should provide standardized tool validation APIs that automatically check input/output schemas when tools are configured, enforce timeouts at the tool level, and check tool availability before execution in LLM-based MAS. Additionally, since con-current tool access by multiple agents can cause session locks, connection failures, and timeouts, MAS frameworks should provide mechanisms for managing tool connections, handling tool connection failures, and retrying failed tool calls. One practitioner reported that “ managing tool con-nections through framework-provided lifecycle hooks (e.g., FastAPI lifespan in Agno) prevented MCP connection fail-ures ” (Agno #4958), highlighting the importance of proper tool lifecycle management for reliable MCP integration in LLM-based MAS. 

4.3. Implications for Researchers of LLM-based MAS 

> Implication 5 . Researchers may consider exploring or-chestration frameworks to improve agent coordination and execution reliability in LLM-based MAS.

Orchestration & Execution Issue , accounting for 22.0% of all reported issues, represents the most critical challenge in LLM-based MAS. Within this category, Coordination & Handoff Issue and Control Flow Error are the most frequent types, highlighting that failures often originate from coordination deficiencies and execution management chal-lenges among agents. This finding is further supported by the root cause analysis, which identifies Workflow Problem

(WP) as the most significant underlying factor, account-ing for 29.4% of all reported causes. Moreover, Workflow Problem (WP) accounts for 57.0% of the causes associated with Orchestration & Execution Issue . This indicates that deficiencies in multi-agent workflow design, execution flow among agents, and inter-agent task coordination are major factors contributing to system-level failures in LLM-based MAS. We encourage researchers to shift their focus from enhancing individual agents reasoning capabilities toward developing robust orchestration frameworks that ensure reli-able handoffs between agents, correct execution order across the agents, and appropriate termination conditions in LLM-based MAS. Specifically, future work could investigate the formal verification of agent workflows and the development of runtime monitoring mechanisms capable of detecting and correcting coordination anomalies, such as infinite loops and premature termination, before they propagate throughout the system. 

> Implication 6 . Researchers could investigate methods for improving the reproducibility of issues in LLM-based MAS.

Given the complex and dynamic behavior of LLM-based MAS, 27 of the 128 unsolved issues involve difficulties in reproducing the reported problems. Without accurately reproducing the problem within LLM-based MAS, it can be difficult for practitioners to identify root causes of the issues, thereby hindering the resolution of the issues. For example, practitioners reported that the “ transfer_task_to_member er-ror occasionally occurs in some random cases when using the aprint_response method ” (Agno #3095), with multi-ple users experiencing different behaviors across versions. Identifying the root cause, in this case, a shared model in-stance issue, requires substantial investigation, highlighting the challenges of reproducing issues in LLM-based MAS and complicating their resolution. Therefore, future research could explore automated failure-reproduction techniques, controlled execution environments, and systematic test-case generation to improve the reproducibility of failures in LLM-based MAS.      

> Implication 7.Researchers could investigate reliable memory mechanisms for LLM-based MAS.

Memory & State Management Issue represents the sec-ond most frequent challenge in LLM-based MAS, account-ing for 14.7% of all reported issues. Within this category, 

Context Loss and State Inconsistency are the most common types, indicating limitations in current memory mechanisms in LLM-based MAS. This finding is further supported by the root cause analysis, which identifies Memory Problem (MP) as the third most significant underlying cause among all identified causes, accounting for 11.1% of all reported causes. Memory & State Management Issue accounts for 51.1% of the issues caused by Memory Problem (MP) . This indicates that deficiencies in context propagation between agents, state synchronization across agents, and memory re-trieval for agent interactions are major contributors to system failures in LLM-based MAS. We recommend that future research focus on developing reliable memory management mechanisms that ensure (1) consistent context propagation across agents during handoffs, (2) reliable state synchroniza-tion during parallel agent execution, and (3) effective context compression to prevent context overflow while preserving information essential for agent reasoning. 

## 5. Threats to Validity 

Construct validity : In our study, data collection was conducted automatically using GitHub APIs, while data filtering and labeling involved both automated and manual processes. Since several phases, including manual filtering, open coding, constant comparison, and data extraction, were performed manually, the study may be subject to personal bias during the classification, labeling, and extraction of issues, causes, and solutions in LLM-based MAS. To reduce this risk and improve the objectivity and reliability of the results, pilot studies were conducted using both automated and manual approaches before each formal phase to evaluate the effectiveness of methods, prompts, and criteria across different researchers, thereby improving construct validity. Following each pilot and formal study, we first performed automated filtering to obtain initial results. The first author then conducted a manual analysis and discussed the findings with the second and third authors to reach a consensus on the results related to the RQs. Since open coding and constant comparison were mainly carried out by the first author, there is still a risk of subjective interpretation affecting the results. The first author’s six years of software development experience, along with two years of experience working with LLM-based MASs, helped mitigate this risk, although it could not be eliminated entirely. In addition, future work may adopt more advanced automated or semi-automated techniques to further reduce bias in data filtering, labeling, and extraction. 

External validity : In our study, the main threat to ex-ternal validity comes from the selection of data sources. To address this, we used the “Issues” pages of GitHub projects as the primary data source. GitHub is the largest platform for hosting open-source projects. Each project includes an “Issues” section. Practitioners use it to report bugs, errors, and development difficulties. It also allows discussion be-tween users and developers. To extract more reliable in-formation on causes and solutions, we focused on closed issues. These usually contain resolved discussions and more complete resolution details of issues. However, even with these selection choices, we acknowledge that the dataset may not fully represent all practices in the development and use of LLM-based MAS. Some relevant information may reside outside GitHub or in undocumented settings, which could be explored as potential sources in future work. 

Reliability : To address potential uncertainties arising from the research methodology, we took several steps to

As described in Sec-tion 2.3.1, we first conducted pilot studies to check the agreement between the first and third authors on data la-beling before starting the formal study. In this pilot phase, Cohen’s Kappa value was 0.82, which indicated a good level of agreement. During the data labeling, extraction, and analysis stages, we regularly discussed cases where there were differences in interpretation. In addition, the dataset used in this study has been made publicly available (Rehman et al., 2026), so that other researchers can replicate the study and validate the results independently. 

## 6. Related Work 

The existing literature is organized into three categories. We first review empirical studies using software repository mining to investigate practitioner-reported issues, causes, and solutions in software systems and LLM-based projects in Section 6.1. We then discuss LLM-based MAS for soft-ware engineering in Section 6.2, followed by studies ex-ploring challenges, failure patterns, and limitations of LLM-based MAS in Section 6.3. Finally, we summarize the re-search gaps motivating this study in Section 6.4. 

6.1. Mining Software Repositories for Exploring Issues, Causes, and Solutions 

Mining software repositories has become a widely used approach for understanding practitioner-reported is-sues, their underlying causes, and corresponding solutions. Waseem et al. (2021) conducted an empirical study to un-derstand issues in open-source microservice systems. They manually analyzed 1,345 closed GitHub issues from five projects and developed a taxonomy of 17 issue categories. Their results show that Technical Debt , Build , and Security 

are the most frequently reported categories. They later ex-tended this work by analyzing 2,641 GitHub issues from 15 open-source microservice systems. They also conducted 15 practitioner interviews and surveyed 150 practitioners. Based on these findings, they developed comprehensive taxonomies of issues, root causes, and solutions in microser-vice systems. Zhao et al. (2023b) conducted a large-scale empirical study of 570 performance issues from 13 open-source projects. They identified eight common root causes and corresponding solutions. Their findings show that 27% of the issues require design-level optimization, highlighting the importance of architectural improvements for addressing performance issues. With the growing adoption of AI and LLMs in software engineering, researchers have extended repository mining to investigate practitioner-reported issues in LLM-based projects. Cai et al. (2025b) analyzed 994 GitHub issues from 15 open-source LLM projects. They de-veloped taxonomies of issue categories, root causes, and so-lutions. Their results show that Model Issue is the most com-mon issue category, while Model Problem , Configuration Problem , and Connection Problem are the most frequent root causes. Model Optimization is the predominant solution. Zhou et al. (2025b) investigated GitHub Copilot, an LLM-based AI programming assistant, by analyzing 473 GitHub issues, 706 GitHub discussions, and 142 Stack Overflow posts. Their findings show that Operation Issue and Com-patibility Issue are the most common problem categories. 

Copilot Internal Error , Network Connection Error , and IDE Compatibility Issue are the most frequent causes, while Bug Fixed by Copilot , Modify Configuration , and Use Suitable Version are the predominant solutions. Collectively, these studies establish a foundation for understanding practitioner-reported issues, their root causes, and solutions through repository mining across different software domains. 

6.2. LLM-based Multi-Agent Systems for Software Engineering 

Recent advances in LLMs have led to the emergence of LLM-based MAS, where multiple specialized agents collaborate to automate software engineering tasks. Qian et al. (2024) introduced ChatDev, a chat-powered software development framework that assigns specialized roles (e.g., CEO, CTO, Programmer, Reviewer, and Tester) to LLM-based agents. By using a chat chain to decompose tasks and support sequential interactions among agents, ChatDev enables collaborative software development and achieves an 88% executability score, reflecting the proportion of software projects generated by ChatDev that successfully compile and execute. These results demonstrate the effec-tiveness of LLM-based MAS for software development. Likewise, Hong et al. (2024) introduced MetaGPT, a meta-programming framework that incorporates Standard Op-erating Procedures (SOPs) into multi-agent collaboration. By assigning specialized roles to agents and enforcing structured workflows, MetaGPT enables agents to collab-oratively perform software engineering tasks through role-based coordination. Similarly, Wu et al. (2024) introduced AutoGen, a generalized multi-agent conversation framework for developing LLM-based applications. AutoGen enables agents to perform tasks using LLMs, human feedback, and external tools, while supporting flexible communication pat-terns for designing complex multi-agent workflows. Auto-Gen achieves 69.48% accuracy on the MATH dataset, a standard benchmark comprising 12,500 high-school com-petition math problems, substantially outperforming vanilla GPT-4. In addition, Nguyen et al. (2025) introduced Ag-ileCoder, a multi-agent software development framework inspired by Agile methodology. AgileCoder assigns spe-cialized roles (e.g., Product Manager, Scrum Master, De-veloper, and Tester) to agents and organizes development into iterative sprints based on user feedback. It further intro-duces a Dynamic Code Graph Generator to maintain code dependencies and provide relevant context for code genera-tion and modification. Evaluations on HumanEval, MBPP, and ProjectDev show that AgileCoder outperforms existing frameworks such as ChatDev and MetaGPT, demonstrating

the effectiveness of integrating Agile principles into multi-agent software development. To provide a broader perspec-tive on designing LLM-based MAS for software engineering tasks, Cai et al. (2025a) examined the quality attributes, design patterns, and design rationale adopted in existing LLM-based MAS. Their analysis shows that Code Gener-ation is the most frequently addressed software engineering task, Functional Suitability is the most emphasized quality attribute, and Role-Based Cooperation and Self-Reflection 

are the most commonly adopted design patterns. They also report that improving code quality and simulating human problem-solving are the primary motivations behind these design choices. Together, these studies demonstrate the ef-fectiveness and growing adoption of LLM-based MAS for software engineering tasks. However, improving their real-world deployment requires a deeper understanding of the challenges practitioners encounter, their underlying causes, and potential solutions. 

6.3. Exploration of Issues, Causes, and Solutions in LLM-based MAS 

While existing studies have demonstrated the potential of LLM-based MAS for software engineering tasks, recent research has started investigating the practical challenges, limitations, and failure patterns of these systems. Liu et al. (2025) conducted an empirical study of open-source LLM-based MAS frameworks by analyzing over 42,000 commits and 4,700 resolved issues across eight frameworks. They identified common development and maintenance issues, including bugs, infrastructure problems, and agent coordi-nation challenges, highlighting the difficulties in building re-liable MAS. Similarly, (Asgari et al., 2026) investigated de-veloper challenges in LLM-based MAS frameworks through Stack Overflow discussions and GitHub issues. Their study identified several challenges related to retrieval, agent mem-ory, orchestration, execution control, and runtime reliability, which affect the development and maintenance of LLM-based MAS frameworks. Furthermore, Hamid et al. (2026) explored practitioner difficulties in agentic frameworks by analyzing 2,658 Stack Overflow posts. They identified chal-lenges related to framework configuration, tool integration, vector database operations, and agent execution behavior, with runtime-related issues emerging as particularly chal-lenging to address. In addition, Cemri et al. (2025) ana-lyzed over 1,600 execution traces from seven LLM-based MAS frameworks and proposed the MAS Failure Taxon-omy (MAST), identifying 14 failure modes across different stages of agent execution. These studies provide insights into LLM-based MAS challenges and failure patterns; however, a comprehensive understanding of practitioner-reported is-sues, their causes, and solutions remains limited. This study addresses this gap through empirical analysis of the issues of LLM-based MAS.

## 7. Conclusions 

In this study, we investigated the issues that practitioners encounter when developing and using open-source LLM-based MAS, as well as their underlying causes and potential solutions. We collected 22,848 closed issues from open-source LLM-based MAS on GitHub that met our inclusion criteria, as shown in Table 2. Then, we applied a mixed auto-mated and manual filtering approach to reduce the dataset to 944 issues related to LLM-based MAS. Finally, we extracted 944 issues, 540 causes, and 427 solutions based on our data extraction criteria. Our results show that Orchestration & Execution Issue is the most common issue faced by practi-tioners. Workflow Problem (WP) , Tool Integration Problem (TIP) , and Memory Problem (MP) are identified as the most frequent causes of the issues, while Optimize Workflow (OW) 

was employed as the predominant solution to address the issues.

Based on the study results, we provide implications for users, developers, and researchers of open-source LLM-based MAS. Users can address the most frequent Orches-tration & Execution Issue through explicit handoff design between agents and multi-agent execution controls, such as retry limits, timeouts, and monitoring mechanisms in LLM-based MAS. They can also refine prompt design to obtain more reliable responses from LLM-based MAS. Developers can improve tool integration reliability through schema vali-dation, execution controls, and tool connection management in LLM-based MAS. Researchers can explore methods to reproduce and diagnose unsolved issues and develop reliable memory management mechanisms to improve context prop-agation between agents, state synchronization across agents, and memory retrieval for agent interactions in LLM-based MAS.
